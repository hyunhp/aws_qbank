@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.1 advanced use-case
S: A lender's AI credit model approves applicants from one postal region at a much lower rate than others with similar finances. The model does not use region as an input. What is the most likely cause, and the right first step?
A: Other inputs act as proxies for region, so the team should measure outcomes by group and investigate those features
B: The model cannot be biased because region is not an input, so the approval gap must be a reporting error
C: The model is working as intended, since lenders are allowed to use any statistically predictive pattern
D: The gap proves the training data was stolen or tampered with, so the model should be retired and the incident reported to police
K: A
W: Removing a sensitive attribute does not remove bias; correlated features such as address history or income patterns can act as proxies. Measuring outcomes across groups and examining those features is the right start.
X: B=Proxies can reproduce bias without the attribute itself. | C=Predictive patterns can still be unfair or unlawful. | D=Bias is a fairness issue, not evidence of theft.
T: Test outcomes across groups; excluding a sensitive attribute does not guarantee fairness.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.1 applied definition
S: A company tells customers when they are chatting with an AI assistant and explains what data the assistant uses. Which responsible AI principle does this primarily support?
A: Transparency, so users understand when AI is involved and how it reaches its outputs
B: Robustness, so the assistant keeps working correctly when inputs are unusual or noisy
C: Controllability, so administrators can shut the assistant down during an emergency
D: Veracity, so every answer the assistant gives is guaranteed to be factually correct
K: A
W: Disclosing AI involvement and the data used helps people understand and calibrate their trust, which is the core of transparency.
X: B=Robustness concerns reliable behavior under varied inputs. | C=Controllability concerns oversight and intervention. | D=Veracity aims for accuracy, but disclosure does not guarantee correctness.
T: Telling users when AI is involved and what it uses is transparency.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 advanced use-case
S: A European retailer plans an AI system that screens job applicants. Which governance step should happen before deployment?
A: A documented risk assessment covering fairness, human oversight, and applicable regulations for high-impact uses
B: A marketing plan announcing that hiring decisions will soon be fully automated, to attract more applicants to open roles
C: A decision to keep the system's use secret from applicants so that candidates cannot game the screening
D: A contract clause stating that the software vendor alone is legally responsible for any unfair outcomes
K: A
W: Hiring decisions significantly affect people and are treated as high risk under regulations such as the EU AI Act. A documented assessment of fairness, oversight, and legal requirements belongs before launch.
X: B=Announcing full automation raises risk rather than managing it. | C=Secrecy conflicts with transparency obligations. | D=Deploying organizations keep accountability for how they use AI.
T: High-impact AI uses need a documented risk and compliance assessment before deployment.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 applied use-case
S: A company has 30 teams experimenting with generative AI and no common rules. Leadership wants consistency without stopping innovation. Which governance structure fits best?
A: A cross-functional AI governance group that sets shared policies and risk tiers while teams build within them
B: A rule that only the IT department may use generative AI, with every other team required to submit tickets for each request
C: No central policy, so each team can decide its own rules according to the needs of its own customers
D: A complete ban on generative AI until an external auditor certifies every possible future use case
K: A
W: A cross-functional group with clear policies and risk-based review sets guardrails while letting teams move quickly on lower-risk work.
X: B=Centralizing all use in IT becomes a bottleneck. | C=No shared rules leads to inconsistent risk handling. | D=A blanket ban stops innovation and cannot pre-certify every use.
T: Govern AI with shared policies and risk tiers, not bans or a free-for-all.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: Employees are pasting confidential customer contracts into a free public chatbot to summarize them. Which response best reduces the risk while meeting the business need?
A: Provide an approved AI tool with data protections for this task and set a clear policy on public tools
B: Discipline the employees involved and take no further action, since the acceptable-use policy already existed
C: Block all internet access for employees who work with customer contracts until further notice
D: Allow public chatbots to continue, since summaries do not contain the original contract text
K: A
W: Employees turned to public tools because they had a real need. An approved tool with enterprise data protections plus a clear policy addresses both the need and the leakage risk.
X: B=Punishment alone leaves the need unmet, so the behavior recurs. | C=Blocking internet access is disproportionate and disruptive. | D=The full contract text is still sent to the public service.
T: Meet the business need with approved, protected tools, and set clear usage policies.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 applied comparison
S: A board asks which risk is specific to generative AI compared with traditional predictive models. Which answer is best?
A: Producing fluent but fabricated content, such as invented citations or facts presented as true
B: Needing large amounts of historical data to learn patterns before the system can make any useful predictions
C: Requiring computing resources and ongoing operating costs to run the system in production
D: Performing worse over time if the business environment changes after the system is deployed
K: A
W: Generating convincing but false content (hallucination) is a characteristic risk of generative models. The other items apply to machine learning in general.
X: B=All machine learning models need training data. | C=All software systems need compute and operating budget. | D=Drift affects predictive and generative systems alike.
T: Hallucination is a hallmark generative AI risk to govern explicitly.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.1 advanced tradeoff
S: An insurer can choose a slightly more accurate model that cannot explain its decisions or a slightly less accurate model whose reasons are clear. Customers can appeal claim denials. Which choice is most defensible?
A: The explainable model, because appeals require clear reasons and the accuracy difference is small
B: The more accurate model, because accuracy always outweighs every other consideration in insurance
C: Neither model, because any automated support for claims decisions is inherently irresponsible
D: The more accurate model, with explanations written afterward by staff who guess the model's reasoning
K: A
W: Where people can challenge decisions, the ability to explain them matters. A small accuracy gain rarely justifies losing that ability.
X: B=Accuracy is important but not absolute when decisions affect people. | C=Well-governed automation with oversight can be responsible. | D=Guessed explanations are not real explanations.
T: When decisions must be explained and appealed, favor explainable models if the accuracy cost is small.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: A bank's customer chatbot must never give investment advice and must hide account numbers in its replies. Which control addresses both requirements consistently?
A: Guardrails that deny the investment advice topic and mask sensitive information in responses
B: A longer system prompt asking the model politely to avoid advice and account numbers in replies
C: A weekly manual review of a random sample of conversations to catch violations after they happen
D: Training every customer service agent to correct the chatbot when they notice a problem in chats
K: A
W: Configurable guardrails enforce denied topics and sensitive-information filters on every request and response, which is more reliable than instructions alone or after-the-fact review.
X: B=Prompt instructions can be bypassed and are not enforced. | C=Sampling reviews find problems after customers are affected. | D=Agents are not present in every chatbot conversation.
T: Enforce content rules with guardrails; prompts and reviews alone are not enough.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.1 applied use-case
S: A company deploys an AI tool that recommends which employees receive promotions. Which TWO practices best support fairness and accountability? (Select TWO)
A: Keep a human decision-maker responsible for final promotion decisions, with the ability to override
B: Monitor recommendation outcomes across demographic groups and investigate unexplained gaps
C: Hide the tool's use from managers so they are not influenced by knowing a model was involved
D: Remove all performance data from the tool so it relies only on tenure when recommending people
E: Let the tool make final decisions automatically to remove human bias from the process entirely
K: A,B
W: For consequential decisions about people, human accountability and ongoing monitoring of outcomes across groups are central safeguards.
X: C=Hiding AI use undermines transparency and accountability. | D=Using only tenure distorts the purpose and can introduce other biases. | E=Full automation removes oversight rather than bias.
T: Keep humans accountable and monitor outcomes by group for high-impact decisions about people.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 applied troubleshooting
S: Six months after launch, a fraud model's alerts have become less accurate. Transaction patterns shifted after a new payment method was introduced. Which governance practice would have caught this sooner?
A: Ongoing monitoring of model performance and input data against agreed thresholds, with alerts to owners
B: A one-time validation of the model before launch, repeated only when the vendor releases a new version of its software
C: An annual report of how many fraud alerts the model generated compared with the previous year
D: A policy requiring the model to be replaced every month regardless of how well it is performing
K: A
W: Models degrade when data or behavior shifts. Continuous monitoring of performance and input distributions with thresholds lets owners detect drift and act early.
X: B=One-time validation cannot detect later drift. | C=Annual volume reports are too slow and do not measure accuracy. | D=Replacing models on a fixed schedule wastes effort and may not address the cause.
T: Monitor production models continuously; drift is expected, not exceptional.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: An airline wants an AI agent that can rebook passengers and issue vouchers automatically. Which TWO controls best limit the risk of costly mistakes? (Select TWO)
A: Set limits on voucher amounts and require human approval above a threshold
B: Grant the agent only the specific systems and actions it needs, and log every action taken
C: Allow the agent to access every airline system so it can solve any problem without escalation
D: Let the agent choose its own voucher amounts based on how upset the passenger seems in chat
E: Disable logging for the agent to protect passenger privacy and keep conversations confidential
K: A,B
W: Limits with human approval for high-value actions and least-privilege access with full logging bound what an agent can do and make its actions reviewable.
X: C=Broad access widens the impact of errors or manipulation. | D=Unbounded discretion invites costly and inconsistent outcomes. | E=Without logs, mistakes cannot be detected or audited; privacy is handled by access controls on logs.
T: Constrain AI agents with least privilege, spending limits, human approval, and logs.
