# AIB-C01 batch 04: Domain 4 (business readiness, leadership, AI transformation)
@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: Before committing to a customer-churn AI project, a telecom company runs a readiness assessment. Which finding is the most serious blocker?
A: Customer records are split across five systems with no shared customer identifier to link them
B: The marketing team would prefer the project to start in the spring instead of in the winter quarter
C: The company has not yet chosen a name or internal logo for the churn prediction project and its dashboard
D: Two executives disagree about whether the dashboard should use bar charts or line charts for trends
K: A
W: Churn models need a joined view of each customer. Without a common identifier, data cannot be combined reliably, which blocks the project until it is fixed.
X: B=Timing preferences are easy to resolve. | C=Naming has no effect on feasibility. | D=Chart style is a presentation detail.
T: Data that cannot be joined or trusted is the most common AI readiness blocker.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.1 advanced comparison
S: An executive asks how an AI maturity assessment differs from a capability gap analysis. Which answer is accurate?
A: A maturity assessment places the organization on stages of AI capability; a gap analysis lists what is missing for a specific goal
B: A maturity assessment measures model accuracy, while a gap analysis measures how much the AI budget has been spent so far
C: They are identical activities with different names, used interchangeably by consultants and technology vendors
D: A maturity assessment is required by law in every country, while a gap analysis is an optional internal exercise
K: A
W: Maturity models describe where an organization stands overall across stages; gap analysis identifies the specific skills, data, or processes needed for a defined objective.
X: B=Neither measures model accuracy or spending. | C=They answer different questions. | D=Neither is universally required by law.
T: Maturity shows where you are overall; gap analysis shows what you need for a specific goal.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A manufacturer's AI demand forecasts are unreliable because each plant records inventory differently. Which foundational investment helps most?
A: Common data definitions and a shared, governed data platform that all plants feed consistently
B: A more powerful foundation model that can guess what each plant's inventory records actually mean
C: More dashboards so each plant manager can view the unreliable forecasts in more places at once
D: A new AI vendor, since unreliable forecasts show that the current vendor's algorithms are weak
K: A
W: Consistent definitions and a governed data platform give models reliable inputs; no algorithm can fully fix inconsistent source data.
X: B=Guessing meaning adds error rather than removing it. | C=More views of bad forecasts do not improve them. | D=The issue is data consistency, not the vendor.
T: Fix data foundations before expecting reliable AI outputs.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.3 advanced use-case
S: Employees fear an AI assistant will replace their jobs, and adoption is low three months after launch. Which leadership action is most effective?
A: Explain how the assistant supports their roles, involve them in improving it, and offer role-specific training
B: Require every employee to use the assistant daily and publish a ranking of the lowest users each week
C: Stop communicating about the assistant so employees gradually forget about their concerns over time
D: Replace the current assistant with a different product, since low adoption proves the tool is inadequate
K: A
W: Adoption problems rooted in fear respond to clear purpose, involvement, and skills building. Mandates and silence tend to deepen resistance.
X: B=Coercion and public rankings increase fear and resentment. | C=Silence leaves fears unaddressed. | D=The cause is concern about jobs, not the product.
T: Address fear with purpose, involvement, and training; mandates rarely build real adoption.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.4 applied use-case
S: A generative AI pilot succeeded in one department. Leadership wants company-wide rollout within a year. Which approach best manages risk?
A: Expand in phases to a few more departments, measure results, and refine before a broad rollout
B: Deploy to every department on the same day so all employees benefit from the tool at the same time
C: Keep the tool in the pilot department permanently, since success elsewhere cannot be guaranteed
D: Rebuild the solution from scratch for each department so that every team gets a fully custom tool
K: A
W: Phased expansion tests whether results hold in different contexts and lets the team fix problems while the blast radius is small.
X: B=A big-bang rollout multiplies the impact of any problem. | C=Refusing to scale forfeits proven value. | D=Rebuilding for every team wastes effort and slows adoption.
T: Scale AI in phases, with measurement and refinement between waves.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.4 advanced troubleshooting
S: An AI proof of concept performed well in testing but failed within weeks of going into production because nobody monitored it or planned for outages. What was missing?
A: Production practices such as monitoring, versioning, rollback, and a continuity plan for failures
B: A larger test dataset, since proofs of concept that pass testing never fail once they reach production
C: A more expensive foundation model, since cheaper models are the main reason production systems fail
D: A formal launch event, since systems launched without executive attention are more likely to fail
K: A
W: Moving from experiment to production requires operational practices: monitoring, controlled releases, rollback, and a plan for when the system fails.
X: B=Passing tests does not guarantee production success. | C=Model price is not the root cause described. | D=Launch events do not keep systems running.
T: Production AI needs operations: monitoring, rollback, and continuity planning.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A company wants AI skills across the business quickly on a limited budget, and also needs buy-in from teams. Which workforce approach fits best?
A: Upskill motivated employees who know the business, and add a few specialist hires for hard gaps
B: Replace the existing workforce with newly hired AI specialists over the next two quarters
C: Outsource all AI work permanently so that no employee needs to learn anything about AI
D: Wait until universities produce enough AI graduates before starting any internal AI projects
K: A
W: Upskilling people with domain knowledge builds capability and buy-in at lower cost, while targeted hires fill gaps that training cannot cover quickly.
X: B=Mass replacement is costly and destroys institutional knowledge and trust. | C=Permanent outsourcing leaves the business unable to judge or direct AI. | D=Waiting delays value indefinitely.
T: Upskill domain experts and hire selectively to build AI capability with buy-in.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.2 advanced tradeoff
S: A company's on-premises data center cannot handle unpredictable spikes in AI inference demand, and buying hardware for peak load would sit idle most of the year. Which option best addresses this?
A: Move suitable inference workloads to cloud services that scale with demand and are billed by use
B: Buy enough on-premises GPUs for peak demand and accept that most of them will sit idle most of the year
C: Reject inference requests during demand spikes so the existing on-premises hardware is never overloaded
D: Limit the AI system to a fixed number of users so that demand never exceeds on-premises capacity limits
K: A
W: Elastic cloud capacity matches unpredictable demand without paying for idle peak hardware.
X: B=Buying for peak locks capital into idle equipment. | C=Rejecting requests harms users and value. | D=Capping users limits value to protect infrastructure.
T: Use elastic, pay-per-use capacity when AI demand is spiky.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.4 applied use-case
S: A company is moving a generative AI assistant from pilot to use by 5,000 employees. Which TWO preparations are most important? (Select TWO)
A: Monitoring, logging, and rollback procedures for the production system
B: A business continuity plan for how work continues if the assistant is unavailable
C: A new internal logo and brand name for the assistant before the wider rollout begins
D: Removing the feedback button so employees are not distracted from their daily work tasks
E: Replacing the pilot's approved model with the newest model released that same week
K: A,B
W: At scale, the organization needs to detect and recover from problems, and to keep operating when the assistant is down.
X: C=Branding does not affect production readiness. | D=Feedback is essential for improvement at scale. | E=Switching to an untested model right before rollout adds risk.
T: Production readiness means observability, rollback, and continuity planning.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.3 advanced use-case

C: Agents continue sorting every ticket by hand while the AI system classifies the same tickets in parallel

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.1 advanced use-case
S: An AI readiness assessment finds strong executive enthusiasm but staff who lack skills and are anxious about change. Which TWO actions address the readiness gap? (Select TWO)
A: Run role-based training that shows staff how AI applies to their own work
B: Set up a change-management plan with clear communication and channels for feedback
C: Accelerate the rollout so that staff have no time to worry before the tools arrive
D: Limit AI discussions to executives until all tools have been fully deployed company-wide
E: Buy more AI tools, since having more options will increase staff confidence on its own
K: A,B
W: The gap is people readiness. Relevant training builds skills, and structured change management addresses anxiety through communication and involvement.

T: Close people readiness gaps with training and change management.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.4 advanced tradeoff
S: A company has three AI pilots running. One exceeds targets, one is on track, and one has missed milestones twice with no clear fix. How should leadership manage the portfolio?
A: Scale the successful pilot, continue the on-track one, and stop the one that keeps missing milestones
B: Continue all three pilots equally, since stopping any of them would signal failure to the organization
C: Stop all three pilots and restart with new ideas, since mixed results show the strategy is unclear
D: Move budget from the successful pilot to the failing one, since the failing pilot needs the most help
K: A
W: Portfolio discipline means investing more in what works, continuing what is progressing, and stopping initiatives that repeatedly miss milestones without a path forward.
X: B=Equal treatment ignores evidence. | C=Stopping successes wastes proven value. | D=Starving the winner to rescue a failing pilot misallocates resources.
T: Scale winners, stop repeated misses, and let evidence drive AI portfolio decisions.
