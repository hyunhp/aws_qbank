# AIB-C01 D3 batch b19: governance/compliance (T3.2) and AI risk mitigation (T3.3)

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 advanced tradeoff
S: A software company wants its sales team to use a third-party generative AI service to draft proposals containing customer pricing and contract terms. Procurement is negotiating the vendor agreement. Which contractual position BEST protects the company's confidential data while still allowing the team to use the service?
A: The agreement states that prompts, outputs and uploaded files are not used to train or improve the vendor's models, sets a retention limit with deletion on request or termination, and lists any subprocessors.
B: The agreement relies on the vendor's public marketing statements about security, since these describe current practice and can be cited in a dispute if the vendor later changes how it handles customer content.
C: The agreement lets the vendor use prompts for model improvement in exchange for a lower price, on the basis that the vendor removes customer names before any prompt is added to a training set.
D: The agreement transfers all responsibility for data protection to the vendor, so the company can skip its own data classification and does not need to define which content employees may submit.
K: A
G: ## Why contracts matter for generative AI
When employees send a prompt to a hosted AI service, the content leaves the company's control. What happens to it next, such as whether it is stored, who can read it, and whether it becomes training material, is decided by the vendor's terms, not by the employee's intent.
## What good terms cover
- **Data use**: an explicit statement that customer content is not used to train or improve the vendor's models.
- **Retention and deletion**: how long prompts, outputs and logs are kept, and deletion on request or when the contract ends.
- **Subprocessors and locations**: which other parties touch the data and where it is processed.
- **Audit and incident duties**: the vendor's obligation to report security incidents within a defined time.
Contract terms are binding and enforceable; marketing pages and verbal assurances are not.
W: The written no-training, retention-and-deletion and subprocessor terms turn expectations into enforceable obligations, so the sales team gains the productivity benefit without giving up control of pricing and contract data.
X: B=Marketing statements: public web pages can change at any time and are usually not part of the signed agreement. If the vendor changes its practice, the company would have no contractual right to point to.
C=Discount for training use: removing customer names does not remove pricing details, deal structure or other confidential context that can still identify a customer or reveal strategy. Trading confidential data for a lower price is the opposite of protecting it.
D=Skip internal classification: a vendor agreement does not transfer the company's own accountability for its data. The company must still classify content and set rules for what may be submitted to which tool.
T: Put data-use, retention, deletion and subprocessor commitments in the signed contract; marketing claims and vendor promises are not controls, and the company keeps its own data-handling duties.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 advanced use-case
S: A healthcare technology company serves clients in several countries. Some clients have obligations to keep patient-related data inside their home country. The company wants to add a generative AI assistant that summarizes case notes. Before choosing a hosting approach, what should leadership require FIRST?
A: A map of where prompts, retrieved documents, logs and fine-tuning data are processed and stored, checked against each client's residency obligations, including how requests are routed across Regions.
B: A written statement from the model provider that summaries are accurate in every supported language, since summary quality is the main factor that decides where the assistant can be deployed.
C: A plan to keep all data in one central Region for every client, because a single location is easier to secure and removes the need to look at any client's individual obligations.
D: A commitment to encrypt all case notes in transit and at rest, because encryption removes location restrictions on where regulated data may be processed or stored.
K: A
G: ## Data residency is about where data is processed and stored, not only where it starts
Some laws and client contracts limit where certain personal or regulated data may be stored or processed. A generative AI system creates more places for that data to travel than a normal application:
- the **prompt** that contains the case note,
- **retrieved documents** pulled in to give context,
- **logs and traces** that record inputs and outputs for auditing,
- **fine-tuning or evaluation datasets** built from real notes,
- and, if the service can route requests across Regions for capacity, the **inference location** itself.
## Order of decisions
Leaders first learn what each obligation requires and where the data would actually flow. Only then can they choose a Region setup, configure routing limits, and decide what can be logged.
W: Mapping every data flow against each client's residency obligations is the step that makes the later hosting and routing choices sound, and it also shows where routing or logging could quietly move data out of a permitted location.
X: B=Language accuracy: quality matters for the product, but it does not answer whether the data is allowed to be processed in a given place. A very accurate assistant can still breach a residency obligation.
C=One central Region: this can break obligations for clients who need their data kept in-country. A single location may be simpler to operate, but it is a design preference and not a compliance analysis.
D=Encryption as a substitute: encryption protects data from unauthorized reading, but residency rules concern the location of storage and processing. Encrypted data stored in a prohibited location is still in a prohibited location.
T: Start with a data-flow map (prompts, documents, logs, training data, inference routing) and test it against residency obligations before picking where to host.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 advanced tradeoff
S: A bank must be able to explain AI-assisted credit decisions to auditors up to seven years later. The privacy team, however, wants to minimize how long personal data is kept, and the AI assistant logs full prompts containing customer details. Which approach BEST reconciles the two goals?
A: Keep one complete copy of every prompt indefinitely, since audit needs always outweigh privacy duties and the question of a retention limit can be handled later.
B: Define retention by purpose: keep decision records (model version, key inputs, output, reviewer action) for the audit period, redact personal data in logs, and restrict access.
C: Delete all AI logs after thirty days to satisfy privacy, and rely on staff memory and the final customer letter to reconstruct the reasoning if an auditor asks.
D: Keep logs only in each business unit's own tools with local retention settings, so that each team decides how long its own records are kept and who can access them.
K: B
G: ## Two duties that pull in opposite directions
- **Accountability and audit** call for records: which model or version produced a result, what information it was given, what it returned, and what a human did with it.
- **Data minimization** calls for keeping personal data no longer than needed for a stated purpose.
## Resolving the tension
The two duties are compatible when records are designed instead of dumped:
- Separate a compact **decision record** from raw conversation logs.
- Set retention **per purpose**, so the decision record is kept for the audit period and raw prompts are kept only as long as they are needed.
- **Redact or pseudonymize** personal data where the audit does not need the identity.
- Control access, and log who views the records.
W: Purpose-based retention with redaction and restricted access keeps the evidence auditors need for the full period while limiting how much personal data sits in logs, so neither duty is sacrificed to the other.
X: A=Keep everything forever: an unlimited copy of full prompts creates a large store of personal data that is kept longer than any purpose justifies, which is a privacy failure and a bigger target in a breach.
C=Thirty-day deletion: this fails the seven-year audit requirement. Reconstructing decisions from memory or a customer letter cannot show which model version and inputs were used.
D=Local retention per unit: inconsistent settings mean some records vanish early and others are held too long. Auditors need a consistent, centrally governed record.
T: Reconcile audit and privacy with purpose-based retention: keep compact decision records for the audit period, redact personal data in logs, and centrally control access.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.2 advanced troubleshooting
S: A retailer's customer service assistant, built on a generative AI model, displayed another customer's order details in a reply. The company suspects a configuration error and has not yet confirmed the scope. Which TWO actions belong in the first phase of the incident process? (Select TWO)
A: Involve legal and privacy leads immediately so that notification duties and deadlines to regulators and affected customers can be assessed as the facts become known.
B: Wait until the root cause is fully confirmed before telling anyone outside the engineering team, so that all statements made later are complete and never need to be corrected.
C: Preserve prompts, outputs, access logs and configuration history for the affected period, so that the investigation and any later audit can establish what data was exposed.
D: Replace the assistant's model with a newer one straight away and clear the old logs, since a new model removes the cause and old logs only add storage cost.
E: Ask the marketing team to post a general statement saying the company's AI is safe, without describing the incident, so that customers are not alarmed unnecessarily.
K: A,C
G: ## Incident reporting is a governance process, not only an engineering one
An AI incident that exposes personal data is also a privacy and legal event. Many obligations, including those in contracts with customers and rules from regulators, set time limits that start when the company becomes aware of the incident, not when the root cause is proven.
## First-phase priorities
- **Escalate** to the people who can judge obligations (legal, privacy, security), so decisions on notification are made early with partial facts.
- **Preserve evidence**: logs, prompts and outputs, configuration versions. Without them the scope cannot be established.
- **Contain** the exposure, for example by disabling the affected feature or the faulty setting, without destroying evidence.
- **Communicate** accurately, with statements that reflect what is known.
W: A=Escalate to legal and privacy: this starts the assessment of notification duties and deadlines while facts are still incomplete.
   C=Preserve logs and configuration history: this makes it possible to prove what was exposed and to whom, for the investigation and any audit.
X: B=Waiting for full root cause: notification deadlines can run from awareness of the incident. Delaying escalation until every fact is known can cause a missed deadline.
D=Replace the model and clear logs: swapping the model may not touch the real cause, which was suspected to be configuration, and deleting logs destroys the evidence needed to scope the exposure and to satisfy auditors.
E=Reassuring statement: a generic claim that the AI is safe, made while an exposure is under investigation, can be misleading and can damage trust more when the facts emerge.
T: In an AI incident, escalate to legal and privacy early and preserve logs and configuration history; do not delay reporting until root cause is known, and do not destroy evidence.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: An insurer plans to introduce a generative AI assistant that drafts responses to policyholder claims questions. Errors could lead to complaints and regulatory attention. Which rollout approach BEST limits business risk while still giving leadership real evidence about value?
A: Launch to all policyholders at once with a prominent disclaimer, since a disclaimer transfers responsibility for any incorrect answer to the customer and removes the need for a slower rollout.
B: Test the assistant only on a curated set of ideal questions written by the project team, and launch to everyone once it passes every one of them, to keep the schedule.
C: Start with a small group of trained internal agents who review every draft, then widen to limited customers with agreed success and stop thresholds, expanding only when monitored results stay within them.
D: Run the assistant in a hidden test environment for a full year with no real users, because more months of isolated testing always reduce risk more than exposure to real questions.
K: C
G: ## Staged rollout
A staged (or phased) rollout exposes a new AI capability to progressively larger groups, and moves to the next stage only when evidence shows it is safe to do so.
## What makes a stage useful
- A **small, well-defined audience** so that any mistake affects few people.
- **Human review** at early stages, so errors are caught before they reach a customer.
- **Pre-agreed thresholds**: measures such as error rates, complaints or escalation counts that say when to proceed, pause or roll back.
- **Monitoring** during each stage, so the thresholds are checked against real behavior.
This produces real-world evidence that a lab test cannot: real questions, real edge cases and real user reactions.
W: Starting with internal reviewers and widening only when monitored results meet stated thresholds keeps early mistakes small and produces actual evidence of value and risk before customers depend on the assistant.
X: A=Full launch with disclaimer: a disclaimer does not remove the company's responsibility to policyholders or regulators, and a full launch exposes everyone to any error before it has been seen in practice.
B=Curated ideal questions: a test set written by the builders tends to reflect what they expect. Real policyholders ask unusual, ambiguous and emotional questions that such a test will never contain.
D=A year of isolated testing: waiting long with no real users delays value and still cannot show how the assistant behaves with real questions. Risk is reduced by controlled exposure with monitoring, not by indefinite delay.
T: Roll out AI in stages: small trained group with human review first, then wider exposure gated by pre-agreed thresholds and monitoring.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced troubleshooting
S: A finance team uses an AI tool that drafts invoice-coding suggestions, which an accountant approves. A manager notices accountants approve about 99 percent of suggestions in a few seconds each. An error audit then finds the tool is wrong on roughly one suggestion in twenty. What is the MOST effective change to the control?
A: Replace the reviewers with a second AI tool that checks the first, since two automated systems are cheaper than salaried staff and are unlikely to repeat each other's mistakes.
B: Add a required checkbox stating that the accountant has read the suggestion, since a signed confirmation makes each approval a personal record and should prompt a closer look.
C: Tell accountants in an email to be more careful, and increase the number of invoices each person must process per day so that time spent per invoice becomes the metric.
D: Track override rates and review time, insert known-wrong items to test whether reviewers catch them, and redesign the step so the reviewer decides before seeing the suggestion.
K: D
G: ## Automation bias
Automation bias is the tendency to accept an automated suggestion without checking it, especially when it is right most of the time. A human-in-the-loop step then exists on paper but does not function as a control.
## Signs and remedies
- **Signs**: very high approval rates, very short review times, and errors that reach production despite review.
- **Measure the control itself**: override rate, time per review, and whether reviewers catch **seeded errors** (items known to be wrong, inserted on purpose).
- **Change the design**: ask the reviewer to form a judgment first, or show the reasoning and source so that checking is easier, and match the review effort to the risk of each item.
W: Measuring override rates and review time, seeding known errors, and having reviewers decide before seeing the suggestion tests whether the human check actually works and rebuilds it where it does not.
X: A=A second AI reviewer: another automated layer can share the same blind spots and does not restore genuine human accountability. Two tools agreeing does not mean both are right.
B=Read checkbox: a confirmation box adds clicks but does not change how carefully the item is examined, and people quickly learn to tick it automatically.
C=Email and higher quota: a reminder is weak, and raising workload reduces the time available for checking, which makes rubber-stamping more likely, not less.
T: A human check that approves almost everything in seconds is not a control; measure override rate, review time and seeded-error catch rate, and design the review to force independent judgment.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: A media company is about to launch a public-facing generative AI writing assistant. The product team says the model passed all of its functional tests. Which action BEST addresses the risk that the assistant will be misused in ways the team has not imagined?
A: Commission red-teaming by a group independent of the builders, who deliberately try to make the assistant produce harmful, off-brand or leaking output, and log findings with owners and deadlines.
B: Rely on the model provider's published safety report, because the provider has already tested the model and its results apply unchanged to this company's use case and audience.
C: Ask the developers to run additional functional tests using the same scripts, because repeating the passing tests more often gives greater confidence about unexpected user behavior.
D: Post the acceptable-use policy on the product page and treat any misuse after launch as a user violation, since users who break the rules take responsibility for the results.
K: A
G: ## Testing for the unexpected
Functional tests check that the product does what its designers intended. They rarely reveal what happens when someone acts adversarially or in a way nobody planned for.
## Red-teaming
Red-teaming is structured, adversarial testing: people try to make the system fail, for example by getting it to produce offensive text, reveal hidden instructions, or ignore its limits.
- Use testers **independent of the builders**, who have different assumptions.
- Cover **business-specific harms** (brand, legal, safety), not only generic ones.
- Record each finding in a tracker with an **owner and deadline**, then retest after fixes.
- Repeat after major changes, since new prompts, tools or models change the risk.
W: Independent adversarial testers explore the misuse the builders did not foresee, and logged findings with owners turn discoveries into fixes before the public sees the failures.
X: B=Provider's safety report: it describes the base model under the provider's own tests, not this company's prompts, data, audience and brand risks. Results do not transfer unchanged to a specific deployment.
C=More of the same tests: repeating passing scripts confirms known behavior but does not discover unknown failure modes. The gap is the unexpected, which needs a different kind of test.
D=Policy on the product page: a posted policy does not stop harmful output, and reputational damage from misuse lands on the company regardless of whether the user broke the rules.
T: Functional tests confirm expected behavior; red-teaming by independent testers finds unexpected misuse, and its findings need owners and retests.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: The risk committee is building a risk register for the company's AI portfolio. Which TWO details should each register entry include so the register supports decisions instead of being a list of worries? (Select TWO)
A: A named accountable owner and a rating of the remaining risk after existing mitigations are applied, so leaders can see what is still exposed and who must act.
B: The name of the vendor's sales representative, so that any question about the risk can be handled through a personal relationship rather than through the company's own processes.
C: A description of the likely scenario with its impact and likelihood, plus a monitoring indicator or trigger that shows when the risk is increasing and mitigation should change.
D: A statement that the risk has been accepted permanently, so the entry never needs to be reviewed again and the register stays short even as new uses of the AI system are added.
E: A list of the technical model parameters, since parameter counts are the most reliable predictor of business harm and are the value that leaders use to rank risks.
K: A,C
G: ## What a risk register is for
A risk register is the running record of identified risks and how the organization is handling them. It is useful only if each entry supports a decision: accept, reduce, transfer or avoid.
## Useful entry fields
- **Scenario**: what could go wrong, written in business terms.
- **Likelihood and impact**, to rank it against other risks.
- **Existing mitigations and residual risk**: what remains after controls.
- **Owner**: one accountable person.
- **Indicator or trigger**: a measure that signals the risk is rising, and a review date.
Entries are reviewed on a schedule and whenever the AI system, its data or its use changes.
W: A=Owner and residual rating: an accountable owner shows who must act, and the residual rating shows what exposure remains after controls.
   C=Scenario, impact, likelihood and trigger: these let leaders rank the risk against others and know when to change course.
X: B=Vendor sales contact: a personal contact is not a control. The register should point to internal accountability, not to an individual relationship at a supplier.
D=Permanent acceptance: risks change as data, usage and models change. An entry that is never reviewed again quickly becomes inaccurate and gives false comfort.
E=Model parameters: technical size figures do not predict business harm. Risk is ranked by scenario, impact and likelihood in the context of how the system is used.
T: A useful AI risk register entry names a scenario, impact and likelihood, existing controls and residual risk, an accountable owner, and a trigger for review.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced tradeoff
S: A logistics company uses an AI model to forecast delivery volumes. The data science team proposes retraining every year on a fixed calendar. Operations leaders worry that customer behavior can shift suddenly, for example after a new competitor arrives. Which monitoring approach BEST manages drift risk?
A: Retrain on a fixed monthly schedule regardless of results, on the view that frequent retraining keeps the model current and that no forecast-error measurements are needed to show whether it degraded.
B: Watch the model's compute cost, response time and service uptime on an operations dashboard, on the view that a healthy technical system means the forecasts are also still accurate for the business.
C: Wait for customer complaints about late deliveries before reviewing the model, on the view that complaints are the clearest real-world sign of forecast problems and cost nothing to collect.
D: Track forecast error against actual volumes and input-pattern changes, with agreed thresholds and a named owner who triggers investigation or retraining when a threshold is crossed.
K: D
G: ## What drift is
Drift is a change over time that makes a model's past learning a poorer guide to the present. Data drift means inputs change (different customers, seasons, products). Concept drift means the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes (a competitor alters how customers order).
## Monitoring that leaders can govern
- **Outcome KPIs**: compare forecasts to actual results as they become known.
- **Input indicators**: changes in the mix of incoming data, which can warn earlier than outcomes.
- **Thresholds and an owner**: agreed limits that trigger a review, so action does not depend on someone noticing.
- **Response options**: investigate, retrain, adjust, or fall back to a manual process.
A fixed calendar can be too slow for sudden change and wasteful when nothing has changed.
W: Comparing forecasts with actuals and watching input patterns, with thresholds and an owner, detects sudden shifts promptly and triggers action based on evidence instead of a schedule.
X: A=Monthly retraining regardless: it costs effort and can even introduce new problems, and without measurements nobody knows whether the model degraded or whether retraining helped.
B=Cost, latency and uptime only: these measure whether the system runs, not whether its answers are right. A model can be fast, cheap and badly wrong.
C=Wait for complaints: complaints arrive after customers have been harmed, so this is the slowest and most costly detection method. The point of monitoring is to catch degradation earlier.
T: Manage drift with outcome and input monitoring, agreed thresholds and an accountable owner, not just a retraining calendar or complaints.

@ AIB-C01 D3 T3.3 advanced use-case
S: A travel company's AI assistant posts replies on social media. A screenshot of an offensive reply spreads widely overnight. Leadership wants to be prepared for the next event of this kind. Which preparation would have reduced the damage MOST?
A: A larger monthly advertising budget, so that positive campaigns can outweigh negative posts and the offensive screenshot is quickly forgotten by the public.
B: An incident response plan with named roles, a pre-approved way to pause the assistant immediately, monitoring of public channels, and ready communication steps for customers and the press.
C: A promise never to use AI in public channels again, since removing the technology from the company entirely is the only reliable way to avoid all reputational harm.
D: A policy that engineers alone decide how to respond after each event, because technical staff understand the model best and communications should stay out of technical matters.
K: B
G: ## Reputational harm moves faster than investigation
Public AI failures can spread within hours. The damage depends heavily on how quickly the company stops the harm and how clearly it speaks about it.
## What an incident response plan provides
- **Roles and authority**: who decides, and who may pause the feature without waiting for senior approval.
- **A pause or rollback mechanism** that has been tested, sometimes called a kill switch.
- **Monitoring** of the channels where the assistant speaks, so problems are found by the company first.
- **Communication playbooks** covering customers, press and employees, with legal and communications involved.
- **Review afterward**, feeding lessons into guardrails, testing and the risk register.
W: A plan with named roles, a pre-approved pause and channel monitoring lets the company stop further harm within minutes and communicate clearly, which limits how far and how long the reputational damage spreads.
X: A=More advertising: spending on positive messaging does not stop the assistant from repeating the behavior and can look tone-deaf while the incident is still unresolved.
C=Ban AI everywhere: abandoning the technology gives up its value and does not build any capability to handle incidents, which can also come from human staff or other tools.
D=Engineers alone decide: communication, legal and customer-facing judgment are essential in a public incident. Leaving them out slows the response and risks statements that make the situation worse.
T: Reputational risk from AI is limited by preparation: named roles, a tested pause mechanism, channel monitoring and ready communications.
