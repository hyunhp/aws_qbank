G: ## Tangible versus intangible KPIs
A KPI (key performance indicator) is a number leadership tracks to judge whether an initiative works. For AI business cases, KPIs fall into two groups:
- **Tangible (hard) KPIs** can be expressed directly in money or in countable operational units: incremental revenue, cost savings, hours saved, error rates, tickets resolved. They go straight into an ROI calculation.
- **Intangible (soft) KPIs** are real benefits that are hard to price precisely: brand perception, employee morale, customer sentiment, innovation culture. They are usually measured with surveys or indexes and reported alongside, not inside, the financial numbers.
## Example
A retailer adds AI product recommendations. Revenue from orders that include a recommended item, compared with a control group, is tangible. A survey saying customers "feel the site understands them" is intangible, even though it may eventually lead to revenue.
W: A=Incremental revenue from AI recommendations: it is money the AI system added on top of the baseline, measurable in dollars (ideally against a control group), so it enters ROI directly.
B=Cost savings from automating a manual process: fewer labor hours or lower processing cost is a direct, countable financial result that a leadership report can total and compare to spend.
X: C=Improved brand perception: being seen as an innovator is valuable, but it is measured through surveys or reputation indexes and has no direct dollar value, so it is an intangible benefit.
D=Higher employee morale: less repetitive work can improve retention and engagement, but morale itself is a soft outcome measured by surveys, not a hard financial result.
E=Better customer sentiment: survey scores show how customers feel; they may predict future revenue but are not themselves a financial figure the report can count as ROI.
T: Hard KPIs are money or countable operational results (revenue, savings); brand, morale and sentiment are real but intangible benefits reported separately.

G: ## The basic ROI formula
Return on investment compares what an initiative gains with what it costs:
`ROI = (Gain - Cost) / Cost`, usually shown as a percentage.
- Gain is the total benefit attributed to the initiative (cost savings plus new revenue).
- Cost is the total investment for the same period (build, licenses, infrastructure, people, change management).
- A negative ROI means the initiative has not yet paid back its cost in that period; it does not automatically mean the project is a failure.
## Working the numbers
Gain = 200,000 and Cost = 500,000.
`(200,000 - 500,000) / 500,000 = -300,000 / 500,000 = -0.6`, so the year-one ROI is -60 percent.
AI projects often show a negative first year because implementation costs land up front, while benefits accumulate over several years. That is why business cases also look at multi-year ROI and payback period.
W: Negative 60 percent: subtracting the 500,000 cost from the 200,000 gain gives a net loss of 300,000, and dividing by the 500,000 cost gives -0.6, or -60 percent for year one.
X: A=250 percent: this is Cost divided by Gain (500,000 / 200,000 = 2.5), which inverts the ratio and ignores that the gain is smaller than the cost.
C=150 percent: this is the net difference divided by the gain (300,000 / 200,000) and drops the negative sign, so both the denominator and the direction are wrong.
D=40 percent: this is Gain divided by Cost (200,000 / 500,000), which shows how much of the cost was recovered but not the net return after subtracting the cost.
T: Subtract the cost first, then divide by the cost: a 200,000 gain on a 500,000 investment is -60 percent ROI, not a recovery ratio like 40 percent.

G: ## Leading versus lagging indicators
- A **lagging indicator** reports an outcome after it has happened: quarterly revenue, annual profit, year-end customer lifetime value. It is the final score, but it arrives too late to steer.
- A **leading indicator** moves early and predicts the outcome: adoption, engagement, conversion steps. If it drops, you can act before revenue drops.
## Example for a recommendation engine
The causal chain is roughly: users see recommendations, click them, add items, buy, and come back.
- Weekly feature usage and click-through rate sit at the start of that chain and can be read within days of launch.
- Quarterly revenue and annual CLV sit at the end and only confirm success months later.
Good AI programs track both: leading indicators to adjust the product quickly, lagging indicators to prove business value.
W: D=Weekly click-through rate on recommendations: it measures whether users find the suggestions relevant, changes within days of a model or UI change, and predicts later purchases.
E=Weekly active usage rate of the feature: adoption must come before any revenue impact, and a weekly usage trend gives an early signal of whether the engine will deliver.
X: A=Conference sessions attended by the team: this measures team activity, not user behavior or business outcomes, so it predicts nothing about the engine's success.
B=Quarterly total revenue growth: revenue is the outcome itself, reported after the fact and influenced by many factors beyond the engine, which makes it a lagging indicator.
C=Annual customer lifetime value: CLV reported at year-end summarizes a whole year of behavior and arrives far too late to steer the product, so it is lagging.
T: Engagement signals like weekly usage and click-through move first and predict results; revenue and CLV confirm results only after the fact.

G: ## Why generative AI costs follow users
Most managed generative AI services, including Amazon Bedrock on-demand inference, charge per unit of consumption, typically per input and output token. Cost therefore scales with how many requests users send and how long the prompts and answers are.
If users suddenly start pasting long documents or a feature goes viral, the bill rises the same day, regardless of the budget plan.
## Controls that tie spend to policy
- **Quotas and rate limits**: cap requests or tokens per user, team or application (for example through an API gateway usage plan or application-level throttling).
- **Budgets and alerts**: AWS Budgets can notify owners when actual or forecasted spend crosses a threshold, and Budgets actions can apply restrictive controls.
- **Design levers**: shorter prompts, response length limits, caching, and routing simple requests to smaller, cheaper models.
W: Usage quotas, rate limits, or consumption budget alerts: these act on the exact driver of the problem, per-request consumption by end users, by capping it and warning owners before spikes become surprise bills.
X: A=Fixed annual seat-based license: switching every use case to per-user pricing is often not available for API-based model consumption and can mean paying for unused seats; it changes the billing shape rather than managing the behavior driving usage.
B=Removing cost monitoring dashboards: taking away visibility makes spikes harder to see and explain, so costs would stay unpredictable and be discovered even later.
D=Accepting the cost increase: this applies no control at all and leaves spending entirely at the mercy of user behavior, which is the problem the company wants to solve.
T: When cost is driven by user consumption, control the consumption itself with quotas, rate limits and budget alerts instead of hiding or merely re-packaging the bill.

G: ## Three common AI pricing models
- **Consumption-based (pay-per-use)**: you pay for each request, token, image or minute processed. Examples: Amazon Bedrock on-demand tokens, Amazon Rekognition per image, Amazon Transcribe per second of audio. Costs follow usage exactly and drop to near zero when idle.
- **Instance-based (provisioned capacity)**: you pay per hour for compute you keep running, such as a SageMaker real-time endpoint instance or an EC2 instance, whether or not it is busy.
- **Seat-based (per user)**: you pay a fixed amount per user per month for an application, for example an AI assistant subscription such as Amazon Q Business.
## Choosing between them
Spiky or unknown traffic suits consumption pricing; steady high traffic can be cheaper on provisioned instances; broad employee productivity tools are commonly priced per seat.
W: Consumption-based, instance-based, seat-based: per API call is paying for each unit consumed, hourly endpoint billing is paying for provisioned instance time, and per user per month is a seat license, in exactly the order listed.
X: B=All three are consumption-based: only the per-call price varies with actual usage; an hourly instance is billed even when idle and a per-user fee is charged regardless of how much each user does.
C=Seat-based, consumption-based, instance-based: this labels the per-call service as per-user pricing and the per-user assistant as instance pricing, which shifts every definition by one position.
D=Instance-based, seat-based, consumption-based: this calls the per-request service an hourly instance and the per-user subscription consumption pricing, reversing what each model charges for.
T: Per request is consumption, per hour of running capacity is instance-based, per user per month is seat-based.

G: ## Discount options for steady AWS compute
- **On-Demand**: no commitment, full price.
- **Savings Plans**: you commit to a consistent amount of spend per hour (for example 20 dollars per hour) for one or three years and get lower rates on usage up to that amount.
  - **Compute Savings Plans** apply across EC2 instance families, sizes, Regions and operating systems, and also to AWS Fargate and AWS Lambda, but they do not cover SageMaker usage.

- **Dedicated Hosts**: a whole physical server for you, mainly for per-socket or per-core software licensing and compliance needs.

ROI is only meaningful if the gain in the formula is the gain the investment actually produced. That is called **incremental** impact: the difference between what happened with the AI system and what would have happened without it.
- A business unit that earned 50 million dollars last year would have earned most of that without any AI.
- If AI recommendations lifted revenue by 1 million dollars, the gain is 1 million dollars, not 50 million.
## How teams measure incremental impact
- A/B tests or holdout groups that do not get the AI feature
- Before-and-after comparisons against a pre-AI baseline, adjusted for seasonality
- Tracking only the savings from tasks the AI actually replaced

G: ## What a baseline is
A baseline is the measured value of a metric before a change is introduced. Improvement targets like "reduce resolution time by 30 percent" only make sense relative to that starting point.
## Example
- Before the AI assistant, the team measures average ticket resolution time over three months: 10 hours.
- A 30 percent reduction means the target is 7 hours.
- After launch, the team measures again with the same definition and time window. If the average is 8 hours, they achieved 20 percent and are not yet at target.
Good baselines use the same definitions, data sources and comparable periods as the later measurements, so the difference reflects the AI and not a change in how things were counted.
W: It's the pre-AI reference point for measuring progress: without a measured starting value there is nothing to compute a 30 percent reduction from, so the baseline is what turns the target into a checkable number.
X: A=Only for marketing purposes: the baseline is a measurement tool at the core of evaluation; marketing may quote results, but they are computed from the baseline.
B=Set equal to the target value: a baseline is measured, not chosen; setting it to the target would show zero change and make progress impossible to measure.
C=Irrelevant once the target is set: a percentage target is relative, so it depends on the baseline throughout the project to compute actual improvement.
T: A percentage improvement target is meaningless without a measured pre-AI baseline to compare against.

G: ## Matching pricing commitments to certainty
Pricing options trade flexibility for discount:
- **On-demand / pay-as-you-go**: highest unit price, but no commitment. If the project stops, spending stops.
- **Commitments** (Savings Plans, reserved capacity, multi-year licenses): lower unit price, but you pay for the committed amount whether or not you use it, for the whole term.

## Example
A pilot might be cancelled after six months. A three-year commitment would leave roughly two and a half years of payments for something no longer in use, wiping out any discount. Paying on demand costs more per unit, but total exposure ends when the pilot ends. Once usage is steady and the project is confirmed, the company can buy commitments sized to real data.
W: On-demand pricing until usage patterns stabilize: it carries no commitment, so a cancelled or small pilot only costs what it actually used, and later commitments can be sized on real usage.
X: A=Reserved capacity for year-three usage: it commits the startup to paying for capacity sized for a future it may never reach, and the payments continue even if the pilot is stopped.
B=Long-term seat license for maximum users: it locks in payment for many users who do not exist yet, for a term far longer than the six-month pilot window.
C=3-year All Upfront Savings Plan: it pays three years of committed spend in advance for a workload that may end in six months, turning the discount into a large loss.
T: When usage is uncertain and the project may stop soon, avoid commitments and pay on demand; commit only once usage is proven and stable.

G: ## The data flywheel
A data flywheel is a self-reinforcing loop:
1. More customers use the product.
2. Their interactions generate proprietary data (clicks, purchases, feedback).
3. That data improves the model.
4. Better recommendations attract and retain more customers, and the loop repeats.
## Why it is hard to copy
A competitor can use the same algorithms or foundation models, but it cannot download your users' behavior history. Every month the loop runs, the gap in data and model quality can grow. This is why proprietary data is often described as a moat, while features built only on public models are easy to match.
The advantage still needs maintenance: data quality, privacy compliance and continued model improvement keep the flywheel turning.
W: An advantage from a hard-to-copy proprietary data flywheel: usage creates unique data, the data improves the engine, and the better engine drives more usage, a loop competitors cannot replicate without the same users and history.
X: A=Temporary first-mover advantage: being first alone erodes once others launch, but here the advantage compounds with continued usage and proprietary data, which makes it durable.
B=Seat-based pricing structure: seat pricing describes how customers are charged per user and says nothing about why the engine improves or is hard to copy.
D=Build-buy-partner sourcing decision: that is about how to acquire the capability, whereas the scenario explains why the resulting capability stays ahead of competitors.
T: Proprietary data that grows with usage and keeps improving the model is a durable advantage; being first or picking a pricing model is not.

G: ## Sizing AI investment
Both extremes are risky:
- **Overinvesting**: large budgets committed before any use case has shown value, spread across too many projects, or on infrastructure no one uses yet.
- **Underinvesting**: waiting until the market is fully proven, which lets competitors build data, skills and customer expectations first.
## The incremental approach
1. Pick a few use cases tied to measurable business value (cost, revenue, risk).
2. Run small pilots with clear success criteria and baselines.
3. Scale the ones that meet their targets, stop the ones that do not, and reinvest the lessons.
Managed and pay-as-you-go services, such as Amazon Bedrock, help keep early costs proportional to use.
W: Scale investment incrementally, starting with value-tied pilots: limited capital goes first to use cases with measurable value, results decide what gets more funding, and the company builds capability without betting everything up front.
X: A=Avoid AI until competitors prove the market: removing all risk means entering late, after rivals have built data advantages and customer expectations have moved on, which is underinvestment.

C=Equal investment across every possible use case: spreading scarce funds evenly starves the most valuable projects and funds weak ones, so none gets enough to succeed.
T: With limited capital, fund a few value-tied pilots and scale what works, avoiding both all-in bets and waiting on the sidelines.

G: ## Data network effects
A **network effect** means a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. A marketplace already has one: more sellers attract more buyers and vice versa.
A **data network effect** adds a second loop: every search, click and transaction becomes training data that improves the matching algorithm, so each new user also makes matches better for everyone else.
## Why it becomes a moat
- A newcomer starts with few users and little data, so its matches are worse, which makes it harder to attract users.
- The leader's advantage grows with scale rather than shrinking.
- The algorithm itself can often be reproduced; the accumulated users and interaction data cannot.
The advantage still needs ongoing investment in data quality, model updates and trust to keep compounding.
W: Value compounds with adoption; scale and data are hard to copy: each additional buyer and seller improves matching for all users, and competitors cannot replicate the accumulated user base and interaction data even if they write a similar algorithm.
X: B=Competitors can't legally use similar algorithms: matching algorithms are widely known and generally not legally exclusive; the advantage comes from users and data, not legal barriers.
C=Needs no further investment once launched: the model must keep being retrained, monitored and improved, and the platform must keep earning trust, or the advantage erodes.
D=Code is secret and can't be reverse-engineered: similar algorithms can be built independently from public research; secrecy of the code is not what makes the advantage durable.
T: Durability comes from compounding users and data (data network effects), not from secret or legally protected algorithms.

G: ## Responsible AI by design
Responsible AI practices are most effective and cheapest when they shape the system from the start, the same idea as security by design.
At the design stage, the team can:
- define intended use, users and out-of-scope uses
- run a risk and impact assessment (who could be harmed, how badly)
- choose data sources and check them for bias and consent
- set fairness, safety and quality metrics and acceptance thresholds
- plan guardrails, human oversight and monitoring
These practices then continue through development, testing, launch and operation.
## Why not later
Discovering bias after the feature is built can mean new data, retraining or redesign. Discovering it after launch adds harm to users, reputational damage and possible regulatory exposure.
W: During initial design, before development begins: early risk assessment and bias planning shape data, model and product choices when changes are cheapest and before anyone can be harmed.
X: A=Only after a customer complaint: waiting for complaints means harm has already reached users, and fixing it then is slower, costlier and damages trust.
C=Only after the feature is fully built: issues found at that stage often require redesign or new data, and fixed design choices limit which mitigations are still possible.
D=Only when a regulator requests an audit: this is purely reactive and treats responsible AI as compliance paperwork rather than a way to prevent harm.
T: Integrate responsible AI at design time and keep it through the lifecycle, rather than reacting to complaints, launches or audits.

G: ## Speed versus risk in generative AI launches
Launch decisions often trade time-to-market against risk reduction.

G: ## Human oversight and decision stakes
The amount of human oversight should rise with the potential impact of an AI decision on people.
- **Low stakes**: product recommendations, email sorting. Full automation is usually acceptable, with monitoring.
- **High stakes**: hiring, termination, credit, insurance, healthcare, legal outcomes. Errors can seriously harm individuals, and many regulations and ethics frameworks expect meaningful human review and a way to contest the outcome.
## Patterns
- **Human-in-the-loop**: a person reviews and approves before the decision takes effect.
- **Human-on-the-loop**: a person monitors and can intervene.
AWS offers Amazon Augmented AI (A2I) to route selected predictions to human reviewers, for example low-confidence or high-impact cases.
W: High-stakes decisions affecting people: terminating employment seriously affects someone's livelihood, so a human must review the AI's recommendation, check context the model may miss, and remain accountable.
X: A=Only customer decisions, not employees: employees are affected just as seriously by AI decisions, and employment decisions are widely treated as high-stakes.
B=Never, if the model was tested before launch: pre-launch testing cannot catch every individual error or drift over time, and it does not provide accountability for decisions about specific people.
C=Only below 50% accuracy: even a highly accurate model makes errors, and in termination decisions each error harms a real person, so oversight is needed regardless of an accuracy threshold.
T: The higher the impact on people, the more human oversight is needed; accuracy or pre-launch testing does not replace review of high-stakes decisions.

G: ## Transparency and model documentation
**Transparency** in responsible AI means giving stakeholders enough information to understand what a system is, how it was built, what it is for, and where it can fail.
A **model card** is a standard document that records:
- intended use cases and out-of-scope uses
- training data sources at a suitable level of detail
- evaluation results and known limitations or risks
AWS supports this with Amazon SageMaker Model Cards for documenting your own models, and publishes AWS AI Service Cards for some of its own AI services.
## Why it matters
Customers, auditors and regulators use this information to decide whether a model fits their use, to spot risks early, and to hold providers accountable. Withholding it makes users rely on the system blindly.
W: Reduced transparency, which can erode trust: without information on data sources, limitations and intended use, stakeholders cannot judge whether the model is appropriate, which weakens trust and accountability.
X: A=Reduced robustness against adversarial attacks: robustness depends on how the model is built and defended, not on whether its documentation is public, so hiding a model card does not directly weaken it.
B=Lower costs with no offsetting risk: skipping publication may save a little effort, but it carries the risk of lost trust and harder accountability, so it is not risk-free.
D=Improved fairness across groups: withholding documentation does not change how the model treats different groups, and it makes fairness harder for others to verify.
T: Model cards support transparency; keeping training data, limitations and intended use secret mainly costs trust and accountability.
