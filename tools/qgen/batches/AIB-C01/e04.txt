@ Q000217 explain
G: ## What "AI-ready data" means
A model can only be as good as the data it learns from or retrieves. Data readiness is usually judged on four factors:
- **Quality**: values are accurate, consistent and not duplicated. A customer listed three times with three birthdates teaches a model noise.
- **Completeness**: the records and fields the use case needs actually exist, for example two years of order history rather than six months.
- **Accessibility**: the right teams and systems can reach the data through catalogued, documented sources instead of email exports.
- **Governance**: every dataset has an owner, access rules, privacy handling and lineage, so you know where it came from and who may use it.
## A quick example
A retailer wants a demand forecast. If sales data is accurate but locked in a store system nobody can query, it is not ready. If it is accessible but half the stores never upload returns, it is incomplete. All four factors must hold together.
W: Quality, accessibility, completeness and governance: these are the properties that decide whether data can be trusted, reached and used lawfully by an AI system, so they are what a readiness assessment measures.
X: B=Server purchase dates: hardware age is an infrastructure question. New servers holding inaccurate, ungoverned data still produce unreliable AI results.
C=File names, folder colors and fonts: these are cosmetic presentation details. They say nothing about whether the content is accurate, complete or permitted for use.
D=Number and brands of databases: counting systems or naming vendors describes the landscape, not the condition of the data inside it. Ten well-governed sources can be ready while one messy source is not.
T: Data readiness is about the data itself (quality, completeness, access, governance), not the hardware or tools that happen to store it.

G: ## Why fragmented data blocks AI at scale
When the same customer lives in twelve legacy systems, each copy tends to drift: different addresses, different IDs, different policy statuses. An AI model or assistant built on top of that data inherits the contradictions.
Two things fix this:
- **Integration**: bring the sources together, for example into a governed data lake or warehouse with a shared customer identifier, so AI workloads read one consistent view.
- **Ownership**: name a data owner (and data stewards) accountable for accuracy, definitions and who may access the data. Without an owner, nobody fixes errors and nobody approves access.
## Example
An insurer building a claims-triage model needs policy, claims and customer data joined correctly. If no team owns the customer master and the systems cannot be joined reliably, the model will mis-link claims no matter how good the algorithm is.
W: Clear data ownership and integrated sources: the scenario names two root problems, disconnected systems and no accountable team. Integration gives AI one consistent view, and ownership makes someone responsible for keeping it accurate and controlled.
X: B=More sales representatives: headcount in sales changes revenue capacity, not the state of customer data spread across twelve systems.
C=Fewer AI use cases: narrowing the portfolio can focus effort, but every remaining use case still depends on the same fragmented, unowned data, so the foundation problem remains.
D=Bigger marketing budget: promotion affects demand and awareness. It does nothing to reconcile records or assign accountability for data accuracy.
T: Before scaling AI, integrate scattered sources and give the data an accountable owner; business spending elsewhere does not repair the data foundation.

G: ## Garbage in, garbage out for fine-tuning
Fine-tuning adapts a foundation model by training it further on your examples. The model learns whatever patterns are in those examples, including mistakes.
- **Duplicates** make the model over-weight repeated examples and can make evaluation scores look better than they are if duplicates land in both training and test sets.
- **Inconsistent labels** teach contradictory answers, for example the same complaint labeled "billing" in one row and "technical" in another.
- **Missing metadata** makes it hard to filter, balance or trace data, so you cannot tell which examples came from where.
## Spending a limited budget
Compute for a training run is spent whether the data is good or bad. Cleaning, deduplicating and cataloging first (for example with data preparation tools and a data catalog) means the one run you can afford produces a useful model.
W: Clean, deduplicate and catalog first: fixing the data raises the quality of any later fine-tuning run, so the scarce compute budget buys a better model instead of learning noise.
X: A=Fine-tune now on existing data: the model would absorb duplicated and contradictory examples, and the limited compute would be spent producing a weaker model that likely needs retraining.
B=Skip fine-tuning, use prompts only: prompt engineering is a valid alternative in some cases, but abandoning the plan does not address the data problems, and cleaned data may still be needed for evaluation or retrieval.
D=Buy more GPU instances: extra compute only makes training on flawed data finish sooner. It also spends the limited budget without improving the result.
T: With a tight budget, invest in data quality before training; more compute on bad data just produces a bad model faster.

A data strategy defines which data the organization collects, the quality standards it must meet, how it is governed and where it lives. Data infrastructure is the shared platform that implements it, for example a common data lake with a catalog and consistent pipelines.

D=Too many prompt-engineering trainees: better-trained users would generally improve results. Training does not create inconsistency in the underlying data.
T: Consistent, fair AI outputs across regions require one data strategy and shared infrastructure; organizational factors unrelated to data do not explain data-driven inconsistency.

G: ## The four pillars of AI infrastructure
Planning infrastructure for AI means checking each layer the workload touches:
- **Compute**: training needs bursts of accelerated capacity (GPUs or AWS Trainium), and inference needs capacity that scales with traffic. Cloud services let you add capacity for a training spike and release it afterward.
- **Storage**: large, durable and cost-effective storage for training data, checkpoints and model artifacts, such as Amazon S3.
- **Network**: enough bandwidth and low enough latency to move data to compute and to serve predictions, including hybrid links to on-premises sites.
- **Integration**: secure connections to systems of record such as an ERP, through APIs, connectors or data pipelines, so models get current data and results flow back into business processes.
## Example
A forecasting model that reads ERP orders nightly needs a pipeline from the ERP, storage for history, training capacity once a week and an endpoint the ERP can call. Missing any one layer breaks the solution.
W: Compute, storage, network and integration: training spikes stress compute and storage, and the ERP connection depends on network and integration, so all four must be evaluated together.
X: A=Only office Wi-Fi speed: employee Wi-Fi affects end-user connectivity, not training capacity, data storage or system-to-system ERP integration.
B=Only laptops per employee: end-user devices do not run large training jobs or host integrations; the heavy work runs on servers or cloud services.
C=Only email storage quota: mailbox size is unrelated to where training data, models or ERP integrations live.
T: AI infrastructure planning covers compute, storage, network and integration as a set; office IT details do not address training spikes or ERP connections.

G: ## Classifying readiness gaps
AI readiness assessments sort findings into categories so each gap goes to the team that can fix it. Common categories:
- **Data gaps**: problems with the data itself, such as inconsistent formats, duplicates, missing fields or unclear ownership. Fixed by data engineering and governance.
- **Technology gaps**: problems with infrastructure and tools, such as compute that cannot scale, missing platforms or weak integration. Fixed by platform and IT teams, often with cloud services.
- **People and culture gaps**: missing skills, resistance to change, low trust in AI.
- **Leadership and governance gaps**: missing sponsorship, unclear decision rights or policies.
## Applying it
"Customer records formatted differently across systems" describes the condition of data, so it is a data gap. "Servers cannot scale for real-time inference" describes infrastructure capacity, so it is a technology gap.
W: Finding 1 is a data gap, Finding 2 a technology gap: inconsistent records are a property of the data, while servers that cannot scale are a property of the infrastructure, so they belong to different categories and different owners.
X: B=Same type of gap: one finding is about data content and the other about compute capacity. Grouping them would send both to one team that can fix only one of them.
C=Both workforce culture gaps: neither finding mentions skills, attitudes or resistance. Both are concrete data and infrastructure conditions.
D=Technology gap and leadership gap: formatting inconsistencies are a data problem, not a technology gap, and server scaling is a technical capacity issue, not a leadership one.
T: Classify each readiness finding by what is actually broken (data content versus infrastructure) so the right team owns the fix.

G: ## Centralized versus decentralized data governance
- **Centralized**: one team sets data policies, definitions and quality standards for the whole enterprise. Strength: consistency. Risk: slower decisions for business units.
- **Decentralized**: each business unit sets its own rules. Strength: speed and local fit. Risk: every unit defines "customer" or "active account" differently.
- **Federated (hybrid)**: a central team owns core standards (shared definitions, quality rules, security and privacy policies), while business units own their domain data within those rules. Many enterprises land here.
## Why core policies matter for AI
Enterprise AI combines data from many units. If each unit uses its own quality bar and definitions, the combined dataset is inconsistent and models trained or grounded on it behave inconsistently. Centralizing the core rules gives every unit a common baseline.
W: Consistent quality and standards: centralizing core ownership policies means every dataset feeding enterprise AI follows the same definitions and quality bar, which is the main argument for centralizing before scaling.
X: A=Decentralized always gives higher quality: nothing guarantees this. Independent standards often produce inconsistent quality across units, which is the risk centralization addresses.
C=Units never need their own data: centralized policy does not remove business units' access to data; they still use it daily, just under shared rules.
D=Centralization removes quality controls: central governance is where quality controls are defined and enforced, so it increases rather than eliminates them.
T: Centralize at least the core data standards so enterprise AI sees consistent data, accepting some loss of local agility in exchange.

G: ## Cultural barriers and how to overcome them
When employees fear that AI will replace their judgment, a mandate tends to create quiet resistance: people work around the tool, report problems loudly and trust it less.
Change management practice for AI adoption:
- **Involve users early**: let dispatchers test the tool in a pilot and shape how it fits their work.
- **Position AI as augmentation**: show that the tool proposes and the dispatcher decides, keeping humans in control of exceptions.
- **Train** people on how the tool works and when to override it.
- **Collect feedback** and visibly act on it, so users see their concerns changing the product.
- **Use champions**: respected dispatchers who adopt early can persuade peers better than leadership memos.
## Example
A two-week mandate might reach 100 percent installation but low real use; a phased pilot with dispatcher input starts smaller but builds real, lasting adoption.
W: Engage dispatchers early through pilots and training: it addresses the actual barrier (fear and distrust) by giving users hands-on experience, a voice in the design and the skills to use the tool with confidence.
X: A=Quietly monitor compliance: surveillance without communication confirms fears and erodes trust, making resistance worse.
C=Cancel the initiative permanently: skepticism is a normal, fixable barrier. Cancelling gives up the business value instead of managing the change.
D=Mandate full adoption immediately: forcing adoption on skeptical users usually leads to workarounds and resentment, so usage on paper does not become real adoption.
T: Overcome cultural resistance by involving users through pilots, training and feedback; mandates and surveillance deepen distrust.

G: ## Build, buy or borrow AI skills
Organizations get AI skills in three main ways:
- **Build (upskill)**: train existing employees. Lower cost per person, keeps business knowledge and creates buy-in because people are part of the change.
- **Buy (hire)**: bring in specialists for deep skills that are hard to train quickly, such as ML engineering or AI architecture. Faster for niche expertise but expensive.
- **Borrow (partners and consultants)**: useful to start quickly or fill gaps, but capability leaves when the engagement ends.
## Blending them
Most organizations combine these. With a limited budget and a need for buy-in, the usual balance is to upskill the broader workforce and make a small number of targeted specialist hires who can lead technical work and coach others. Partners can accelerate early projects while knowledge transfers in-house.
W: Upskill existing staff, adding a few specialist hires: upskilling builds skills and buy-in cost-effectively, while a handful of hires covers expertise that cannot be trained quickly enough.
X: A=Replace the workforce with AI specialists: extremely costly, loses business knowledge and destroys the buy-in the company is trying to build.
C=Avoid hiring or training: unsupervised learning is slow and uneven and does not create either expertise or organizational commitment on any reliable timeline.
D=Rely only on external consultants: consultants can help early, but permanent dependence is expensive and leaves no internal capability or ownership.
T: Balance cost, speed and buy-in by upskilling existing staff and adding a few specialists, rather than replacing people or outsourcing everything.

G: ## Incremental deployment of AI models
No model is perfect, and waiting for perfection means the business never benefits. Recommended practice is to deploy a model once it is good enough to add value, but in a controlled way:
- **Limited scope**: start with a subset of transactions, a region or a product line.
- **Safeguards**: keep existing controls running, add human review for high-impact decisions, and define thresholds that trigger rollback.
- **Monitoring**: track accuracy, false positives, drift and business impact in production.
- **Iterate**: retrain and improve with real-world feedback, then widen the scope as performance is proven.
Patterns such as shadow deployment (the model scores traffic without acting) or canary deployment (a small share of live traffic) support this.
## Example
A fraud model that already catches more fraud than current rules is applied to card-not-present transactions first, with analysts reviewing its flags. Results guide improvements before it covers all payments.
W: Deploy to a limited scope now and improve iteratively: the bank gains value from an already-effective model while controlling risk and using real data to make it better.
X: A=Wait until the model is perfect: perfection is unattainable, so the model would never ship and fraud losses continue in the meantime.
B=Abandon the initiative: an effective model already exists; dropping it discards real value because of an unrealistic target.
D=Deploy to all transactions immediately: full rollout without a proving stage exposes every customer to any undiscovered error, such as false positives that block legitimate payments.
T: Ship an effective AI model to a limited scope with monitoring and improve it iteratively, instead of waiting for perfection or rolling out everywhere at once.
