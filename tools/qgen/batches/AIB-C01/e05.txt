@ Q000243 explain
G: ## From proof of concept to production
A proof of concept (PoC) answers one question: can this work? Production asks different ones: will it keep working, can we tell when it stops, and can we undo a bad change quickly?
**MLOps** is the set of practices that answers those questions for ML and generative AI systems:
- **Versioning**: pin the model version, prompts and configuration, so a provider or team update does not silently change behavior.
- **Monitoring**: track output quality, error rates, latency and cost, with alerts when they drift.
- **Controlled release and rollback**: test a new model version against an evaluation set, roll it out gradually, and switch back to the last known-good version if results degrade.
**Business continuity safeguards** add a fallback (a manual or rules-based path) so the customer process keeps running while the AI is fixed.
## Example
A team calls a model through a "latest" alias. The provider updates the model, answer formats change, and a downstream parser breaks. With a pinned version, an evaluation gate and a rollback plan, the update would have been caught before customers saw it.
W: Missing MLOps and continuity safeguards: no version pinning, no monitoring and no rollback meant an unexpected model update reached customers unnoticed and could not be reversed quickly, which is exactly the failure described.
X: B=Overinvestment in training: training people to use AI builds skills and adoption; it does not change model versions or remove rollback plans, so it cannot explain inconsistent outputs after an update.
C=Excessive executive sponsorship: sponsorship provides funding and priority. The disruption came from missing operational controls, not from too much leadership support.
D=Too much collaboration: cross-functional work (IT, security, business) usually helps a PoC reach production safely; it is not what let an untested model change hit a customer process.
T: A PoC proves feasibility; production needs MLOps (versioning, monitoring, rollback) and a fallback, otherwise any model change becomes a customer-facing incident.

@ Q000244 explain
G: ## Why problems appear at scale
A pilot with 50 users sees a narrow slice of real behavior. At 5,000 users the system meets new question types, languages, edge cases, peak loads and people who use it in ways nobody planned. Some of these issues never show up in pilot metrics.
## Feedback loops
**Continuous feedback mechanisms** collect signals from real use and route them back into improvement:
- Explicit feedback: thumbs up/down, ratings, "report a wrong answer" buttons.
- Implicit feedback: users rephrasing the same question, abandoning a session, or escalating to a human.
- Operational signals: error reports, support tickets, usage analytics by team or task.
The team reviews these signals, groups them into themes (for example "wrong answers about the new pricing"), fixes prompts, data or workflows, and measures again.
## Feedback and monitoring together
Technical monitoring shows that latency rose or errors spiked. User feedback shows that answers are unhelpful even when the system is technically healthy. Scaling safely needs both.
W: Surface issues for iterative improvement: feedback from a much larger and more varied user base reveals problems the pilot could not show, and gives the team concrete evidence for the next round of fixes.
X: A=Replace monitoring: feedback complements technical monitoring. Users do not report latency, cost spikes or silent failures reliably, so dashboards and alarms are still needed.
B=Guarantee no new issues: no mechanism prevents new issues; feedback helps you find and fix them sooner once they appear.
D=Only useful during the pilot: feedback becomes more valuable at scale, because more users bring more edge cases and the cost of an unnoticed problem grows.
T: Scaling exposes issues a pilot never sees; continuous feedback surfaces them so the solution improves iteratively, alongside, not instead of, monitoring.

G: ## The shared responsibility model for managed AI
AWS splits security and operations duties with customers:
- **AWS is responsible for security OF the cloud**: physical data centers, hardware, networking, the host operating systems and virtualization layer, and the managed service software itself.
- **The customer is responsible for security IN the cloud**: their data, identities and permissions, configuration, and how they use the results.
Amazon Bedrock is a fully managed service, so the customer never sees or patches servers. The customer's share moves to data and access.
## What that means for fine-tuning
When you fine-tune a model in Bedrock, you supply training data from Amazon S3 and receive a custom model. You decide:
- Whether that data is accurate, representative, free of content you are not allowed to use, and handled under your data governance rules (for example, removing personal data you do not need).
- Who can invoke or manage the custom model, through IAM policies.
- Encryption choices such as your own KMS keys, and how outputs are used.
W: Fine-tuning data quality, governance and model access: these are the customer's inputs and configuration. AWS cannot judge whether your training data is correct or appropriate, and you control who can use the resulting model.
X: A=Host OS patching: in a fully managed service like Bedrock, AWS patches the underlying hosts; customers have no access to that layer.
B=Data center physical security: physical security of facilities is always an AWS responsibility.
C=Hardware maintenance: AWS owns and maintains the servers and accelerators that run the models.
T: With a managed AI service, AWS runs the infrastructure; you own your data, its quality and governance, and who gets access.

G: ## Two ways to scale a successful AI use case
- **Horizontal scaling**: reuse the solution in other departments or regions that have a similar need, for example taking a claims-summarization assistant from auto insurance to home insurance.
- **Vertical scaling**: deepen the solution where it already works, for example adding more document types, integrations or automation for the original team before going elsewhere.

- **Similarity of processes and data**: if other departments follow similar workflows and have comparable data (formats, quality, access), the solution transfers with little rework. If their processes differ, horizontal expansion becomes a new project.
- **Solution maturity**: a solution that is still unstable, lacks monitoring or has open quality issues should usually be strengthened first; expanding it spreads the problems.
Other practical inputs are business value in each target area, sponsor support and the capacity of the team to support more users.
W: Process and data similarity plus maturity: these determine whether the solution can be reused with little change and whether it is robust enough to be expanded, which is the core of the horizontal-versus-vertical decision.
X: A=Founding date: when the company was founded says nothing about whether departments share processes or data, or how mature the solution is.
C=Press releases issued: publicity reflects communication activity, not whether the solution fits other departments or is ready to scale.
D=Number of UI colors: interface styling has no bearing on data compatibility, process fit or production readiness.
T: Scale sideways where processes and data are similar and the solution is mature; otherwise deepen it where it already works first.

- Share lessons learned and build skills across teams.

G: ## When AI becomes mission critical
Once a process such as claims triage depends on an AI system, an AI failure is a business outage. AI systems can fail in two ways:
- **Hard failure**: the service or model endpoint is unavailable, times out or hits quota limits.
- **Soft failure (degradation)**: the system keeps responding but quality drops, for example after data drift, a model update or a change in claim types. This is harder to notice.
## Business continuity for AI
- **Monitoring**: availability, latency and error metrics, plus quality indicators such as reviewer override rates or sampled accuracy checks, with alerts.
- **Fallback procedures**: a documented way to keep triaging claims without the AI, such as routing to human adjusters or a simpler rules-based queue, with clear triggers for when to switch.
- **Tested recovery**: rehearse the fallback, keep a known-good model version to roll back to, and define who decides.
W: Fallback procedures and monitoring: monitoring detects outages and quality degradation, and fallback procedures keep claims moving while the AI is fixed, which is the essence of business continuity.
X: A=Vendor uptime marketing claims: advertised availability is not a plan. It does not cover quality degradation and says nothing about what your team does when the service fails.
B=Remove human reviewers: human reviewers are often the fallback path; removing them to save cost leaves no way to operate if the AI degrades.
D=Deploy untested versions: skipping testing raises the chance of a bad release that breaks a critical process, the opposite of continuity.
T: Treat mission-critical AI like any critical system: monitor for outages and quality drops, and keep a tested fallback ready.

G: ## Prioritizing a large AI backlog
With 25 ideas and budget for three, the question is which projects will deliver real value and can actually be delivered. A common method is a **value-feasibility matrix**:
- **Business value**: revenue impact, cost savings, risk reduction, customer experience, strategic fit.
- **Feasibility**: data availability and quality, technical complexity, integration effort, skills, regulatory and responsible AI risk, time to value.
Each use case is scored on both axes, for example 1 to 5, and plotted on a 2x2 grid.
- **High value, high feasibility**: start here; these become early wins that fund and justify the program.
- **High value, low feasibility**: plan for later, after data or platform groundwork.
- **Low value**: deprioritize, however interesting the technology.
## Example
An internal claims-document summarizer with good data and moderate risk might score high on both axes, while a fully automated customer-facing claims decision engine may be valuable but low on feasibility because of regulatory risk.
W: Score on value and feasibility: consistent criteria let the insurer pick the three use cases most likely to deliver measurable value within budget and build momentum for the rest.
X: A=Most advanced technology: technical novelty is not business value, and cutting-edge approaches are often the least feasible to deliver in a year.
B=Every department in parallel: no central criteria means more than three projects compete for a three-project budget, spreading funds thin with no way to compare value.
C=Highest-risk customer-facing first: starting with the riskiest use case raises the chance of a visible failure that can stall the whole program.
T: Prioritize AI use cases on value times feasibility and start with those high on both, not on novelty or visibility.

G: ## Why ROI needs a baseline
Return on investment compares the situation after a change with the situation before it. If nobody measured "before", any improvement is a guess.
A sound measurement plan for a contact-center assistant defines, before the pilot:
- **KPIs tied to business outcomes**: average handle time, first-contact resolution, after-call work time, customer satisfaction, agent ramp-up time.

- **Targets and a method**: for example "reduce average handle time from 7.5 to 6.5 minutes in 3 months", with a comparison group of agents who do not yet use the assistant.
## Example
Six months after launch, handle time is 6.8 minutes. Without a baseline, leadership cannot tell whether that is an improvement, seasonal noise or no change at all.

T: No baseline, no proof: capture KPIs and set targets before launch so the value of an AI solution can be demonstrated.

G: ## Management system standards
A **management system standard** defines how an organization sets policies, assigns roles, assesses risk, runs controls, measures results and continually improves (the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle). An accredited certification body can audit and certify that the system meets the standard.

G: ## Why chatbots invent policies
A foundation model generates likely-sounding text from patterns in its training data. When asked about a specific retailer's return policy it does not know, it can produce a confident but wrong answer, known as a **hallucination**. In a customer-facing channel, that can create financial, legal and reputational risk.
## Layered mitigation
- **Grounding with RAG**: retrieval-augmented generation fetches relevant passages from approved policy documents (for example with Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases) and instructs the model to answer only from them.
- **Guardrails**: Amazon Bedrock Guardrails can apply contextual grounding checks that flag or block responses not supported by the retrieved source, plus denied topics and content filters.
- **Human escalation**: when the answer is not supported or confidence is low, the bot hands off to a human agent instead of guessing.
## Example
A customer asks about returning opened electronics. The bot retrieves the current policy passage, answers from it, and if no relevant passage exists it says so and transfers the chat.
W: RAG, grounding guardrails and human escalation: answers come from approved documents, unsupported answers are caught, and uncertain cases go to people, which reduces both how often invented policies occur and their impact.
X: A=Disclaimer only: a disclaimer does not stop the bot from inventing policies, and customers may still act on the wrong answers.
C=Bigger model, no human agents: a larger model can still hallucinate about company-specific policies, and removing humans takes away the safety net.
D=Increase creativity settings: raising temperature makes outputs more varied and less predictable, which tends to increase invented content.
T: Reduce hallucination risk in customer-facing bots by grounding answers in approved sources, checking grounding with guardrails and escalating to humans.

Generative AI costs are usage based and can grow fast. If all calls come from shared accounts with no labels, the bill shows one large number and nobody owns it.

G: ## When everyone has the same model
Foundation models are widely available: any competitor can call the same model through the same services. The model on its own is therefore not a lasting advantage, and prompts or model choices are easy to copy.

- **Customer relationships and distribution**: trust, channels and feedback loops that keep improving the solution with real usage.

G: ## Why hiring AI is treated as high risk
Automated applicant screening affects access to employment and can reproduce historical bias, for example penalizing career gaps or schools correlated with protected characteristics. Many regulators treat it as high risk:
- The EU AI Act lists AI used for recruitment and candidate selection as a high-risk category with requirements such as risk management, documentation, human oversight and transparency.
- Some local laws, such as New York City's rules on automated employment decision tools, require bias audits and notice to candidates.
Requirements differ by jurisdiction, so a multinational deployment needs a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction review.
## Governance practices
- Classify the system as high risk in the AI inventory.
- Run impact and bias assessments before launch and periodically afterwards.
- Document data, model, intended use and limitations.
- Keep humans responsible for final decisions and inform candidates where required.
- Remember that using a vendor's tool does not transfer the employer's accountability.

D=Let the vendor handle compliance: the vendor can support compliance, but the employer making hiring decisions remains accountable for how the tool is used.

G: ## Activity metrics versus outcome metrics
Executives want to know whether an investment improved the business. Metrics fall into two groups:
- **Activity and technical metrics**: number of prompts, tokens consumed, model size, team size. They show usage or effort, not whether anything got better.
- **Business outcome metrics**: results customers and the company care about.
## Outcome metrics for a customer-service assistant
- **Containment rate**: share of contacts resolved without handing off to a human agent.
- **Customer satisfaction (CSAT)**: whether customers were happy with the answer.
- **Cost per resolved contact**: total support cost divided by contacts resolved, which captures both savings and quality.
Each should be compared with a **baseline** measured before launch. For example, containment rising from 20% to 45% while CSAT stays stable and cost per contact drops from 6 to 4 dollars is a clear value story.
W: Containment, CSAT and cost per resolved contact against baseline: these connect the assistant directly to efficiency and customer experience and show the change it caused.
X: A=Model parameter count: a technical property of the model that says nothing about customer outcomes or cost savings.
C=Number of engineers: this measures investment and effort, which is an input, not a result.
D=Prompts and tokens: usage volume shows the tool is being used and drives cost, but high usage can coexist with poor answers and no savings.
T: Show AI value with business outcome metrics compared with a baseline, not with activity or technical measures.

G: ## Shared responsibility for a Bedrock application
Amazon Bedrock is fully managed. AWS runs the infrastructure and the model hosting; the customer builds an application on top of it.
**AWS is responsible for** physical data centers, hardware, the hypervisor and host operating systems, the global network, and operating the Bedrock service and model hosting.
**The customer is responsible for**:
- **Access**: IAM policies that control who and what can invoke models, plus authentication in the application.
- **Prompt and context data**: what data is sent to the model, whether it includes personal or confidential data, and encryption choices such as customer managed KMS keys.
- **Guardrails**: configuring Amazon Bedrock Guardrails for denied topics, content filters, sensitive information filters and grounding checks that fit the use case.
- **Output use**: reviewing and deciding how model responses are used, for example human review before sending generated text to customers.
W: App access, prompt data, guardrails and output use: these are configuration and data decisions only the customer can make, so they sit on the customer's side of the model.
X: B=Physical data center security: securing facilities is always AWS's responsibility.
C=Hypervisor patching: the virtualization layer is part of the infrastructure AWS operates and patches.
D=Global network backbone: AWS builds and maintains its global network; customers have no control over it.
T: On Bedrock, AWS secures the infrastructure; you secure access, the data you send, the guardrails you configure and how you use the outputs.
