G: ## Why AI tools fail after launch
Most AI rollouts do not fail because the tool is weak; they fail because people keep working the old way. Change management is the work of helping people move from "this is new" to "this is how I work now".
People adopt a tool when they can answer one question: **what does this do for me, today?** A generic announcement rarely answers it. A role-specific example does.
- For a sales team: drafting a follow-up email from call notes in two minutes instead of fifteen.
- For HR: turning a policy change into a clear FAQ for employees.
- For support: summarizing a long ticket thread before replying.
Good rollouts combine these examples with training, internal champions who help colleagues, and an easy channel for questions and feedback, so early problems get fixed instead of quietly driving people away.
W: D=Role-specific examples of value: showing each team how the assistant helps with its own daily tasks makes the benefit concrete and personal, which is the strongest driver of voluntary, lasting adoption.
X: A=One email, no support: a single announcement creates awareness but no skill or habit; when users hit their first problem there is nobody to help, so most stop using the tool.
B=Secret rollout: hiding the tool removes the chance to gather feedback, build champions and address concerns early, and a surprise launch tends to breed suspicion rather than trust.
C=Quotas with public shaming: forced usage targets and naming people create fear and box-ticking use, not real value; people learn to game the metric and resent the tool.
T: Adoption follows perceived personal value, so show each team concrete wins in its own work instead of relying on announcements, secrecy or pressure.

G: ## Same tool, different results
When one AI assistant is rolled out everywhere and results differ sharply between offices, the model is usually the same in every office. What differs is how people use it:
- **Training**: one office had hands-on sessions; another got a link to a user guide.
- **Workflow fit**: one office built the assistant into its daily process; another treats it as optional.
- **Prompts and inputs**: high performers often share prompt templates and give the tool better context.
- **Local champions**: someone who answers questions and spreads tips makes a large difference.
The first step is diagnosis, not a fix. Compare usage data and talk to users in strong and weak offices, then spread the practices that work. Example: if the top office uses a shared prompt library for customer emails, publishing that library to every office is a quick, cheap improvement.
W: A=Compare usage and share best practices: investigating how offices use the tool finds the real cause of the variation, and spreading what high performers do is the fastest, lowest-cost way to lift weaker offices.
X: B=Stop measuring by office: large, consistent gaps between offices are a signal, not random noise; hiding them removes the data needed to find and fix the cause.
C=Retrain per office: this assumes a model problem with no evidence; the same model usually serves every office, and separate retraining is costly while leaving process and training gaps untouched.
D=Remove the tool without investigating: pulling it from weaker offices throws away value that better training or workflow changes could unlock, and nobody learns why results differed.
T: When results vary across offices using the same model, investigate usage first and spread what works before assuming the model is at fault.

G: ## The speed-versus-control dilemma
Security reviews exist to protect data, but when every new AI tool waits months for approval, teams either stop learning or go around the rules ("shadow AI"). Both outcomes are bad.
A common middle path is a **pre-approved sandbox**: an environment that the security team approves once, with guardrails built in, so teams can experiment inside it without a new review each time.
- Access limited to approved services and models, for example a sandbox AWS account that can use Amazon Bedrock with guardrails and logging turned on.
- Only synthetic, public or non-sensitive data; no customer records or production systems.
- Budgets, logging and time limits so experiments stay visible and contained.
When an experiment proves valuable, it then goes through the full review before touching production data. Security reviews one environment instead of every idea, and teams get speed.
W: B=Pre-approved governed sandbox: guardrails plus safe data let teams experiment immediately within limits security has already agreed to, while the full review is saved for the ideas that move toward production.
X: A=Personal cloud accounts: this is shadow IT that bypasses every control, so the company has no visibility into what data is used or where it goes, and nothing stops sensitive data from leaking.
C=Stop until everything is approved: approving every possible tool may never finish, so the company loses months of learning while competitors move ahead; it trades all speed for control.
D=Any tool with production data: "temporary" experiments can still leak production data to unapproved vendors permanently; the risk comes from the data exposure, not from how long the experiment lasts.
T: A pre-approved sandbox with guardrails and non-sensitive data gives teams speed inside controls, instead of choosing between no experiments and ungoverned ones.

@ Q002321 explain
G: ## Right-sizing the model
Foundation models come in different sizes. Larger models are generally more capable on hard reasoning tasks, but they cost more per token and are often slower. For many well-defined tasks, such as summarizing a document, a smaller model can be just as good.
The practical way to decide is to **evaluate on the real task**:
- Run both models on a representative set of documents.
- Have users or reviewers rate the summaries without knowing which model wrote them.
- Compare quality, latency and cost per request.
Example: if both models score the same with users but the smaller one costs one-fifth as much, a feature handling 1 million summaries a month keeps the same value for roughly 80% less model spend. Services such as Amazon Bedrock make this comparison easier because several models sit behind the same API, and model evaluation features can score outputs.
After switching, keep watching quality ratings: real traffic can include documents the test set missed.
W: B=Switch to the smaller model and keep monitoring: equal user-rated quality at a fraction of the cost means the same business value for less money, and ongoing monitoring catches any quality drop on real-world inputs.
X: A=Run both and let users choose: calling two models per request raises cost instead of lowering it, and asking users to compare two summaries every time adds work they did not ask for.
C=Keep the largest model: bigger is not automatically better value; when tests show equal quality for this task, the extra cost buys nothing users notice.
D=Remove the feature: the tests show a cheaper model delivers the same quality, so the feature can be made affordable; dropping it throws away value users rate highly.
T: Pick the smallest model that meets the quality bar for the task, then monitor; do not pay for capability users cannot see.

G: ## Cost allocation with tags
A tag is a key-value label attached to a cloud resource, such as `BusinessUnit=Claims` or `CostCenter=4410`. Once a tag key is activated as a **cost allocation tag** in AWS Billing, tools like AWS Cost Explorer and the Cost and Usage Report can group and filter spending by that tag.
For generative AI this matters because many teams often share the same services. Ways to make usage attributable include:
- Tagging the resources each team uses (endpoints, knowledge bases, agents, custom models).
- In Amazon Bedrock, creating an application inference profile per team or application and tagging it, so on-demand model calls made through that profile carry the team's tag.
- Using separate AWS accounts per business unit in AWS Organizations, which splits costs by account.
Example: Cost Explorer grouped by `BusinessUnit` shows Claims at 42% of the AI bill and Marketing at 8%, so finance knows who drives growth and each unit can own its budget. A tagging policy is needed so new resources are not left untagged.
W: C=Tag by business unit and report by tag: tags tie actual usage to its owner, so cost reports split spending precisely and each business unit sees and owns what it consumes.
X: A=Equal split: dividing the bill evenly ignores real usage, so light users subsidize heavy ones and nobody learns which unit is driving growth.
B=Quarterly self-estimates: surveys rely on guesses that are often wrong and arrive months late; they cannot be reconciled with the actual bill.
D=Charge everything to IT: managing the account is not the same as consuming the service; this hides the real drivers and removes any incentive for business units to control usage.
T: Attribute AI costs from actual usage data with tags or account separation, not with equal splits, guesses or charging the platform owner.

G: ## On-demand versus committed capacity
Most generative AI services are priced **on demand**: you pay per request or per token, with no commitment. That is ideal for spiky or uncertain traffic.
When traffic is steady and predictable, committed options can lower the unit cost or guarantee capacity. In Amazon Bedrock, **Provisioned Throughput** reserves model capacity (model units) for a term, and longer commitments typically carry lower hourly rates. Other AWS services follow the same idea, such as Savings Plans for compute.
The decision is a simple comparison:
- Estimate steady usage and what it costs on demand.
- Compare with the committed price for capacity sized to that volume.
- Consider the risk: if usage falls, you still pay for the commitment.
Example: a feature that runs at a stable, high volume all day may cost less on committed capacity than on per-token pricing; a feature used heavily only one week a month probably does not. Workloads that do not need immediate answers may also suit discounted batch processing.
W: D=Commit to capacity sized to steady volume: predictable, sustained usage is exactly where commitment-based pricing can reduce unit cost, and weighing the discount against the commitment risk keeps the decision sound.
X: A=Commitments never help: this is backwards; steady, predictable workloads are the classic case where commitments or reserved capacity lower cost.
B=Most expensive tier for volume discounts: higher model tiers generally cost more per token, and choosing a more expensive model does not trigger automatic discounts.
C=Turn the feature off every other day: this halves the bill by halving the value delivered, and users lose a feature they depend on; it cuts usage, not the price paid per unit.
T: For steady, predictable volume, compare committed or provisioned capacity with on-demand pricing; commitments pay off when utilization stays high.

G: ## Start from problems, not technology
Good AI opportunity discovery works backward from business pain, not forward from a list of AI features. The people who do the work know where time, money and quality are lost.
A typical discovery process:
- **Workshops** with business teams to map processes and list pain points, such as "agents spend 20 minutes reading each claim file".
- **Assessment** of each pain point: can AI realistically help, is the data available, what is the value if solved, and how hard is it?
- **Prioritization** on a value-versus-feasibility grid, picking a few high-value, feasible use cases for pilots.
Example: a workshop reveals that underwriters retype data from PDF forms. Document extraction fits well, the data exists and the time saved is measurable, so it becomes a strong first candidate. Because the business team named the problem, they are also more likely to adopt the solution.

X: B=Ask the biggest-spending vendor: a vendor recommends its own products, and marketing budget says nothing about which problems the company actually has.
C=Copy big companies in unrelated industries: their use cases reflect their own data, processes and customers, which may have little to do with this company's needs.
D=Apply every AI capability somewhere: this technology-first approach produces solutions looking for problems, spreads effort thin and rarely delivers measurable value.
T: Find AI opportunities by mapping real business pain with the people who feel it, then test AI fit, instead of starting from vendors, trends or technology lists.

G: ## When does moving first pay off?
Being first to market is only an advantage if the lead **compounds**. Otherwise fast followers can learn from the pioneer's mistakes and catch up cheaply.
Sources of lasting first-mover advantage with generative AI include:
- **Data flywheel**: early users generate feedback and usage data that improve the product, which attracts more users and more data.
- **Workflow integration**: once customers build the feature into their processes, switching becomes costly.
- **Proprietary learning**: the team learns what works for its specific customers faster than anyone else.
Example: a legal software company that launches first collects thousands of lawyer corrections on AI drafts. Those corrections improve prompts, evaluations and fine-tuning in ways a competitor starting a year later cannot buy.
When the feature is built only on public data and commodity models, there is no such loop, and waiting to learn may be the better choice.
W: A=Early feedback compounds into an advantage: when early user data and learning build on each other, the leader's edge grows over time and is hard for later entrants to close.
X: B=Only public data and commodity tools: anything built entirely from resources every competitor can use equally is easy to copy, so being first gives little lasting advantage.
C=No clear use, just press: launching without a real use case wastes investment and risks a poor product; publicity alone does not create durable value.
D=Feature soon to be banned: rushing to launch something regulators are about to prohibit creates compliance risk and sunk cost with no lasting return.
T: Move first when early data and feedback create a compounding flywheel; when the feature relies only on commodity inputs, followers can catch up easily.

G: ## Who owns AI-generated content?
When an agency hands AI-generated images or text to a client, the client assumes it can use them freely: on ads, packaging, websites. That assumption depends on several things:
- **The tool's terms of service**: they define who owns outputs and what uses are allowed. Terms differ between providers and plans, including whether commercial use is permitted.
- **The client contract**: it usually promises that deliverables are original, owned by the client or properly licensed.
- **Legal protection**: in some jurisdictions, content produced purely by AI with little human creative input may not qualify for copyright, which affects how exclusive the asset really is.
- **Third-party risk**: outputs that resemble existing brands, artworks or people can raise infringement or likeness concerns; some providers offer indemnity for certain outputs.
Example: a campaign image created with a tool whose free plan restricts commercial use could leave the client exposed. Clarifying rights up front, and writing them into the contract, avoids disputes later.
W: B=Ownership and usage rights: before delivery, the agency must confirm that the tool's terms and the client contract allow the client to own or license and commercially use the generated assets.
X: A=Vendor office location: where a vendor has offices does not decide who owns generated content or what uses are permitted; that comes from terms, contracts and law.
C=Images per minute: generation speed and plan throughput are operational capacity questions that affect workload planning, not the legal status of the delivered assets.
D=Designers' web browser: the browser used to view images has no effect on ownership, licensing or legal risk.
T: Before delivering AI-generated assets, confirm ownership and usage rights under the tool's terms and the client contract; operational details do not settle legal rights.

G: ## Outcome metrics versus activity metrics
A metric should match the question being asked. For **customer experience**, the question is whether customers were helped and left satisfied.
- **Outcome metrics** measure results for the customer: customer satisfaction (CSAT) after a conversation, resolution rate (was the issue solved without escalation), repeat contacts, net promoter score.

B=Average response length: longer or shorter answers are not better in themselves; a short answer that solves the problem beats a long one that does not.
D=Project meetings held: meeting counts measure team activity during the build, not any result customers experience.

G: ## The sunk cost fallacy
A **sunk cost** is money already spent that cannot be recovered, whatever you decide next. The **sunk cost fallacy** is continuing a project because of what was already invested rather than because of what it will deliver.
Rational investment decisions look only forward:
- What will it cost from here to finish or fix the project?
- What benefit is realistically expected, given results so far?
- Is there a better use for the same money and people?
Example: 2 million dollars is spent either way. If another 1 million dollars is expected to produce only 300,000 dollars of value, continuing loses more money. If a changed approach costing 500,000 dollars is expected to deliver 3 million dollars, continuing makes sense. The earlier 2 million dollars does not change either comparison.
Options include stopping, pivoting to a narrower use case, or continuing with a revised plan, chosen on future value.
W: B=Decide on future costs and benefits: past spending cannot be recovered, so only the expected future cost and value of each option should drive the decision to stop, pivot or continue.
X: A=Double the budget: more money does not fix a flawed approach, data problem or poor use-case fit; without a diagnosis it usually just enlarges the loss.
C=Continue at any cost: this is the sunk cost fallacy itself; the 2 million dollars is gone either way, and unlimited funding risks losing far more.
D=Blame the vendor and change nothing: public blame damages the relationship and reputation, and keeping the same plan guarantees the same poor results.
T: Ignore sunk costs; decide whether to continue an AI initiative based only on expected future costs and benefits.

G: ## What makes a good key result
In **OKRs**, the objective is the qualitative goal ("make claims handling faster and more accurate") and key results are the measurable proof that the goal was reached.
A well-formed key result is:
- **Specific**: names exactly what changes, such as claims processing time.
- **Measurable**: has a number, ideally with a baseline and a target (10 days to 6 days).
- **Time-bound**: has a deadline (by year end).
- **Outcome-focused**: measures a business result, not just activity such as "launch pilots" or "use AI in more places".
Example: "Reduce average claims processing time from 10 days to 6 days by year end" lets anyone check at year end whether it happened. "Become more innovative with AI" cannot be checked at all.
For AI programs this matters because it keeps the focus on business value rather than on how much AI is used.
W: D=Specific, measurable, time-bound outcome: a baseline, a target and a deadline on a business result make progress objectively checkable and tie AI work to value.
X: A=Most innovative company: this is an aspiration, closer to an objective; there is no number or measurement to show whether it was achieved.
B=Several exciting pilots with positive feedback: "several" and "exciting" are vague, and internal sentiment measures enthusiasm, not a business outcome.
C=AI in as many processes as possible: this counts AI usage rather than results, has no target, and can encourage using AI where it adds no value.
T: A good key result states a measurable business outcome with a baseline, target and deadline, not an aspiration or an activity count.

G: ## Does this problem even need AI?
AI, and machine learning in particular, is strong at finding patterns in large amounts of data that are hard to write down as rules. It is probabilistic: it is usually right, but not always.
Signals that AI is a good fit:
- **Complex patterns**: fraud detection, document classification, demand forecasting, where rules would be endless or brittle.

D=Once a year, ten minutes: the effort to build, validate and maintain an AI solution far exceeds the ten minutes a year it would save.

Fraud patterns often cross company lines: the same fraud ring may hit several banks or insurers. A **data consortium** pools anonymized or aggregated data from members so a model can learn from far more cases than any single member has.

G: ## Reading a probability score
Many classification models do not output a yes or no; they output a **probability** or score between 0 and 1. A churn model saying `0.82` for a customer means the model estimates a high chance that this customer will leave, based on patterns in past customers who looked similar.
How to use such a score:

- Human judgment still matters: the manager may know the customer just signed a new project.
Example: out of 100 accounts, the top 10 by score get personal outreach this week, while the rest get standard communication.
W: D=High likelihood, not certainty: 0.82 is the model's estimated probability of churn, so it should guide which accounts get attention first, combined with the account manager's judgment.

C=Training 82% complete: the score is an output of a trained model for one customer, not a measure of how far training has progressed.
T: A model probability ranks risk to guide action; it is neither a certainty nor a count, and it should support rather than replace human decisions.

G: ## Training data versus test data
A model can look excellent on the data it learned from simply because it has memorized it. That says little about how it will do on new cases. To get an honest estimate, teams split the data:
- **Training set**: the model learns from it, often 70-80% of the data.
- **Validation set**: used while tuning the model and choosing settings.
- **Test (holdout) set**: kept aside and used only at the end, to estimate performance on data the model has never seen.
Example: a fraud model scores 99% accuracy on training data but only 85% on the held-out test set. The gap signals **overfitting**, and 85% is the realistic expectation for production.
Holding out data therefore protects the quality of the evaluation. It is not about security, storage or speed.

X: B=Held-back data is encrypted: holding data out of training is an evaluation practice, not a security control; encryption is configured separately and applies to all data regardless of use.

G: ## What a token is
Language models do not read text as whole words or documents. A **tokenizer** splits text into **tokens**: small pieces such as a word, part of a word, a punctuation mark or a space.
- A common word like `cat` may be one token; a longer word like `unbelievable` may be split into several pieces.
- In English, a rough rule of thumb is that one token is about three-quarters of a word, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 700-800 words. The exact ratio depends on the model's tokenizer and the language.
Why managers care about tokens:
- **Limits**: the context window (how much input a model can consider) and the maximum output length are set in tokens. A 1,000-token output limit means a response of roughly 750 English words, not 1,000 documents.
- **Cost**: services such as Amazon Bedrock price on-demand text model usage by the number of input and output tokens.
- **Speed**: longer outputs take longer to generate.
W: A=Small unit of text: a token is a word or word piece that the model reads and writes, and both limits and pricing are measured in these units.
X: B=Prepaid payment voucher: tokens are units of text; billing counts tokens used, but a token is not a voucher you buy in advance.
C=Complete document: a token is much smaller than a document, often less than a word, so a 1,000-token limit caps output at roughly 750 English words.
D=Security credential: the word "token" is also used for access tokens in authentication, but in a model's output limit it refers to units of text, not credentials.
T: A token is a small chunk of text; model limits and costs are counted in tokens, not documents, vouchers or credentials.

G: ## Fresh data needs to be fetched, not remembered
A foundation model's knowledge comes from its training data, which stops at a cutoff date. It cannot know today's exchange rates, stock prices or inventory levels, and if asked, it may confidently produce a plausible but wrong number.
For data that changes often, the reliable pattern is to **retrieve it at question time**:
- The assistant recognizes it needs a rate.
- It requests a tool (often called function calling or tool use), and the application runs it to query a live rates service.
- The service returns the current value, and the model uses it in its answer.
On AWS, this can be built with tool use in the Amazon Bedrock Converse API, where the model returns a tool request that your code executes, or with action groups in Amazon Bedrock Agents, which are typically fulfilled by an AWS Lambda function.
Example: "How much is 500 euros in dollars?" triggers a call to `getExchangeRate(EUR, USD)`, which returns today's rate; the model does the conversion and cites the date.
W: B=Call a rates service at question time: fetching live values through a tool or API grounds the answer in current data instead of anything the model memorized.
X: A=Last year's rates in the prompt: exchange rates can move materially within a year, so answers based on stale values can be wrong.
C=Estimate from training data: training data stops at a cutoff date, so the model cannot know today's rates and may produce confident but incorrect numbers.
D=Fine-tune daily: fine-tuning is slow and costly, is not a reliable way to make a model recall exact numbers, and would still be out of date as rates change during the day.
T: For fast-changing facts, have the assistant fetch current data through a tool or API at question time instead of relying on the model's memory or retraining.

G: ## What "multimodal" means
A **modality** is a type of data: text, images, audio, video. A **text-only model** takes text in and produces text out. A **multimodal model** can take in, and reason across, more than one modality in the same request.
Examples of multimodal tasks:
- Upload a photo of a damaged car and ask "Which parts look damaged?"
- Give a chart image and ask for a summary of the trend.
- Provide a scanned invoice and ask the model to extract the total.
Some multimodal models also produce images or other media. In Amazon Bedrock, several model families accept both images and text as input.
Multimodality is about input and output types. It is separate from:
- **Language support**: many text-only models handle dozens of human languages.
- **Regional availability**: which AWS Regions offer a model depends on the service and model, not on whether it is multimodal.
- **Cost**: pricing depends on the model and usage, and image inputs can add cost.
W: D=Reasons over more than one input type: a multimodal model accepts and combines different kinds of data, such as an image plus a text question, in one task.
X: A=Several human languages: supporting multiple languages is a separate property; text-only models are often multilingual, so this does not distinguish them.
B=Several AWS Regions: Region availability is decided per service and model, and has nothing to do with how many data types a model accepts.
C=Always cheaper: multimodal models are not inherently cheaper; processing images or other media can cost more, and price depends on the model and the task.
T: Multimodal means handling more than one data type, like images and text together; it is not about languages, Regions or price.
