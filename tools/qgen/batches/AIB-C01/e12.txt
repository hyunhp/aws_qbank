G: ## Why models get worse over time
A machine learning model learns patterns from a fixed snapshot of data. It assumes the future will look like that snapshot. When the real world moves on, the patterns it learned stop matching what it sees. This is called **drift**:
- **Data drift**: the inputs change. Example: shoppers who mostly bought in stores in 2021 now buy mostly on mobile, so features like "store visits" look very different.
- **Concept drift**: the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes. Example: a price level that signaled a "premium" buyer in 2021 is normal after years of inflation.
A model trained on 2020 to 2022 data and used in 2026 has four or more years of drift. The typical symptom is exactly what the company sees: accuracy slides a little more each month rather than failing all at once.
## What to do about it
- Monitor live prediction quality and input distributions; on AWS, Amazon SageMaker Model Monitor can compare production data with a training baseline.
- Retrain on recent data on a schedule or when monitoring shows drift.
W: Outdated learned patterns: customer behavior and business conditions have changed since training, so the model is applying 2020-2022 relationships to 2026 data; monitoring and retraining on recent data fix this.
X: A=Fixed one percent monthly decay: there is no built-in decay rate; a model's weights do not change after deployment, and any decline depends entirely on how much the real-world data has shifted.
B=Expired software license: a license governs whether you may use the software, not the math inside it; the same model on the same inputs gives the same outputs regardless of paperwork.
D=Model too accurate for 2026: accuracy is measured against real outcomes, so "too accurate" is not a failure mode; the observed problem is falling accuracy, which calls for retraining, not deliberately degrading the model.
T: Models are snapshots of the past; when behavior and conditions drift, performance declines until you monitor and retrain on recent data.

G: ## How search by photo works
Visual similarity search lets a shopper upload a picture and get back products that look like it.
- A computer vision model turns each catalog image into an **embedding**: a list of numbers that captures visual features such as shape, color, pattern and texture.
- The shopper's uploaded photo is turned into an embedding the same way.
- A vector search finds the catalog embeddings closest to the photo's embedding, and those products are shown as matches.
Example: a shopper photographs a friend's green floral dress. The system returns catalog dresses with similar cut, color and print, even though the shopper typed no words at all.
On AWS, multimodal embedding models available in Amazon Bedrock can create image embeddings, and a vector store such as Amazon OpenSearch Service can run the similarity search.
The key point for a business leader: the input is an image and the output is visually similar items, so the capability needed is image understanding plus similarity matching.
W: Visual similarity search: it compares the visual features of the uploaded photo with catalog product images and returns the closest matches, which is exactly "search by photo."
X: A=Speech recognition: it converts spoken audio into text; the shopper here provides a photo, not a voice description, so there is nothing for speech recognition to process.
B=Sentiment analysis of reviews: it scores whether review text is positive or negative, which can rank popular items but cannot tell which products look like a specific uploaded image.
D=Sales forecasting: it predicts future demand from historical sales, which helps inventory planning but does not match a shopper's photo to products.
T: Search by image needs a vision model that compares visual features; text, sentiment or demand models work on different inputs and cannot find look-alike products.

G: ## Why a model gives different answers to the same question
A large language model picks each next word from a probability distribution. Two things make its answers vary:
- **Sampling randomness**: the `temperature` setting controls how adventurous the choice is. A high temperature (for example 0.9) often picks less likely words, which is good for brainstorming. A low temperature (for example 0 to 0.2) almost always picks the most likely word, so the same question tends to get the same answer.
- **What the model is working from**: if the model answers from its general training knowledge, it may recall a policy differently each time. With **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**, the application first retrieves the relevant policy text and places it in the prompt, so every answer is based on the same source wording.
Example: "How many days do I have to return an item?" With temperature 0.9 and no retrieval, one reply says 30 days and another says "about a month, depending on the item." With low temperature and the retrieved return-policy paragraph in the prompt, answers consistently quote 30 days.
In Amazon Bedrock, temperature is an inference parameter, and Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases provide managed retrieval.
W: A=Lower temperature: reducing temperature makes the model choose the most likely wording far more often, so repeated identical questions produce much more consistent answers.
E=Ground in retrieved policy text: supplying the same authoritative policy passage each time anchors the content of the answer to the real policy instead of the model's variable memory.
X: B=Longer maximum output: the length limit only caps how many tokens can be generated; it does not reduce randomness, and longer answers give more room for variation.
C=Remove the system prompt: the system prompt carries instructions on role, tone and format; removing it gives the model less guidance, which usually increases variation rather than reducing it.
D=Model with more image generation: the assistant answers text policy questions, so image-generation capability has no effect on whether its text answers are consistent.
T: Consistency comes from less sampling randomness and a shared source of truth; changing output length, removing instructions or adding image features does not fix it.

G: ## Three nested circles
The three terms describe fields inside each other, from broadest to narrowest:
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)**: the broad goal of making computers perform tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing images or making decisions. It includes rule-based systems as well as learning systems.
- **Machine learning (ML)**: a subset of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. Example: a spam filter that learns from millions of labeled emails rather than a fixed list of banned words.
- **Deep learning**: a subset of ML that uses neural networks with many layers. It excels at unstructured data such as images, audio and text. Example: image recognition, speech-to-text and the large language models behind generative AI.
A simple way to say it to an executive: all deep learning is machine learning, and all machine learning is AI, but not the other way around. A chess program using hand-coded rules is AI but not ML; a logistic regression churn model is ML but not deep learning.
W: Nested subsets: machine learning is one approach within AI, and deep learning is a family of machine learning methods built on multi-layer neural networks.
X: A=Deep learning broadest: this reverses the hierarchy; AI is the widest field, and it covers far more than image recognition, including language, planning and decision making.
B=Unrelated technologies: the terms are not just marketing overlap; they have a clear technical containment relationship, with each narrower field defined as a type of the broader one.
C=AI equals ML, deep means archives: AI also includes non-learning approaches such as rule systems, and "deep" refers to the many layers in a neural network, not to long-term data storage.
T: AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning; the depth in deep learning is about neural network layers, not storage.

G: ## AI as decision support in high-stakes work
In medicine, a missed or wrong diagnosis can seriously harm a patient, so the design question is not only "how accurate is the model?" but "who is responsible for the decision?"
A common responsible pattern is **decision support with human-in-the-loop**:
- The model reviews every scan and highlights regions that might be abnormal, often with a confidence score.
- A radiologist reviews the scan and the AI's flags, then makes and signs the final diagnosis.
- Cases where the radiologist disagrees with the AI are recorded and used to monitor and improve the model.
Example: the AI marks a small shadow on a chest X-ray that a tired reader might overlook late in a shift. The radiologist looks again, decides it is a real nodule and orders follow-up imaging. The AI improved attention; the expert kept accountability.
This pattern captures the benefits of AI (consistency, speed, a second pair of eyes) while keeping clinical judgment, patient context and legal responsibility with a qualified person.
W: Decision support with radiologist sign-off: the AI flags possible findings to focus expert attention, and the radiologist, who carries clinical and legal responsibility, makes the final diagnosis.
X: A=Show only normal scans: hiding the scans the AI flags as abnormal removes exactly the cases that need expert review and would delay diagnosis of real problems.
C=Fully automatic diagnosis: models make errors, can fail on unusual cases or new equipment, and cannot take accountability, so removing human review creates unacceptable patient risk.
D=Avoid AI entirely: every tool, and every human, makes some mistakes; well-validated assistive AI under expert oversight can improve detection, so a blanket ban gives up real patient benefit.
T: In high-stakes domains, AI should support expert judgment by flagging findings, not replace the person who is accountable for the decision.

G: ## Few-shot prompting versus training from scratch
Traditional ML classification needs a model trained on many labeled examples, often thousands per category. With only a handful, a model trained from scratch cannot learn reliable patterns.
A **foundation model** has already learned language from huge amounts of text. You can often get it to classify just by describing the task and showing a few examples in the prompt. This is **few-shot prompting**, and it needs no training job at all.
Example prompt for ticket routing:
- `Ticket: "I was charged twice this month." Category: Billing`
- `Ticket: "The app crashes when I upload a file." Category: Technical`
- `Ticket: "How do I add a new user to my account?" Category: Account`
- `Ticket: "(new ticket text)" Category:`
The model completes the pattern with the right category for the new ticket. On AWS, this can be done by calling a model in Amazon Bedrock. As more labeled tickets accumulate, the team can measure accuracy and later consider fine-tuning if needed.
W: Few-shot prompting a foundation model: the model already understands language, so a few labeled examples in the prompt are enough to demonstrate the categories and get useful classification immediately.
X: A=Customers classify their own tickets: customers do not know the internal categories, often choose wrong or generic options and gain extra work, so this shifts effort without delivering reliable automation.
B=Wait for one million labels: collecting that volume could take years and delays all value, while a foundation model can already perform the task with a few examples.
D=Train from scratch on a handful: a new deep learning model has no prior knowledge of language, so a few examples lead to severe overfitting and unusable accuracy.
T: When labeled data is scarce, show a few examples to a pre-trained foundation model instead of training a new model from almost nothing.

G: ## Quality and representativeness beat sheer volume
A model can only be as reliable as the data it learns from. Two properties matter most:
- **Clean data**: errors, duplicates and missing values teach the model wrong patterns. Example: if the same fraudulent transaction appears 50 times through a logging bug, the model overweights that one pattern. Missing income values stored as `0` look like very poor customers rather than unknown ones.
- **Representative data**: training examples should cover the situations the model will actually face. Example: a loan model trained only on customers from one region and one economic period may perform badly in other regions or after conditions change.
Typical practices include profiling the data before training, removing duplicates, deciding how to handle missing values, and checking that important groups, seasons and edge cases appear in the training set.
More data only helps when it is relevant and accurate; adding unrelated or skewed data often makes a model worse, not better.
W: B=Check for errors, duplicates and missing values: fixing data quality problems before training stops the model from learning false patterns caused by bad records.
E=Match training data to real use: when training data reflects the conditions and populations seen in production, the model's accuracy in testing carries over to real use.
X: A=Many unrelated datasets: data that does not relate to the prediction task adds noise and spurious correlations, so volume goes up while reliability goes down.
C=Only the last week of data: a single week misses seasonality, rare events and longer-term patterns, so the model sees a narrow and unrepresentative slice of reality.
D=Remove all rare cases: rare cases such as fraud or unusual failures are often the ones that matter most, and a model that never saw them will handle them badly in production.
T: Reliable models come from clean data that looks like the real world, not from piling on more data or trimming away the hard cases.

G: ## How historical bias becomes model bias
A model trained on past decisions learns to reproduce those decisions, including any unfairness in them. It does not know which patterns were fair and which were not.
Salary history is a well-known example. Past offers may reflect pay gaps by gender, race or age, or differences in how hard people negotiated. If the model learns "people like this were offered less," it will keep suggesting less, now with the apparent authority of an algorithm.
- Removing sensitive fields such as gender is not enough: related features (previous salary, gaps in employment, certain job titles or schools) can act as **proxies**.
- The effect can grow over time, because new offers based on the model become the next training data.
Responsible steps include auditing the historical data for pay gaps, testing suggestions for disparities across groups, basing pay on role and skills bands, keeping a human decision-maker and monitoring outcomes after launch.
W: Learning historical pay gaps: salary suggestions trained on past offers can copy and perpetuate unfair pay differences between groups, which is a fairness and legal risk for the company.
X: A=Preferred font: page styling is a usability preference that can be changed in minutes and has no effect on whether salary suggestions are fair.
B=Slow suggestions: generating a salary number from a trained model is fast, and even if it were slow, speed is an operational issue rather than a responsible AI concern.
C=Storage size: salary records are small structured data such as numbers and job codes, so storage is trivial and unrelated to the ethical risk of the tool.
T: A model trained on past human decisions inherits their biases, so pay tools must be checked for historical inequities before and after launch.

G: ## Every threshold trades one error for another
A moderation model gives each post a score for "likely harmful." The team chooses a **threshold**: posts above it are removed.
- A **low threshold** catches more harmful posts (fewer false negatives) but also removes more legitimate posts (more false positives).
- A **high threshold** leaves more legitimate posts up but lets more harmful content through.
No setting eliminates both kinds of error. Example: at threshold 0.5 the model removes 95% of harassment but also 3% of normal posts; at 0.8 it removes 80% of harassment and 0.5% of normal posts. Which is better depends on the platform: a children's service may accept more false positives; a news discussion forum may worry more about silencing legitimate speech.
Because this is a values and policy decision, leadership should own it, document the reasoning and review it with real data. An **appeal path** lets users contest wrongful removals, which corrects individual mistakes and gives feedback for tuning.
W: Deliberate balance with appeals: weighing the real harms of missed harmful posts against wrongful removals for this platform, then providing an appeal process, handles both error types responsibly.
X: B=Vendor picks the threshold: the vendor does not know this company's users, legal duties or values, and the company remains accountable for its own moderation policy.
C=Always maximize removal: wrongful removals silence legitimate users, damage trust and can have legal or commercial consequences, so they are real harms, not free.
D=Always minimize wrongful removals: letting harmful content through exposes users to harassment and abuse and harms the platform, so ignoring that error type is also irresponsible.
T: Tuning a classifier always trades false positives against false negatives, so leaders must choose the balance deliberately and give people a way to appeal mistakes.

G: ## Guardrails on both sides of the model
A public chatbot will meet users who try to make it say offensive things, sometimes to screenshot the result and share it with the company's logo attached. The most direct defense is **content filtering** applied at two points:
- **Input filtering**: checks the user's prompt for categories such as hate, insults, sexual content, violence or attempts to bypass instructions, and blocks it before the model responds.
- **Output filtering**: checks the model's reply before it is shown, catching harmful content that slips through even when the prompt looked harmless.
Example: a user writes a disguised request for a joke insulting a religious group. The input filter flags it; if a clever rewording gets past, the output filter still blocks the offensive reply and returns a polite refusal.
On AWS, Amazon Bedrock Guardrails provides configurable content filters, denied topics and word filters that apply to both prompts and responses. Filters should be tested and monitored, since no filter is perfect.
W: Input and output content filters: blocking harmful categories on both prompts and responses prevents offensive content from being generated or displayed under the company's brand.
X: B=Larger model: model size does not guarantee safe behavior; larger models can still produce offensive content when prompted, so they still need explicit safety controls.
C=Business hours only: restricting hours reduces overall usage but a determined user can misuse the chatbot during open hours just as easily.
D=Disclaimer: a disclaimer does not stop offensive content from being generated or shared, and a screenshot with the brand attached still damages reputation.
T: Stop harmful content with filters on what goes into and comes out of the model; size, hours and disclaimers do not prevent misuse.

G: ## Vendor concentration and continuity
When a critical customer process depends on one AI vendor, the company inherits that vendor's risks: outages, API changes, model retirement, price increases, policy changes or the vendor leaving the market.
Good business continuity planning treats the AI service like any other critical dependency:
- Identify what happens to customers if the service is down for an hour, a day or a week.
- Define a **fallback**: a manual process with trained staff, a simpler rules-based system, or an alternate model or provider behind the same interface.
- Document the process and the switch-over steps.
- **Test** the fallback periodically, because an untested plan often fails when it is needed.
Example: an insurer uses an AI service to triage claims. If the service is unavailable, claims route to a human queue with a documented checklist, and once a quarter the team runs a drill to confirm the switch works and staff know what to do.
W: Tested fallback plan: a manual process or alternate model, rehearsed periodically, keeps the critical customer process running if the vendor's AI service fails or degrades.
X: A=Assume constant availability: every service, including those of large providers, can have outages or changes, so planning as if they never happen leaves customers exposed.
B=Move everything to the same vendor: concentrating even more processes on one supplier increases the impact of any vendor problem instead of reducing it.
C=Avoid documentation: without documentation, the company cannot recover, switch providers or train staff quickly, and hiding the process from competitors does not reduce vendor risk.
T: Reduce single-vendor risk with a documented, regularly tested fallback, not by assuming reliability or deepening dependence.

G: ## What privacy by design means
**Privacy by design** means building data protection into a system from its first design decisions, rather than adding it after launch. For an AI project, typical practices include:
- **Data minimization**: collect and use only the data the use case needs. Example: a churn model may need usage and billing history but not customers' exact home addresses or birth dates.
- **Purpose limitation**: use data only for the purposes it was collected for and people were told about.
- **Access control and encryption**: only the right people and systems can reach personal data.
- **De-identification**: pseudonymize or remove identifiers when the model does not need them.
- **Retention limits**: delete data when it is no longer needed.
- **Privacy impact assessments** early in the project, when design changes are still cheap.
The idea is that privacy is a design requirement, like performance or cost, considered at every stage rather than a legal notice added at the end.

G: ## Why middle managers matter in AI adoption
Middle managers translate strategy into daily work. They assign tasks, set priorities and judge performance, so if they resist an AI tool their teams will too, even when executives support it.
Resistance often has understandable causes:
- The tool changes how their teams are measured, and managers fear their numbers will drop during the transition.
- They were not consulted and see workflow problems the designers missed.
- They worry about their own role or authority.
An effective response turns managers into co-owners of the change:
- **Involve them in workflow design**, so the new process fits real work and they understand it.
- **Define success measures** for their teams in the new way of working, so they are not penalized for adopting the tool.
- Give them training and early wins they can share.
Example: in a claims team, managers help decide which claims the AI triages and agree that team targets shift from "claims handled" to "resolution time and accuracy." They then champion the change to their staff.
W: Involve managers in design and success measures: giving managers ownership of the new workflows and clear, fair measures of success addresses their concerns and turns them into advocates for adoption.
X: A=Wait for voluntary requests: waiting for every manager to ask for the tool stalls the rollout indefinitely and does nothing to address the reasons for resistance.
B=Bypass managers: going around managers undermines their authority and usually increases resistance, since they still direct their teams' daily work.
D=Replace resisting managers: replacing people is disruptive and costly, loses experience and signals to others that concerns cannot be raised, making the change harder.
T: Win over middle managers by involving them in redesigning work and defining how success will be measured, not by bypassing or replacing them.
