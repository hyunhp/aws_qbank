G: ## What an AI center of excellence does
An AI center of excellence (CoE) is a small, cross-functional group of experts (data science, engineering, security, legal, business) that helps the whole company adopt AI well. It is an **enabler**, not a replacement for the business.
- It sets shared standards: approved models and services, security and responsible AI guidelines, evaluation practices.
- It builds reusable components such as prompt templates, data connectors, evaluation harnesses and reference architectures, so each team does not start from zero.
- It coaches and supports business teams, who stay accountable for their own use cases and results.
## A concrete example
A marketing team wants a content assistant. The CoE provides the approved foundation model access, a guardrail configuration and a review checklist; marketing defines the use case, owns the KPIs and runs the day-to-day process.

E=Shared standards and reusable components: common guardrails, patterns and building blocks raise quality and consistency and cut duplicated effort across projects.
X: B=Approving all hiring: company-wide hiring authority belongs to HR and line managers; a CoE may advise on AI skills, but controlling every hire is far outside its mandate.
C=Replacing finance budgeting immediately: a CoE does not take over another function's core process, and an abrupt replacement without a pilot or finance ownership would add risk rather than value.
D=Building every AI system centrally: doing all the work without business teams turns the CoE into a bottleneck and leaves solutions disconnected from the people who use them and own the results.
T: A CoE enables and standardizes (experts, guardrails, reusable assets) while business teams own their use cases; it is not a central team that builds everything itself.

G: ## Why pilot results often do not repeat at scale
A pilot usually runs under favorable conditions: a volunteer team that wants it to succeed, close attention from the project team, and a process chosen because it suits AI. Company-wide rollout removes many of those advantages.
- Other teams may be skeptical or busy, so they use the tool less or work around it.
- Their processes, data and edge cases can differ from the pilot team's.
- Support and training are spread thinner across many more people.
## What this means in practice
If the pilot team cut handling time by 30%, a less engaged team with a different workflow might see 10% or nothing. Plan the rollout with change management, training and per-team measurement against a baseline, and expect to adjust the process for each group.
W: Different engagement and processes: the pilot's success was partly driven by a motivated team and a well-suited process, so broader teams with other contexts may not reproduce the results without deliberate adoption support.
X: A=More users reduce accuracy: a model's accuracy does not drop simply because more people call it; capacity and latency are scaling concerns, but prediction quality depends on the data and task, not user count.
C=Scaling doubles accuracy: rolling a model out to more people does not change how accurate it is, so there is no automatic improvement, let alone a doubling.
D=Vendor refuses multi-team use: enterprise AI products and cloud services are built to serve many teams and users; licensing may change with scale, but a blanket refusal is not the realistic risk.
T: Pilots succeed partly because of motivated teams and suitable processes; the main scaling risk is that other teams and contexts will not repeat those results without change management.

G: ## Why uncoordinated customization raises cost
When every team configures the same assistant its own way, cost drivers multiply: some pick larger models than needed, some send very long prompts or whole documents on every call, some duplicate their own retrieval indexes and integrations. Spend then grows faster than the value delivered.
## Standardize and measure unit cost
- Offer shared, approved configurations (model tier, prompt templates, retrieval setup) that fit most needs.
- Reuse common components instead of building one per team.
- Track **unit cost**, such as cost per conversation or per completed task, by team using tags or cost allocation, so outliers are visible.
For example, if one team's cost per request is five times higher, the data points to its larger model or longer prompts, which can then be fixed.
W: Standardize and monitor cost per use: shared configurations remove wasteful variation, and per-team unit-cost tracking shows exactly where spending grows faster than usage so it can be corrected.
X: A=Stop tracking by team: total spend alone hides which configurations are inefficient; without per-team visibility the cause cannot be found or fixed.
B=Unlimited customization: free-form configuration is what created the problem; flexibility without guardrails tends to increase, not reduce, total cost.
C=Remove high-cost teams without analysis: the highest spenders may also get the most value, so cutting them blindly can destroy benefits while leaving the real inefficiency unexplained.
T: Control AI costs at scale by standardizing configurations and watching unit cost per team, not by ignoring team-level data or cutting usage blindly.

G: ## Sharing data safely for AI
AI projects need data from many teams, but some columns, such as salaries, health details or customer IDs, must stay restricted. The usual pattern is a **central data lake with fine-grained access control**.
- Data is stored once, in shared storage such as Amazon S3, and cataloged centrally.
- Permissions are granted per database, table, column or even row, based on the user's role.
- On AWS, `AWS Lake Formation` can grant column-level and row-level permissions on cataloged tables, so an analyst can query a customer table without seeing sensitive columns.
## Example
Marketing and data science both query the `customers` table. Data science is granted all columns; marketing is granted everything except `date_of_birth` and `national_id`. Everyone works from one governed copy, and access is auditable.
W: Centralized fine-grained access control: one governed data lake lets teams share data while column-level (and row-level) permissions keep sensitive fields visible only to authorized users.
X: A=Separate laptops: isolated copies on laptops block sharing, create inconsistent versions and are hard to secure or audit.
C=Full access for everyone: unrestricted access exposes sensitive columns to people who should not see them and breaks least-privilege and privacy obligations.
D=Emailing spreadsheet extracts: ad hoc extracts create uncontrolled copies, go stale quickly and leave no reliable way to limit or audit who sees sensitive data.
T: Share data through one governed data lake with fine-grained (column and row) permissions instead of copying data or opening it to everyone.

@ Q002377 explain
G: ## Context windows and long documents
A foundation model can only read a limited amount of text per request, called its **context window**, measured in tokens (as a rough approximation, one token is about three quarters of an English word; the exact ratio varies by model and tokenizer). A 400-page case file can exceed that limit.
## Hierarchical (map-reduce) summarization
A common approach is to split and combine:
- Split the document into sections or chunks that each fit in the window, such as chapters or 20-page parts.
- Summarize each section separately (the "map" step).
- Combine the section summaries and ask the model for a final summary of those (the "reduce" step), repeating if the combined summaries are still too long.
Every part of the file shapes the final result, because each section contributed its own summary. Including section references in each partial summary helps lawyers trace points back to the source.
W: Summarize sections, then combine: chunking lets the whole file be processed within the context limit, and the final summary is built from summaries of every section.
X: A=Guess from the title: the model would invent content it has never seen, producing hallucinated and legally unreliable summaries.
B=Send only the first pages: everything after the cut-off is ignored, so key facts later in the file cannot influence the summary.
C=Raise the temperature: temperature controls randomness in word choice; it has no effect on how many tokens fit in the context window.
T: When input exceeds the context window, chunk and summarize hierarchically; temperature does not change capacity and truncation loses content.

G: ## Training in plain business terms
Training is how a machine learning model learns from data rather than from hand-written rules.
- The model is shown many examples, such as past loan applications with their outcomes, or emails labeled "spam" or "not spam".
- It makes predictions, compares them with the known answers, and adjusts its internal parameters to reduce the errors.
- After many rounds, it has captured patterns it can apply to new cases it has not seen.
## A business analogy
It is similar to how a new employee becomes skilled by reviewing many past cases and getting feedback, except the model does this automatically and at large scale. The quality of the examples matters: biased or unrepresentative data teaches the wrong patterns.
Training is different from **inference**, which is using the trained model to make predictions, and from deployment, which is making it available in production.
W: Learning from many examples: training means exposing the model to data so it adjusts itself to capture the patterns that make it useful for the task.
X: A=Writing marketing messages: promoting a model's benefits is communication work and does not change what the model has learned.
C=Teaching employees the interface: this is user training, which prepares people to use the tool, not model training.
D=Installing and securing the model: this describes deployment and infrastructure setup, which happens after the model has been trained.
T: Training means the model learns patterns from many examples; it is not user onboarding, marketing or deployment.

G: ## Predictive AI versus generative AI
Many business problems combine two kinds of AI.
- **Predictive (traditional) machine learning** learns from historical, often numeric data to predict an outcome, such as whether a device will fail within 7 days based on temperature, vibration and error counts.
- **Generative AI** creates new content, such as text, from instructions and context. A foundation model can turn a failure prediction plus sensor details into a readable note like "Replace cooling fan on rack 4; temperature has risen 12% over 3 days".
## Why combine them
Each does what it is good at: the predictive model gives a reliable, measurable failure probability from structured sensor data, and the generative model turns that result into clear instructions for technicians. Human review of the notes is still wise for safety-critical repairs.
W: Predictive model plus generative model: a model trained on sensor readings forecasts failures, and a generative model drafts plain-language repair notes from that output.
X: A=Translation and speech models: sensor readings are not a language to translate, and a speech model converts audio to or from text rather than predicting failures or drafting notes.
B=One image classification model: sensor readings are time-series numbers, not images, and a classifier assigns labels; it does not write free-text repair notes.
D=Recommendation and sentiment models: recommenders rank items for users and sentiment models score the tone of existing text; neither predicts equipment failure or generates notes.
T: Use predictive ML for forecasting from structured sensor data and generative AI for writing text; pick each model type for the task it is built for.

G: ## What an AI agent needs
An AI agent uses a foundation model to plan steps and take actions toward a goal. To act on real business data, it needs:
- **Tools**: defined functions or APIs it can call, each with a clear description and input schema, such as `getInventory(sku)` or `getShippingRules(region)`. The model decides which tool to call; the application runs it and returns the result.
- **Guardrails and oversight**: clear instructions, limits on which actions it may take alone (for example, it can quote a date but not change an order), and logging of every step so its decisions can be audited and debugged.
## Example flow
Customer asks for a delivery date, the agent calls the inventory API (item in stock in Dallas), calls the shipping-rules API (2-day ground), then quotes a date. On AWS, Amazon Bedrock Agents supports this pattern with action groups that map to APIs.
W: A=Defined tools or APIs: the agent cannot know live inventory or shipping rules on its own; it must call systems of record to get accurate data.
E=Clear limits and logging: bounded permissions prevent unsafe autonomous actions, and logs provide traceability for audits and troubleshooting.
X: B=Removing all instructions: without instructions the agent has no defined task, scope or policy, which makes its behavior unpredictable and unsafe.
C=Larger image model: rendering product photos does not help look up inventory, check shipping rules or calculate a delivery date.
D=Higher temperature: more randomness makes outputs less consistent; delivery dates should be derived from data, not chosen creatively.
T: Agents need defined tools to reach real data plus limits and logging to act safely; more randomness or fewer instructions only makes them less reliable.

G: ## What makes an AI business case credible
Boards know that AI benefits are uncertain. They trust a business case that shows its reasoning more than one that shows the biggest number.

W: Explicit assumptions with ranges: transparent assumptions and low-to-high estimates let the board test the reasoning and show realistic awareness of uncertainty.

D=Promise of no risks: every project has risks; claiming none signals that they have not been analyzed, which reduces trust.
T: Credible business cases state assumptions and give ranges with risks, rather than inflated single numbers or claims of zero risk.

G: ## Managing the cut-over from manual to AI
Replacing a working process everywhere at once creates a single point of failure: if the AI underperforms, every region is affected and there is no fallback.

G: ## Launch blockers versus minor issues
A readiness assessment checks whether an AI system can go live safely and legally. Findings fall into two groups.

G: ## How a model threshold works
A fraud model outputs a score, such as 0 to 1, for each transaction. A **threshold** turns the score into a decision: above it, an alert is raised.
- **Lower threshold**: more alerts, more fraud caught (higher recall), but more false alarms.
- **Higher threshold**: fewer alerts and fewer false alarms (higher precision), but more fraud slips through.
## Choosing the setting
The right threshold depends on business costs and capacity:
- The cost of a missed fraud case versus the cost of reviewing a false alarm.
- How many alerts analysts can actually review, here 500 a day. Alerts beyond that sit unreviewed, so extra recall is not realized.
For example, the team might pick the threshold that produces about 500 alerts a day with the highest fraud value caught, and revisit it as capacity or fraud patterns change.
W: Balance fraud caught against capacity and error costs: the threshold should maximize value within what analysts can review, weighing missed fraud against false alarms.

D=Always highest threshold: fewer alerts reduce workload but let much more fraud through; fewer alerts is not automatically better.
T: Set a model's threshold by weighing the costs of false positives and false negatives against operational capacity, not at an extreme or at random.

Both classification and regression are **supervised learning**: the model learns from labeled historical examples. The difference is what it predicts.
- **Classification** predicts a category or label: approve or decline, fraud or not fraud, which of five product types. Output may include a probability for each class.
