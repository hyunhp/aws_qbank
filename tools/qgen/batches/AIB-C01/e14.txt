G: ## Correlation versus causation
A predictive model learns which inputs tend to appear together with an outcome. It finds **associations**, not the direction of cause and effect.
- If app users spend more, three stories fit the same data: the app makes people spend more, people who already spend a lot are the ones who install the app, or a third factor (such as loyalty or income) drives both.
- The model cannot tell these apart, because it only saw customers as they were, not what happened when someone was pushed to install the app.
## How to test a causal claim
Run an experiment: randomly offer a download incentive to one group of customers and not to a similar control group, then compare spending. If the treated group spends more, the intervention works; if not, the original pattern was just correlation.
W: Correlation, not causation: the model shows that app use and spending move together. High spenders may simply be the customers most likely to install the app, so pushing downloads to everyone may not raise spending. A controlled test is needed before investing in the campaign.
X: A=Never combine spending and behavior data: combining transaction data with behavior data is normal and useful for analytics and marketing. The problem is how the result is interpreted, not which data was joined.
B=App data cannot be used: mobile app usage data can be used in models, subject to the usual privacy and consent rules. Discarding the finding throws away a useful signal that only needs correct interpretation.
C=Models always prove causation: this is the exact mistake the question warns against. Predictive models capture patterns in historical data and do not show what happens when you change behavior.
T: A model that links app use to spending shows correlation; only a controlled test shows whether pushing downloads actually raises spending.

**Forecasting** predicts a future numeric value over time, such as units sold per product per store next month. It learns from time-stamped history and from known drivers of demand.
- **Historical sales** by store and product show trends and seasonality (for example, higher sales in December).

Amazon SageMaker Canvas offers no-code time-series forecasting, and Amazon SageMaker AI supports custom forecasting models. The key input in every case is clean, granular sales history.

G: ## Why test scores can mislead
An evaluation set is only useful if it looks like real usage. If the test questions are neat and written by the team, while customers use slang, typos, long stories or different terms, high test scores do not predict production quality. This is called a **distribution mismatch** between test and real inputs.
## How to diagnose it
- Take a representative sample of real customer questions (with privacy controls applied).
- Have reviewers rate the assistant's answers on that sample.
- Compare the error types with the test set: for example, "fails when customers use product nicknames" or "misses the question when it is buried in a long message".
The findings show what to fix (prompts, retrieval data, examples) and give a better test set for the future.
W: Evaluate on real customer questions and compare: measuring the assistant on actual production-style inputs reveals exactly where it fails and why the original test set missed it.
X: A=Switch off logging: hiding poor answers removes the evidence needed to find and fix the problem, and the customer experience stays just as bad.
B=Rerun the original test set: the same test set does not contain the differently phrased questions, so repeating it (or tuning to it) can only hide the gap.
D=Blame customers and add a banner: customers ask in their own words, and a warning banner does not improve any answer. The assistant must handle real phrasing.
T: When an assistant passes tests but fails in production, evaluate it on real inputs to find the mismatch instead of re-running the old tests.

@ Q002402 explain
G: ## Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
A model's **context window** limits how much text it can read per request, and every token sent costs money and time. A 3,000-page manual is far too large to send with each question.
**RAG** solves this in two steps:
- **Index once**: split the manual into chunks, turn each into an embedding (a numeric vector of its meaning) and store them in a vector index.
- **Retrieve per question**: find the few chunks most similar to the question and pass only those to the model, which answers from them.
Example: "What torque for the rear axle bolt?" retrieves the two pages on the rear axle, not the whole manual.
**Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases** provides this workflow as a managed feature and can return citations to the source sections.
W: Index for retrieval: each question gets only the relevant sections of the manual, which keeps prompts small and cheap, scales to thousands of pages and grounds answers in the actual text.
X: A=Answer from memory: the manual may be private or newer than the model's training data, and even if seen, the model does not reliably recall exact details. This leads to made-up answers.
C=One-paragraph summary: compressing 3,000 pages into a paragraph drops almost all specifics, so detailed technical questions cannot be answered.
D=Paste the whole manual every time: the manual likely exceeds the context window, and even if it fit, sending it with every question would be slow and very expensive.
T: For large reference documents, retrieve the relevant sections per question (RAG) instead of relying on memory, a summary or the full text.

G: ## Bias and variance
Two common ways a model can fail on new data:
- **High bias (underfitting)**: the model is too simple and misses real patterns, so it performs poorly on both training and new data.
- **High variance (overfitting)**: the model fits the training data too closely, including noise, so it scores very well in training but inconsistently on new cases. Small changes in the training data can produce noticeably different models.
Example: a churn model is 98% accurate on last year's customers but 70% on this quarter's, and retraining on a slightly different sample changes its predictions a lot.
## What it means for the business
Results in production may be unreliable. Remedies include more training data, simpler models, regularization and validation on held-out data.
W: Sensitive to training data, inconsistent on new cases: high variance means the model has learned details specific to its training set, so its performance on new real-world cases can be unstable.
X: A=Many languages: supporting many languages is a feature of some models, not a statistical property called variance.
B=Regional licensing: licensing terms affect where a model can be used, not how its predictions generalize. Variance is about learning behavior, not contracts.
D=Varying monthly cost: cost changes with usage are a billing matter. "High variance" in data science refers to prediction stability, not spending.
T: High variance means overfitting: great on training data, unreliable on new cases.

G: ## Controlling output format with prompts
Models follow instructions and examples well. The quickest way to get a consistent structure is to put it in the prompt:
- **Template**: "Answer using exactly these headings: Summary, Risks, Next steps."
- **Example (few-shot)**: show one complete answer in that structure so the model copies the pattern.
- Put these instructions in the **system prompt** so they apply to every conversation.

Fine-tuning changes model weights with training data and is used when prompts cannot achieve the behavior. For a simple three-part layout, prompting usually works, costs nothing extra and can be changed in minutes.

D=Fine-tune or train from scratch: training a model just to follow a layout is slow and costly when a prompt template achieves the same result.

G: ## Why AI pilots stall
"Pilot purgatory" happens when many experiments start but none reach production. Common causes:
- No **business owner** who needs the result and will change the process.
- No agreed **success metric**, so no one can decide whether to scale.
- No **path to production**: data access, security review, integration and budget were never planned.
## What works
Pick a small number of use cases with high value and feasibility. For each one, name an owner, define measurable targets (for example, "cut claim handling time by 20%") and plan the production requirements from the start. Stop experiments that do not meet their targets and redirect effort to those that do.

C=Stop and wait for the technology: current AI is already delivering value in production elsewhere. Waiting forfeits learning and lets competitors move ahead.
T: To escape pilot purgatory, focus on fewer use cases with clear owners, metrics and a planned path to production.

G: ## Valuing distributed time savings
Small savings spread across many people are real but easy to dismiss. A defensible estimate uses the company's own measurements:
- **Sample** a set of tasks and time them before and after the AI tool (for example, 6 minutes before and 2 minutes after).
- **Multiply** the saved time per task by the **task volume** (for example, 50,000 entries per year gives about 3,300 hours).
- **Value** the hours at the **loaded labor cost** (salary plus benefits and overhead), for example 3,300 hours at $45 is about $150,000.
State the assumptions and note whether saved time is redeployed to other work or reduces overtime, since that affects how the value is realized.
W: Sample before-and-after task times, scale by volume and loaded cost: this uses measured data from the company itself, shows the calculation openly and can be checked and repeated by finance.
X: B=Report no value: small, spread-out savings can be measured by sampling; reporting zero understates the benefit and misinforms investment decisions.
C=Assume one full day per week for everyone: an arbitrary, very large assumption applied to the whole headcount has no evidence behind it and would not survive review.
D=Use the vendor's marketing estimate: vendor averages come from other environments and have an interest in looking high. They are not specific to this company's tasks and volumes.
T: Estimate distributed savings by measuring task time on a sample and scaling by volume and loaded labor cost.

G: ## Hidden costs in AI business cases
Business cases often count only the obvious costs: licenses, model usage and the initial build. Two large categories are frequently missed:
- **Change management and training**: redesigning processes, training users, communicating the change and supporting people while they adapt. Without it, adoption stays low and the benefits never appear.
- **Ongoing operations**: monitoring quality and drift, updating prompts and data, moving to new model versions when old ones are retired, handling incidents and keeping security and compliance controls current.
Example: a tool with a $200,000 build may need a similar amount every year for operations and continued training. Leaving these out makes the projected return look much better than it will be.
W: C=Change management and training: people must learn and adopt new ways of working, and this effort is often large and left out, which then limits real benefits.
E=Ongoing monitoring, maintenance and updates: AI systems need continuous monitoring and updates after launch, and these recurring costs are commonly omitted from business cases.
X: A=Naming and logo design: branding an internal initiative costs very little compared with the project and does not affect the business case meaningfully.
B=Printing the business case: printing a document is a trivial expense and not a significant cost category.
D=Kickoff catering: a one-time meeting expense is negligible relative to build, operations and change costs.
T: AI business cases most often underestimate people change costs and ongoing operations after launch.

G: ## Time to value in buy-versus-build decisions
When a need is **urgent** and **not a core differentiator**, the value of having a solution now usually outweighs the missing features.
- A bought solution that meets 80% of needs starts delivering benefits immediately.
- The remaining 20% can often be covered by adjusting processes, configuring the product, or adding small extensions through its APIs.
- Building a 100% solution means a year of no benefit, plus development risk and ongoing maintenance for a capability that does not set the company apart.
Example: if the solution saves $50,000 per month, waiting 12 months forgoes about $600,000, typically far more than the value of the last 20% of features.
Revisit later if the need becomes strategic.
W: Buy the 80% solution now and adapt: for an urgent, non-core need, fast time to value matters most, and processes or light extensions can close most of the gap.
X: A=Wait for a perfect vendor product: waiting forfeits all benefits in the meantime, and a product meeting every requirement may never appear.
C=Buy and also build in parallel: funding two solutions for the same non-core need duplicates cost and effort, with the build adding little over what was bought.
D=Build the 100% solution: a 12-month delay has real cost for an urgent need, and custom builds for non-core capabilities add maintenance without competitive gain.
T: For urgent, non-core needs, buy a good-enough solution now rather than build a perfect one later.

G: ## Balancing efficiency and growth
AI initiatives usually fall into two groups:
- **Efficiency**: lower cost or effort, such as automating document processing or shortening support calls. Benefits are often faster and easier to measure.
- **Growth**: new revenue, such as personalized offers, new AI-powered product features or better conversion. Benefits can be larger but less certain.
## A balanced portfolio
A strategy states explicit priorities (for example, 60% of the budget to efficiency this year, 40% to growth), sets targets for each initiative and reviews them regularly. Efficiency savings can help fund growth bets, and both groups are measured against the same agreed goals, so executives and the product team work toward one plan.
W: Balanced portfolio with explicit priorities and targets: including both cost and revenue initiatives, with agreed weights and measurable targets, aligns both groups and captures both kinds of value.
X: A=Let each team pursue its own goals: without shared priorities, effort and budget fragment, initiatives compete for the same resources and no one can compare their value.
B=Only cost reduction: AI can and does create revenue through new products and better customer experiences, so excluding growth leaves value on the table.
D=Only revenue growth: AI cost savings are measurable, for example reduced handling time or lower processing costs, so excluding efficiency ignores reliable returns.
T: An AI strategy can pursue both efficiency and growth when a single portfolio sets explicit priorities and targets.
