G: ## Why adoption fades after rollout
Usage of a new AI tool often spikes at launch and then drops. The drop is a symptom, and the cause differs by team:
- **Poor fit**: the tool does not match how the team actually works, so it adds steps instead of removing them.
- **Quality**: answers are wrong or generic often enough that people stop trusting it.
- **Friction**: slow responses, extra logins, or no integration with the tools people already use.
- **Skills**: people never learned how to prompt or apply it to their own tasks.
## Diagnose before you act
Leaders should look at usage data by team and then talk to users directly, through interviews, short surveys or feedback channels. For example, if a finance team says the assistant cannot read their spreadsheets, the fix is an integration, not a new vendor or a mandate. Each fix targets a specific cause, so usage can recover where the tool actually helps.
W: Ask users and fix the causes: the reasons for the drop are unknown, and only the people who stopped using the tool can say whether it is fit, quality, friction or skills. Targeted fixes address the real problem and keep the investment already made.
X: A=Replace with the newest product: switching tools without knowing why usage fell may repeat the same problem, since fit, training and integration issues follow any product, and it throws away the adoption work already done.
B=Mandate daily usage: forced usage produces activity numbers, not value. People log in to comply while the underlying problem remains, and it damages trust in the program.
D=Shut it down: lower usage in several teams does not prove the tool has no value; other teams may still benefit, and the causes in the declining teams may be easy to fix.
T: When adoption declines, first find out why from the users, then fix those causes; replacing, mandating or shutting down without a diagnosis treats the symptom.

G: ## What "culture ready for AI" means
AI projects are uncertain: early prompts, models and workflows often fail before they work. Technology readiness (data, platforms, skills) is only part of the picture; culture decides whether people keep going and use the results.
- **Experimentation**: teams can try small pilots, see some fail, and share what they learned without blame. This matters because most AI use cases need several iterations.
- **Evidence-based decisions**: leaders judge ideas and results with data, such as baseline metrics and pilot outcomes, rather than instinct or rank alone. AI produces predictions and measurements; a culture that ignores evidence will ignore them too.
## Example
A company where a failed chatbot pilot ends careers will see few future pilots. A company that reviews the pilot's metrics, keeps what worked and tries again builds momentum.
W: D=Comfort with experimentation: AI progress depends on trying, measuring and iterating, so teams must feel safe running pilots that may fail and learning from them.
E=Data-driven leadership: leaders who decide on evidence will act on model outputs and pilot metrics, which is how AI results turn into business decisions.
X: A=AI mascot: a mascot is branding for a program; it says nothing about how people behave when experiments fail or how decisions are made.
B=Latest office software version: software versions describe IT hygiene, not attitudes, habits or decision-making, which is what culture readiness measures.
C=Personal parking spaces: parking is a facilities perk and has no connection to experimentation, learning or evidence-based decisions.
T: Culture readiness for AI is about behavior, meaning safe experimentation and decisions based on evidence, not branding, tools or perks.

G: ## Data foundations that let AI scale
The first AI use case often succeeds with a one-off data effort. Scaling to many use cases requires data that any approved team can find, trust and reuse.
- **Data catalog**: a searchable inventory describing each dataset: owner, meaning of fields, freshness and access rules. On AWS, `AWS Glue Data Catalog` and `Amazon SageMaker Catalog` (built on Amazon DataZone, which remains available as its own service) play this role.
- **Built-in quality checks**: automated rules in the pipelines (for example, no missing customer IDs, values within expected ranges) that flag or stop bad data before models use it. `AWS Glue Data Quality` is one example.
## Why these two
With a catalog, a new team building a churn model finds the trusted customer table in minutes instead of weeks. With quality checks, that team can trust the table without re-validating it. Together they cut the time and risk of every new use case.
W: A=Data catalog: makes trusted datasets discoverable and understandable, so new use cases reuse existing data instead of hunting for it or rebuilding it.
E=Quality checks in pipelines: catch data problems automatically and consistently, so every model built on the data starts from a reliable foundation.
X: B=Data on individual laptops: data on laptops cannot be shared or governed, is easily lost, and creates security and privacy risks, the opposite of scaling.
C=Emailed manual exports: exports are slow, stale the moment they are sent, not reproducible, and bypass access controls, so each use case depends on someone doing manual work.
D=Separate copies per team: duplicated datasets drift apart, give conflicting numbers and multiply storage and governance effort, so no one knows which version is trusted.
T: AI scales on shared, discoverable and quality-checked data; laptops, email exports and private copies fragment data and slow every new use case.

G: ## Business ownership versus IT ownership
AI creates value only when it changes a business process: how claims are handled, how sales leads are prioritized, how customers are served. The people who own those processes are business leaders.
- **IT or a central AI team** provides platforms, security, data access and technical expertise.
- **Business leaders** choose the problems worth solving, change their teams' workflows, and are accountable for the outcome metrics, such as handling time or revenue.
## Example
If IT builds a forecasting model alone, the planning team may never change how it orders stock, and the model sits unused. If the head of planning co-owns the initiative with a target such as "cut stock-outs by 15%", the team has a reason to adopt it.
W: Business co-ownership with accountability: making business leaders co-owners and accountable for results ties AI to real business outcomes and gives them a reason to drive adoption, while IT keeps providing the platform and expertise.
X: A=Stop reporting results to business units: hiding results reduces awareness and engagement and reinforces the view that AI is an IT activity unrelated to business goals.
B=Rename the program: a new name changes perception at best; accountability, priorities and who drives adoption stay the same.
D=Give IT sole control: this deepens the exact problem, since business units would have even less say in and responsibility for AI outcomes.
T: AI programs succeed when business leaders co-own and answer for outcomes; IT enables, but a program owned only by IT stays "IT's project".

G: ## Rollout is the start, not the finish
An AI system's value can erode after launch: data and user needs change, models drift, new features and model versions appear, and small problems go unreported. Sustaining value is an ongoing operating job, not a project that ends.
- **Measure continuously**: track the outcome metrics defined in the business case (for example, handling time, accuracy, user satisfaction) against targets and review them on a regular cadence, such as monthly.
- **Fund an owning team**: a team with budget to monitor quality, update prompts, data and models, fix issues and support users.
## Example
A document-summary assistant is accurate at launch. Six months later new document formats appear and accuracy drops. A regular review spots the dip, and the owning team updates the extraction step. Without both, the decline goes unnoticed and users quietly give up.
W: B=Ongoing outcome measurement: regular reviews against targets show whether value is holding and trigger action early when it is not.
E=Funded maintenance and support team: someone must own updates, fixes and user support; without budget and ownership, identified problems never get fixed.
X: A=Removing the feedback channel: user feedback is the fastest signal of quality problems and new needs; removing it hides issues until adoption falls.
C=Disbanding the project team at rollout: leaves no one to fix defects, update models or support users just when real-world use begins.
D=Stopping monitoring after one month: models and data drift over time, so one good month says nothing about the next; monitoring must continue.
T: AI value lasts only with ongoing measurement and a funded owning team; removing feedback, people or monitoring lets value erode unnoticed.

G: ## "Good enough" data for a use case
No company has perfectly clean data, and waiting for it means never starting. What matters is whether the data is good enough for a specific use case.
- Check the data the use case needs: is it available, reasonably complete and accurate enough for the decision being made?
- Start there with a pilot, measure results, and note which data gaps actually hurt performance.
- Invest in data quality where the proven use case shows it pays off, then expand.
## Example
A retailer wants demand forecasting. Sales history for its top 500 products is clean; long-tail items are messy. It starts with the top products, shows forecast gains, and uses that result to fund cleanup of the rest. Data work is then prioritized by real value, not guessed.
W: Start where data is good enough and improve it: this balances progress with quality, produces early value, and focuses data investment on the gaps that proven use cases reveal.
X: A=Replace internal data with purchased data: third-party data may not reflect the company's own customers or operations, costs money regardless of relevance, and cannot replace the internal records most use cases depend on.
C=Wait for perfectly clean data: data is never perfect and keeps changing, so this delays value indefinitely without telling the company which data problems actually matter.
D=Ignore data quality: models learn from and reflect the data they are given; poor data produces poor or biased outputs, and no model fixes that automatically.
T: Begin with data that is good enough for one use case and improve quality as value is proven; neither waiting for perfection nor ignoring quality works.

G: ## Detecting versus predicting
A model that flags a failure once it has started is a **detection** model: it learned what "failing" looks like. To warn earlier, the problem must be framed as **prediction over a time horizon**: "will this machine fail within the next 48 hours?"
- **Labels**: for each past failure, mark the readings from a window before it began (for example, 48 to 6 hours prior) as "failure coming".
- **Features**: use sensor trends from that earlier window, such as rising vibration or temperature drift, that precede the failure.
- **Lead time**: chosen so operations have enough time to act, such as scheduling maintenance.
## Example
If bearing temperature starts creeping up two days before a breakdown, a model trained on those earlier readings can raise an alert while there is still time to replace the bearing.
W: Predict a set time ahead using pre-failure data: training on signals from before past failures, labeled with a future horizon, teaches the model the early warning patterns operations need.
X: A=Use less sensor data: faster inference does not change what the model learned to recognize; it still detects failures in progress and may lose useful early signals.
B=Change alert colors: better visibility helps operators notice alerts sooner, but the alert itself still arrives after the failure has begun.
C=More people reading late alerts: more readers do not move the alert earlier; the model still only fires once the failure has already started.
T: Earlier warnings require reframing the model to forecast a future window from pre-failure signals; faster processing or better alert display does not change timing.

G: ## A voice question-answering pipeline
Spoken Q&A is a chain of three capabilities:
- **Speech-to-text**: converts the visitor's spoken question into text. On AWS, `Amazon Transcribe` does this.
- **Grounded question answering**: a generative model answers using the museum's own exhibit information, retrieved from a knowledge source so answers stay accurate rather than invented. On AWS, `Amazon Bedrock` with `Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases` fits this step.

## Example
A visitor asks, "When was this vase made?" Transcribe produces the text, the answering step retrieves the vase's catalog entry and writes a short answer, and Polly speaks it through the audio guide.
W: Speech-to-text, grounded Q&A and text-to-speech: each part covers one step of the interaction: hearing the question, answering it from exhibit information, and speaking the answer.
X: A=Fraud detection, document extraction and label codes: fraud detection and document extraction solve different problems, and none of these understands speech or speaks answers.
B=Image generation, sentiment and demand forecasting: these create images, score opinions and predict visitor numbers; none turns spoken questions into spoken answers.
D=Recommendation systems alone: recommenders rank items based on behavior; they do not transcribe speech, answer free-form questions or produce audio.
T: Spoken Q&A needs speech recognition, a grounded answering model and speech synthesis in sequence; other AI capabilities do not cover the voice interaction.

G: ## When prompting stops being enough
Prompt engineering is the first tool to shape a model's output: instructions, style guides and a few examples. For a subtle, consistent style across thousands of outputs, prompts can still produce drift.
**Fine-tuning** trains a base model further on labeled examples, here pairs such as "product facts" and "approved description". The model learns the brand voice itself, so it applies it consistently without long instructions each time.
- Amazon Bedrock supports fine-tuning for a selection of its foundation models (not every model); the result is a private custom model that you invoke through Amazon Bedrock.
- You need a good set of high-quality approved examples (often hundreds or more) and an evaluation step to confirm the tuned model matches the voice.
## Example
A retailer collects 1,000 descriptions its brand team approved, fine-tunes a model on them, and compares new outputs against the style guide before rolling out.
W: Fine-tune on approved brand-voice examples: when prompts cannot achieve consistent style, training on approved examples teaches the model the voice directly, giving consistent results at scale.
X: B=Switch to a speech model: speech models convert text to audio; the problem is how the text is written, and reading inconsistent text aloud does not fix it.
C=Remove brand guidelines from the prompt: less guidance makes output drift further from the brand voice, not closer.
D=Raise the temperature: higher temperature increases randomness and variety, which makes style less consistent, the opposite of the goal.
T: When prompts cannot make style consistent, fine-tune on approved examples; more randomness or less guidance only widens the variation.

@ Q002457 explain
G: ## Two kinds of knowledge, two retrieval methods
An enterprise assistant often needs both:
- **Structured data** (prices, stock levels, order status) lives in databases. The reliable way to get it is to **query** the database, for example through a tool or API call the assistant makes, which returns exact, current values.
- **Unstructured documents** (return policies, warranty terms) are best handled with **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: split documents into passages, index them, and fetch the passages relevant to each question.
The model then combines both results into one grounded answer. On AWS, `Amazon Bedrock Agents` can invoke action groups, for example an AWS Lambda function that queries a database, and `Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases` can retrieve relevant document passages.
## Example
"Can I return the X200 headset I bought 40 days ago?" The assistant queries the product database for the headset's category, retrieves the return-policy passage for that category, and answers using both.
W: Query the database and retrieve policy passages, then combine: each source is accessed with the method that suits it, so answers are grounded in exact facts and the relevant policy text.
X: B=Use only the database: policy questions would go unanswered or be guessed, because the rules live in the documents, not the tables.
C=Rely on the model's training data: the model never saw this company's current database or policies, so it would produce outdated or invented answers.
D=Put everything in every prompt: a full database and all policies exceed practical context limits, raise cost and latency on every request, and bury the relevant facts.
T: Ground answers by querying structured data and retrieving relevant document passages; do not rely on model memory or stuff everything into the prompt.

G: ## Why models get worse over time: drift
A model learns patterns from the data it was trained on. If the world changes, those patterns stop matching reality, and accuracy falls even though the model itself did not change.
- **Concept drift**: the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes. Example: a fraud model trained before a new scam technique appeared misses it.
- **Population shift (data drift)**: the people or cases the model serves change, so input data looks different from the training data. Example: a credit model trained on existing customers now scores applicants from a new region with different profiles.
## How to manage it
Monitor input data distributions and prediction quality in production, compare them with the training baseline, and retrain when they diverge. On AWS, `Amazon SageMaker Model Monitor` can detect data and model quality drift.
W: C=Changed real-world patterns: if the relationships the model learned no longer hold, its predictions degrade; this is the core cause of performance decline.
E=Shifted population served: a model applied to a different mix of users or cases sees inputs unlike its training data, so its accuracy can drop.
X: A=Office furniture changes: facilities changes have no effect on the data the model receives or the patterns it learned.
B=Renamed report folder: where reports are stored affects file organization, not the quality of the model's predictions.
D=New website color scheme: a visual redesign of the homepage does not change the data or relationships the model depends on.
T: Model performance decays when data patterns or the served population drift away from training data; monitor for drift and retrain when it appears.

G: ## Solve the cause, not only the symptom
AI is a tool, not the goal. Before choosing an AI solution, look at why the problem exists.
- **Symptom**: long customer wait times.
- **Cause**: a confusing checkout page generates most of the support calls.
A chatbot answers those calls faster, but the calls keep coming, and the company pays to automate avoidable demand. Fixing the checkout page removes the demand itself, often at lower cost and with a better customer experience.
## A simple way to compare
For each option, estimate the value (how many calls avoided or shortened), the cost and the time to deliver. Removing the root cause often wins; AI can then handle the remaining questions that truly need answers.
W: Fix the root cause first: removing the confusing page eliminates most of the calls, which can deliver more value than answering them faster, and leaves a smaller, more meaningful workload for any later chatbot.
X: A=Do both without assessment: spending on both without understanding the cause wastes effort and may build a chatbot sized for demand that disappears once the page is fixed.
C=Always choose the chatbot: choosing AI because it looks modern ignores value; the chatbot leaves the root cause in place and customers still hit the confusing page.
D=Do neither: technology changes clearly can reduce wait times here, since fixing the page removes most of the calls that cause the queue.
T: Assess root causes before choosing AI; removing the source of demand is often simpler and more valuable than automating responses to it.

A vision like "augment every employee" sets direction but cannot be executed as is. Making it actionable means turning it into specific, measurable priorities.

G: ## Measuring employee experience value
To judge whether an AI tool improves the employee experience, measure outcomes the employee feels, not activity the system produces.
- **Outcome metrics**: time to resolve an HR question (for example, from 2 days by ticket to 2 minutes by assistant), resolution without escalation, and employee satisfaction ratings after each answer.

## Example
After launch, an HR assistant resolves 70% of questions in under a minute, and satisfaction scores rise from 3.4 to 4.2 out of 5. Those numbers show real experience value; a count of stored policies does not.
W: Resolution time and satisfaction: these measure what employees actually experience, namely getting correct answers faster and feeling well served, which is the value the assistant was meant to deliver.
X: A=Answer length in words: longer answers are not better and may be harder to use; length says nothing about accuracy, speed or satisfaction.
C=Mentions of the assistant's name in email: mentions could be complaints or jokes; they measure visibility, not whether questions were resolved.
D=Number of policies in the knowledge base: this is an input measure of content loaded, not whether employees get correct, fast answers from it.
T: Measure employee experience with outcome metrics such as resolution time and satisfaction, not with activity counts like answer length or content volume.

G: ## Where durable AI advantage comes from
A new AI feature is often easy to copy: competitors can use similar foundation models and build a similar interface within months. What is hard to copy is:
- **Proprietary data**: the company's own customer interactions, transactions and domain data, which competitors cannot access.
- **Feedback loops**: usage produces feedback (ratings, corrections, outcomes) that improves the feature, which attracts more usage, which produces more feedback.
- **Speed of iteration**: shipping improvements faster than rivals can catch up.
## Example
Two retailers launch AI product recommendations. One retrains weekly on its purchase and return data and customer ratings; after a year its recommendations are measurably better, even though the competitor copied the original feature quickly.
W: Keep improving with proprietary data and feedback: a copied starting point does not include the leader's data or the improvements from continuous learning, so the gap keeps widening.
X: B=Hide the feature from customers: a hidden feature delivers no value to customers or the business, so there is no lead left to protect.
C=Cut prices across the catalog: broad price cuts reduce margins without stopping a competitor from building the feature, and they do not improve the feature itself.
D=Patent the idea and stop investing: the general idea of applying AI to a feature is hard to protect, and a feature that stops improving is soon matched or surpassed by others.
T: Sustain an AI lead through continuous improvement driven by proprietary data and customer feedback, not by hiding, discounting or freezing the feature.

G: ## Making AI value reports credible
Executives fund what they trust. A credible report lets them compare results fairly over time and see the full picture.
- **Fixed baseline and targets**: agree before launch how things were measured before AI (for example, average handling time of 8 minutes) and what success means (6 minutes). Report against those same numbers every period.
- **Full accounting**: include costs such as model usage, licenses, team time and change management, plus negative side effects such as errors or extra review work, not just benefits.
## Why this builds trust
A report that shows a 20% time saving, the cost to achieve it, and a known issue being fixed is believable. A report showing only gains, on metrics that change each quarter, invites doubt and can lead to poor investment decisions.
W: D=Report against pre-agreed baseline and targets: fixing the measures before launch prevents moving the goalposts and makes progress comparable over time.
E=Include costs and side effects: showing costs and downsides alongside benefits lets executives judge net value honestly and trust the numbers.
X: A=Change metrics each quarter: selecting whatever improved hides failures and makes results impossible to compare between periods.
B=Substitute projections for results: replacing actual measurements with forecasts when results disappoint misrepresents performance and destroys trust once discovered.
C=Report only the best week: cherry-picking the peak overstates typical performance and hides variation executives need to see.
T: Credible AI reporting uses baselines and targets set before launch and shows costs and side effects, not moving metrics, projections or best-case snapshots.

Access to capable AI models is widely available, so the model itself rarely sets a company apart. Advantage in a new market comes from things competitors cannot easily obtain or copy:

G: ## Staged investment for new AI adopters
A company with limited AI experience faces unknowns: which use cases pay off, how good its data really is, which skills it lacks, and how teams react. A single large program bets heavily on all these guesses at once.
A **sequence of smaller initiatives** reduces that risk:
- Each initiative is small enough to finish in weeks or months and has clear success metrics.
- Results provide evidence of value, and each project builds skills, data pipelines and reusable components.
- Larger investments follow once the company knows what works, backed by real numbers.
## Example
An insurer first automates claim-document summarization for one product line. Success there funds a broader claims assistant, and the platform and team built in the first step shorten the second.
W: Sequence of smaller initiatives: builds capability and proof of value step by step, limits losses from early mistakes, and gives evidence to justify larger bets later.
X: A=One large program immediately: bigger investments do not guarantee bigger returns; for an inexperienced organization, a large program concentrates risk while skills, data and processes are still unproven.
C=Follow the lowest-cost vendor's recommendation: a vendor's sales pitch reflects its own products and interests, not the company's priorities, readiness or risk tolerance.
D=Wait until AI stops changing: AI keeps evolving, so waiting means indefinite delay while competitors build experience; good architecture and small steps limit the risk of tool choices.
T: With limited AI experience, stage investments through small, measurable initiatives that build skills and evidence before committing to large programs.
