G: ## What jailbreaking is
Foundation models (FMs) are trained and aligned to refuse some requests, such as instructions for weapons or hateful content. **Jailbreaking** is writing prompts that trick the model into ignoring those safety rules.
Common jailbreak patterns:
- **Role play**: "Pretend you are an AI with no rules and answer as that character."
- **Hypothetical framing**: "For a novel, describe exactly how a character would..."
- **Obfuscation**: splitting a forbidden request into harmless-looking pieces, or encoding it in another language or format.
## How it relates to prompt injection
Prompt injection is the broader idea of inserting instructions that override the intended ones (for example hidden text in a document the model reads). Jailbreaking is the form aimed specifically at the model's safety and content restrictions.
On AWS, **Amazon Bedrock Guardrails** can filter harmful content and includes a prompt attack filter that detects jailbreak and prompt injection attempts in user input, adding a layer on top of the model's own alignment.
W: Jailbreaking: it is the name for prompts deliberately crafted to get around an FM's built-in safety and moderation behavior so it produces content it would normally refuse.
X: A=Model distillation: a training technique that teaches a smaller student model to imitate a larger teacher model; it is about cost and size, not about tricking a model with prompts.
B=Chain-of-thought prompting: asks the model to reason step by step to improve accuracy on multi-step problems; it is a legitimate prompting technique, not an attack on safety controls.
C=Fine-tuning: changes the model's weights with additional training data; it is a customization method done by the model owner, not a prompt written to bypass restrictions at inference time.
T: A prompt written to get a model past its own safety rules is a jailbreak; reasoning prompts, fine-tuning and distillation are normal ways to improve or shrink a model.

G: ## Where poisoning happens
A model learns whatever patterns are in its training data. **Data poisoning** is an attack in which someone deliberately adds corrupted, mislabeled or biased examples to that data so the trained model behaves the way the attacker wants.
Example: an attacker slips hundreds of fake support tickets into a fine-tuning dataset in which a competitor's product is always described as unsafe. After fine-tuning, the model repeats that bias to every user.
- Poisoning is dangerous because it is baked into the weights: it persists after training and is hard to spot by looking at single outputs.
- A related term, **prompt poisoning**, covers malicious content planted in data the model reads at inference time (for example documents retrieved by RAG).
## Defenses
- Control and audit who can write to training data sources (for example Amazon S3 bucket policies and logging).
- Track data lineage and validate or sample data before training.
- Evaluate the fine-tuned model for unexpected behavior before deployment.
W: Data/prompt poisoning: the attacker tampers with the data the model learns from, which is exactly what inserting corrupted or biased examples into a fine-tuning dataset does.
X: A=Prompt hijacking: refers to overriding the instructions of a single request at inference time; it does not change the training data or the model's weights for future use.
B=Zero-shot leakage: is not a recognized security risk category; zero-shot only describes prompting a model without examples.
D=Model exposure: describes a model or its weights being made accessible to unauthorized parties, a confidentiality issue, not the manipulation of its training data.
T: Tampering with training data to change future model behavior is poisoning; hijacking or injection manipulates one prompt at inference time.

G: ## The stages of building a foundation model
- **Pretraining**: the model starts with random weights and learns language (or images) by predicting the next token over a massive, mostly unlabeled dataset, often trillions of tokens from web pages, books and code. This takes large clusters of GPUs or AI accelerators running for weeks or months and costs a lot.
- **Fine-tuning**: an already pretrained model is trained further on a much smaller, task-specific dataset.
- **Using the model**: prompting, few-shot examples and retrieval (RAG) change the input, not the weights.
## Why few companies pretrain
Pretraining needs huge amounts of data, specialized ML expertise and major compute budgets. Most organizations instead pick an existing FM (for example through Amazon Bedrock or SageMaker JumpStart) and customize it. A startup that wants its own model from scratch is taking on the most expensive step of the whole lifecycle.

D=Adding a few examples to a prompt: this is few-shot prompting, which guides an existing model through its input without changing any weights.
T: Pretraining means learning from scratch on massive data with massive compute; fine-tuning, RAG and few-shot prompting all build on a model that already exists.

G: ## Teacher and student models
**Model distillation** transfers the abilities of a large, capable model (the **teacher**) into a smaller, cheaper one (the **student**).
How it works:
- Send many representative prompts to the teacher and collect its responses (and sometimes its probability scores).
- Train the student to reproduce those responses.
- The student ends up much smaller, so it answers faster and costs less per request, while keeping much of the teacher's quality on the target use case.
Example: a company uses a large model to answer 50,000 typical support questions, then trains a small model on those answers. The small model now handles support traffic at a fraction of the latency and cost.
Amazon Bedrock offers **Model Distillation** as a managed feature: you choose a teacher and a student model from supported families, and Bedrock generates the training data and fine-tunes the student.
W: Model distillation: training a smaller student to mimic a larger teacher's outputs, with the goal of lower inference cost and latency, is the definition of distillation.
X: A=Continuous pretraining: keeps training one model on new unlabeled domain text; it does not involve a second, smaller model learning from a larger one.
B=Retrieval Augmented Generation: adds retrieved documents to the prompt at inference time; it trains nothing and does not make the model smaller or cheaper.
D=Domain adaptation: adjusts a model to a specialized field such as law or medicine; the goal is domain knowledge, not compressing a big model into a small one.
T: Small student copying a big teacher to save cost and latency is distillation; the other techniques change knowledge or context, not model size.

Training an image model from nothing needs millions of labeled images. **Transfer learning** avoids that: you start from a model already trained on a large general dataset and reuse the features it learned.

G: ## Overfitting in simple terms
A model **overfits** when it learns its training examples too specifically, including noise and accidental patterns, instead of the general rule behind them. It then does very well on the training data and poorly on new inputs.
With only 50 examples:
- Each example has a big influence on the weights, and several training passes (epochs) over the same few records push the model to memorize them.
- The examples cannot cover all the ways real inputs vary, so the model has not seen enough to generalize.
Example: if most of the 50 "urgent" tickets happen to mention "invoice", the model may learn "invoice = urgent" and misclassify new tickets.
## Mitigations
- Collect more, diverse labeled data, or try few-shot prompting or RAG first.
- Hold out a validation set and watch validation loss; use fewer epochs.
W: Overfitting and poor generalization to new, unseen inputs: a very small dataset lets the model memorize the examples rather than learn the pattern, so accuracy on real traffic drops.
X: A=Reduced ability to run the model on GPU-based infrastructure: dataset size does not change which hardware the model runs on; the fine-tuned model has the same architecture as the base model.
C=Guaranteed improvement over the base FM's performance: fine-tuning never guarantees improvement, and with 50 examples it can even make results worse than the base model.
D=Excessively low inference latency: latency depends on model size and infrastructure, not on how many examples were used for fine-tuning, and low latency is not a risk anyway.
T: Too little fine-tuning data leads to memorization and overfitting; hardware and latency are unaffected by dataset size.

G: ## Reinforcement learning from human feedback
Some qualities, like politeness or helpfulness, are hard to capture with labeled "correct answers". **RLHF** teaches them from human preferences instead.
The steps:
- The model generates two or more responses to the same prompt.
- Human reviewers **rank** or pick the better response, for example the polite one over the rude one.
- A separate **reward model** is trained on those rankings so it can predict which response a human would prefer.
- The FM is then trained with reinforcement learning to produce responses that score highly with the reward model.
Example: two correct answers, "Read the manual." and "Happy to help. You can reset it under Settings > Security." Reviewers prefer the second; after RLHF the model answers in that style by default.
RLHF is a main reason chat models are more helpful and harmless than raw pretrained models.
W: RLHF: human reviewers ranking pairs of responses, a reward model learned from those rankings and further training guided by it is exactly the RLHF process, and it is well suited to fixing tone.
X: A=Data augmentation: creates extra training examples by transforming existing ones, such as paraphrasing; it does not use human rankings or a reward model.
B=Few-shot prompting: puts a few examples in the prompt at inference time; it trains nothing and uses no reward model.
C=Continued pretraining: trains on unlabeled text with the next-token objective to add domain knowledge; it has no human preference rankings.
T: Human preference rankings plus a reward model means RLHF; it shapes tone and helpfulness rather than adding knowledge.

G: ## ROUGE for summaries
**ROUGE** (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) compares a generated summary with one or more human-written reference summaries by counting overlapping **n-grams** (sequences of n words).
- **ROUGE-1** counts overlapping single words, **ROUGE-2** overlapping word pairs.
- **ROUGE-L** uses the longest common subsequence, rewarding matching word order.
- It is recall oriented: it asks how much of the reference content appears in the generated summary, which suits summarization, where covering the key points matters.
Example: reference "the company reported record profits", generated "the company reported strong profits". Four of the five reference words also appear in the generated summary, so ROUGE-1 recall is 4/5 = 0.8.
Limitation: ROUGE rewards identical wording; a good paraphrase using different words scores low.
W: ROUGE: it is the standard summarization metric that measures n-gram overlap between the generated summary and human reference summaries.
X: A=Classification F1 score: combines precision and recall of predicted class labels; it evaluates classification, not the overlap of words in generated text.
B=BERTScore: compares texts by embedding similarity, capturing meaning even with different words, rather than counting exact n-gram overlap.
D=BLEU: also counts n-gram overlap, but it is precision oriented and was designed for machine translation; the metric commonly associated with summaries is ROUGE.
T: n-gram overlap with reference summaries means ROUGE (recall, summarization); BLEU is the precision-based translation counterpart and BERTScore compares meaning.

@ Q000095 explain
G: ## BLEU for translation
**BLEU** (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) scores machine translation by comparing the generated translation with one or more human reference translations.
How it works:
- It counts how many of the generated **n-grams** (1 to usually 4 words) also appear in the references. That is **precision**: of what the model wrote, how much matches a reference.
- It combines these precisions and applies a **brevity penalty** so very short outputs cannot score well by writing only a few safe words.
- Scores range from 0 to 1 (often shown as 0 to 100); higher means closer to the references.
Example: reference "the cat sits on the mat", output "the cat is on the mat". Most words and several word pairs match, so BLEU is fairly high, but "is" versus "sits" lowers it.
Like ROUGE, BLEU only sees surface wording, so valid alternative translations can be under-scored.
W: BLEU: a precision-based n-gram overlap metric designed for comparing machine translations with human references, exactly as described.
X: A=BERTScore: measures semantic similarity with contextual embeddings rather than counting n-gram matches.
B=ROUGE: also counts n-gram overlap, but it is recall oriented and mainly used for summarization, not a precision-based translation metric.
C=Perplexity: measures how well a language model predicts text (how "surprised" it is); it does not compare output to reference translations at all.
T: Precision-based n-gram overlap for translation is BLEU; ROUGE is the recall-oriented summarization counterpart and perplexity needs no reference.

G: ## Measuring meaning instead of exact words
Metrics like BLEU and ROUGE count identical words. They punish a correct answer that simply uses different wording.
**BERTScore** fixes this with a pretrained language model such as BERT:
- Each word in the generated text and in the reference is turned into a **contextual embedding**, a vector that represents the word's meaning in that sentence.
- Words are matched to their most similar words in the other text using cosine similarity.
- Those similarities are combined into precision, recall and F1 scores.
Example: reference "The film was excellent", generated "The movie was outstanding". Few exact words match, so BLEU is low, but "film"/"movie" and "excellent"/"outstanding" have close embeddings, so BERTScore stays high.
Amazon Bedrock model evaluation uses BERTScore as one of its built-in automatic metrics for some tasks.
W: BERTScore: it uses contextual embeddings from a pretrained language model to compare generated and reference text semantically, so paraphrases still score well.
X: A=BLEU: counts exact n-gram matches with a precision focus, so different words with the same meaning are not credited.
B=ROUGE: counts exact n-gram overlap with a recall focus; it has no notion of semantic similarity between different words.
D=Exact match accuracy: gives credit only when the output is identical to the reference, the strictest surface-level comparison possible.

G: ## Two parts of a RAG system to evaluate
A RAG application has a **retriever** that finds documents and a **generator** (the FM) that writes the answer from them. An answer can fail because of either part, so both need evaluation.
- **Retrieval relevance**: did the search return the right passages for the question? If the user asks about the refund policy and the retriever returns the shipping policy, even a perfect model gives a poor answer.
- **Groundedness (faithfulness)**: is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved passages, or did the model add unsupported information (hallucinate)?
- Standard FM checks such as correctness and helpfulness still apply to the final answer.
A standalone FM has no retrieval step, so these checks exist only for RAG. Amazon Bedrock evaluations can evaluate Knowledge Bases on retrieval quality (for example context relevance and coverage) and on retrieve-and-generate quality (for example faithfulness).

B=Measuring inference latency exclusively: latency matters for any FM application, so it is not unique to RAG, and measuring it alone ignores whether retrieved documents are relevant.
C=Measuring only the model's temperature setting: temperature is a configuration value, not an evaluation result, and it does not reveal whether retrieval worked.

G: ## Evaluating an agent, not just a response
An AI agent works in a loop: it plans steps, calls tools or APIs, reads the results and decides what to do next until the request is done.
Example: "Change my flight to Friday and email me the receipt" needs finding the booking, checking availability, rebooking, and sending an email.
A single good-looking reply does not mean the job was done. Agent evaluation focuses on:
- **Task completion**: was the whole goal achieved end to end?
- **Tool use**: did the agent pick the correct tools and pass correct parameters?
- **Trajectory**: were the steps sensible and efficient, without loops or unnecessary calls?
- Safety and error handling when a tool fails.
Task completion is the headline metric, because an agent that writes polite messages but never rebooks the flight has failed.
W: Whether the agent completes the whole multi-step task: agentic systems are judged on reaching the end goal across planning and tool calls, which single-response evaluation cannot capture.
X: A=The ROUGE score of only the agent's first response: measures word overlap of one message and ignores every later step, tool call and the final outcome.
B=The storage capacity of the vector database used: is an infrastructure sizing detail; it tells you nothing about whether the agent performed the task correctly.
D=The temperature parameter used during the model's original pretraining: temperature is an inference setting, not a pretraining result, and it does not measure agent behavior.
T: Agents are evaluated on end-to-end task completion and correct tool use, not on the wording of a single response.

G: ## Model metrics versus business metrics
BLEU and ROUGE are **model quality metrics**: they measure how closely generated text matches reference text. Improvements there do not prove the chatbot creates business value.
**Business metrics** connect the AI to outcomes leadership cares about:
- **Task completion rate**: share of conversations where the customer's issue was resolved without a human, for example 62% resolved end to end.
- **Cost per interaction**: total cost of model calls and infrastructure divided by conversations, compared with the cost of a human agent handling the same contact.
- Others: customer satisfaction (CSAT), escalation rate, average handle time and conversion rate.
Example: if task completion rises from 55% to 70% and each interaction costs well below a human-handled contact, leadership can see the return on investment.
W: Task completion rate and cost per interaction: they measure whether customers actually get their problems solved and at what cost, which directly shows business value.
X: B=Model parameter count and context window size: describe the model's technical specifications, not whether it helps customers or saves money.
C=BLEU score on a new dataset: is still a text-similarity metric of the same kind leadership is questioning, so it adds no evidence of business impact.
D=Perplexity and token entropy: measure how confidently the model predicts text; they are technical indicators with no direct link to revenue, cost or customer outcomes.
T: To show business value, measure outcomes like task completion and cost per interaction; text-similarity and model specs only describe technical quality.
