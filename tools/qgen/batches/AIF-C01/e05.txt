@ Q000101 explain
G: ## What BLEU and ROUGE actually measure
BLEU and ROUGE are automatic metrics that count overlapping n-grams (short word sequences) between a model's output and a reference answer.
- **BLEU** is precision-oriented: how many of the generated n-grams appear in the reference. It came from machine translation.
- **ROUGE** is recall-oriented: how much of the reference is covered by the output. It came from summarization.
## Why overlap is not the same as usefulness
Suppose the reference answer is "Reset your password from the account settings page." A reply of "You can reset your password from the settings page of your account, but first contact billing" scores high overlap, yet the extra step may be wrong and unhelpful. N-gram metrics do not check factual correctness, whether the customer's real question was answered, tone, or whether the reply is actionable.
- Good practice combines automatic metrics with **human evaluation** (for example rating helpfulness and correctness) and, on AWS, model evaluation jobs in Amazon Bedrock that support human reviewers or an LLM-as-a-judge.
W: N-gram metrics miss real usefulness: high word overlap with reference answers can coexist with replies that are incorrect, incomplete or unhelpful to real customers, so human review of helpfulness and correctness is needed alongside BLEU and ROUGE.
X: A=Inverse correlation with accuracy: higher BLEU or ROUGE means more overlap with a reference, which generally points toward, not away from, the reference answer. The problem is that overlap is an incomplete signal, not that it runs backward.
B=Temperature necessarily zero: nothing in the scenario forces temperature to zero, and a deterministic temperature would not by itself explain unhappy users when test metrics were strong.
C=Unencrypted knowledge base: encryption at rest protects data from unauthorized access; it has no effect on answer quality or on how customers rate the chatbot's replies.
T: BLEU and ROUGE measure word overlap with references, not helpfulness or correctness, so pair them with human evaluation before trusting them.

G: ## Robustness: stable behavior on messy inputs
Robustness is part of the AWS "veracity and robustness" dimension. A robust model keeps giving reliable output when inputs change in small, realistic ways: typos, different phrasing, extra whitespace, casing, or slightly noisy data.
## How the weakness shows up
- "How do I reset my password?" gets a correct answer, but "how do i rest my pasword" or "I forgot my login secret, what now?" gets a wrong or nonsensical one.
- The model has learned surface patterns of clean training examples rather than the underlying intent.
## How teams improve it
- Test with perturbed inputs (typos, paraphrases, adversarial prompts) as part of evaluation; SageMaker Clarify and Bedrock model evaluation include robustness-style tests for foundation models.
- Add varied, noisy examples to training or fine-tuning data, and monitor production inputs for drift.
W: Robustness: failing badly on typos and slight rephrasing while succeeding on clean inputs is the textbook sign of a model that is not robust to input variation.
X: A=Explainability: explainability is about understanding why a model produced an output; an explainable model can still break on misspelled inputs.
B=Governance: governance covers policies, oversight, approvals and accountability for AI systems; it is an organizational process, not a property of how the model reacts to noisy text.
C=Transparency: transparency means disclosing how the system works, its data and limits; being transparent does not make the model tolerate typos.
T: Good results only on clean inputs point to missing robustness; transparency and explainability are about understanding the model, not its stability.

G: ## Where model bias comes from
A model learns patterns from its training data. If the data over-represents some groups or encodes past unfair decisions, the model reproduces them.
- Example: if historical hires were mostly from one university and one gender, a resume screener trained on them learns to prefer those traits.

- **Vetted sources**: data provenance is known and checked for quality, labeling errors and historical bias.
- **Measured, not assumed**: tools such as Amazon SageMaker Clarify can compute pre-training bias metrics (for example class imbalance between groups) before a model is trained.
Removing a sensitive column is not enough, because other fields (zip code, school, hobbies) can act as proxies for it.

B=Drop demographic fields with no review: other attributes such as location, school or gaps in employment can still act as proxies, and without review the bias is simply hidden rather than removed.
C=Largest dataset regardless of source: volume does not fix skew; a huge unvetted dataset can be even more unrepresentative or carry more historical bias than a smaller curated one.
T: Fairness starts with representative, vetted data; dropping sensitive columns or adding volume alone does not remove bias.

G: ## Why aggregate accuracy can hide problems
Overall accuracy averages across every test example. If one subgroup is small, its errors barely move the average.
- Example: 10,000 test applicants, 9,500 in the majority group at 97% accuracy and 500 in a minority group at 60% accuracy. Overall accuracy is still about 95%.
## Subgroup (disaggregated) analysis
Subgroup analysis computes the same metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, false positive and false negative rates) separately for each segment, such as age band or demographic group, and compares them.
- Large gaps signal that the model does not work equally well for everyone, often because that group was under-represented in training.
- Amazon SageMaker Clarify supports this with post-training bias metrics that compare outcomes and error rates across facets (the groups you choose to compare).
Finding the gap before deployment lets the team collect more data, rebalance or reconsider the model.
W: Subgroup analysis of metrics per demographic segment: computing accuracy for each segment would have revealed the 60% result that the 95% overall number concealed.
X: A=Only overall accuracy: this is exactly the measurement that hid the problem, because a small subgroup's errors hardly affect the aggregate.
B=Higher learning rate: the learning rate controls optimization step size during training; changing it does not measure or reveal performance differences between groups.
D=Fewer training epochs: reducing epochs changes how long the model trains and may cause underfitting, but it is not a diagnostic that shows per-group accuracy.
T: Aggregate metrics hide subgroup failures; evaluate metrics per segment before deploying.

G: ## Overfitting in one picture
A model **overfits** when it learns the training data too closely, including noise and quirks, instead of general patterns.
- Symptom: very high training accuracy (98%) but much lower accuracy on new data (70%).
- The opposite, **underfitting**, means the model is too simple: accuracy is poor on training and new data alike.
## Why small groups suffer most
When a group has only a few training examples, the model can effectively memorize those specific records rather than learn what generalizes for that group. On new members of the group, predictions are worst.
- This turns a generalization problem into a fairness problem: the under-represented group gets the least reliable decisions.
- Remedies include more and more representative data, regularization, early stopping, cross-validation and per-subgroup evaluation.
W: Overfitting, worsened by under-represented groups: the large gap between training and new-data accuracy shows overfitting, and the group with few examples is where memorization hurts generalization the most.
X: A=Perfect generalization: a drop from 98% to 70% on unseen data is the opposite of good generalization.
C=Underfitting across all groups: an underfit model scores poorly on the training data too, not 98%.
D=Too much training data: more data usually reduces overfitting, and the scenario explicitly says the worst group had too few examples.
T: High training accuracy with low accuracy on new data means overfitting, and sparse subgroups are hit hardest.

G: ## Guardrail policy types in Amazon Bedrock
- **Denied topics**: you describe a subject in natural language (a name, a definition and optional sample phrases), and the guardrail detects prompts or responses about that subject, even when phrased indirectly.
- **Content filters**: detect harmful categories such as hate, violence, sexual content, insults and misconduct, plus prompt attacks.
- **Word filters**: block exact words or phrases, including profanity or a custom list.
- **Sensitive information filters**: detect and block or mask PII.
- **Contextual grounding checks**: flag responses that are not supported by the provided source or not relevant to the query.
## Why a topic definition beats a word list
"Should I move my savings into index funds or bonds?" contains no forbidden keyword like "invest", but it is still a request for investment advice. A denied topic defined as "personalized recommendations about buying or selling financial products" catches it because it is evaluated on meaning.
W: Denied topics with a definition and examples: a denied topic is matched on the meaning of the subject, so the assistant refuses investment advice even when users avoid obvious keywords.
X: A=Contextual grounding checks: these check that a response is grounded in the supplied source and relevant to the query; they do not stop the assistant from discussing a forbidden subject.
B=Word filters for investment terms: exact-match word lists miss indirect phrasing that avoids the listed terms, and they can over-block harmless uses of those words.
D=Violence content filters: the violence category targets violent content; investment advice is not violent, so this filter would not trigger.
T: Use denied topics to block a subject by meaning; word filters only match specific words.

G: ## Bias and variance as error types
- **High bias**: the model is too simple or has learned a skewed pattern, so its errors are **systematic**. It is wrong in the same direction again and again.
- **High variance**: the model is overly sensitive to its training data, so its predictions are **erratic**. Small differences in input can swing the output widely.
## Why both matter for responsible AI
- Model A consistently underpredicts approvals for one group regardless of qualifications. That systematic error means one group is predictably disadvantaged: a fairness harm that is directional.
- Model B gives wildly different results for similar applicants. That inconsistency undermines reliability and fair treatment for everyone, since two equally qualified people may get opposite outcomes.
Both are responsible AI concerns, but they show up differently and are fixed differently (for example richer features or rebalanced data for bias, regularization or more data for variance).
W: Bias disadvantages a group systematically; variance is erratic: high bias produces a consistent, predictable skew against one group, while high variance produces unreliable, inconsistent decisions for similar applicants.
X: A=Not relevant to responsible AI: both conditions directly affect whether people are treated fairly and reliably, so they are core responsible AI concerns, not purely academic statistics.
B=Identical fairness effects: one produces a directional, systematic disadvantage for a group and the other produces random inconsistency; the effects differ.
C=Variance always discriminatory, bias never: this reverses the scenario; the systematic disadvantage to a group comes from high bias, and high variance is not automatically discriminatory.
T: High bias means systematic, predictable harm to a group; high variance means inconsistent, unreliable decisions.

G: ## What Amazon Bedrock Guardrails provide
Guardrails is a set of configurable safeguards applied to the prompts and responses of generative AI applications. One guardrail can combine several policies:
- **Denied topics**: refuse subjects such as investment advice, defined in natural language.
- **Content filters**: detect and block harmful categories such as hate, insults, sexual content, violence and misconduct, plus prompt attacks.
- **Sensitive information filters**: detect PII types or custom regex patterns (for example an account number format) and either block them or mask them in the output.
- **Contextual grounding checks**: score whether a response is grounded in the supplied source and relevant to the query, and block ungrounded ones.
- **Word filters**, and automated reasoning checks for some use cases.
Guardrails can be attached to model invocations, agents and knowledge bases, or called separately with the `ApplyGuardrail` API.
W: Amazon Bedrock Guardrails: denied topics, content filters, sensitive information masking and contextual grounding checks cover all four requirements in one place.
X: A=CloudWatch dashboards: dashboards visualize metrics about the application after the fact; they cannot refuse topics, block hate speech or mask data in a response.
B=Bedrock fine-tuning: fine-tuning shifts a model's style or knowledge but does not provide enforceable, configurable rules for blocking topics or masking account numbers.
D=Bedrock Agents action groups: action groups let an agent call APIs or Lambda functions to complete tasks; they are for taking actions, not enforcing content policies.
T: Policy controls on generative AI input and output, such as topics, harmful content, PII masking and grounding, belong in Bedrock Guardrails.

G: ## Shared responsibility for managed ML
The AWS shared responsibility model splits security duties:
- **AWS: security OF the cloud**: physical data centers, hardware, the global network, and the virtualization layer (hypervisor). For a managed service such as Amazon SageMaker AI, AWS also operates the underlying service infrastructure.
- **Customer: security IN the cloud**: who can access what, how data is protected, and how services are configured.
## Customer tasks for a custom model on SageMaker AI
- Grant least-privilege access with IAM roles and policies for users, notebooks and training jobs.
- Encrypt training data and model artifacts (for example S3 with AWS KMS keys) and enable encryption for training volumes and traffic where needed.
- Configure networking such as running jobs in a VPC, and classify and govern the data used.
Example: an S3 bucket of training data left public is a customer failure, not an AWS one.
W: IAM permissions and data encryption: controlling access to the model and data and choosing how data is encrypted are customer responsibilities under shared responsibility.
X: A=Patching the hypervisor: the virtualization layer is AWS infrastructure that customers never access, so AWS patches it.
B=Maintaining global network hardware: AWS owns and maintains the physical network across its Regions and edge locations.
D=Physical security of data centers: AWS controls data center access, guards and facilities; customers have no physical access.
T: AWS secures the infrastructure; the customer secures access, data and configuration of what they build on it.

G: ## Amazon Macie in brief
Amazon Macie is a data security service that uses machine learning and pattern matching to **discover sensitive data in Amazon S3**.
- **Managed data identifiers** detect common sensitive types such as credit card numbers, Social Security numbers, bank account numbers, names and addresses.
- **Custom data identifiers** let you add your own regex patterns, such as internal customer IDs.
- **Sensitive data discovery jobs** scan chosen buckets and produce findings that show which objects contain which types of sensitive data; Macie also evaluates bucket security posture (for example public access).
## Why this matters before fine-tuning
Data used to fine-tune a model can influence its outputs, so PII left in the training set could end up exposed in generated text. Scanning transcripts with Macie first lets the team redact, mask or remove those records before training.
W: Amazon Macie: it is purpose-built to scan S3 objects and flag sensitive data like credit card and Social Security numbers, which is exactly the pre-training check required.
X: B=AWS Config: Config records and evaluates resource configurations, such as whether a bucket has encryption enabled; it does not read file contents to find PII.
C=AWS Trusted Advisor: Trusted Advisor gives account-level best-practice checks for areas such as cost, security and service quotas; it does not inspect data inside objects.
D=Amazon Inspector: Inspector scans workloads and code (EC2 instances, container images, Lambda functions and connected code repositories) for software vulnerabilities and exposure; it does not search S3 data for sensitive content.
T: To find PII inside S3 data before training, use Amazon Macie; Config, Inspector and Trusted Advisor check configurations or vulnerabilities, not data contents.
