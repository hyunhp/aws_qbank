G: ## How Amazon Bedrock on-demand pricing works
With on-demand mode you pay only for what you process, with no upfront commitment.
- **Text models** are billed per input token (the prompt, including any system prompt, history or retrieved context) and per output token (the generated reply). Output tokens usually cost more than input tokens.

- Other modes exist: **batch inference** at a discount, and **Provisioned Throughput** billed per model unit per hour.
## Example estimate
An app handles 100,000 requests a month, each with about 1,500 input tokens and 300 output tokens. That is 150 million input and 30 million output tokens; multiply each by the model's per-token price to get the monthly estimate. Doubling prompt size roughly doubles the input part of the bill.
W: Input and output tokens: on-demand text model charges are calculated directly from the number of tokens sent in and generated, so token volume per request times request count drives the estimate.
X: B=IAM roles: IAM is free of charge, and the number of roles only controls who may call the model. It has no effect on the per-request price.
C=Availability Zones: Bedrock is a Regional managed service; how many AZs the Region has is not a pricing dimension. Price varies by model and Region, not AZ count.
D=Console users: the number of people who can sign in to the console does not create charges. Only actual model invocations and the tokens they process are billed.
T: Estimate Bedrock on-demand cost from tokens in and tokens out per request, multiplied by request volume; account setup items like IAM roles or users do not drive it.

G: ## What an embedding is
An **embedding** is a list of numbers (a vector, for example 1,024 values) produced by an embedding model such as Amazon Titan Text Embeddings. The model is trained so that texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions.
- "How do I reset my password?" and "I forgot my login credentials" end up close together even though they share few words.
- Closeness is measured with **cosine similarity** or distance.
## How search uses embeddings
Every document chunk is embedded and stored in a vector store (for example Amazon OpenSearch Service or Aurora PostgreSQL with `pgvector`). At query time the question is embedded, and the store returns the nearest vectors. This semantic search is the retrieval step of RAG.
W: Embeddings: they are exactly the numeric vectors that encode meaning so that semantically similar sentences sit close together, enabling similarity search.
X: A=Hyperparameters: these are settings chosen before or during training, such as learning rate, batch size or number of epochs. They configure how a model learns and are not representations of sentences.
B=Tokenizers: a tokenizer splits text into tokens and maps them to integer IDs. Those IDs are the model's input, but they do not capture meaning or place similar sentences near each other.
D=Checkpoints: a checkpoint is a saved snapshot of model weights during training so a job can resume or be rolled back. It stores the model, not vectors for individual sentences.
T: Meaning-preserving vectors used for semantic search are embeddings; tokenizers only split text into IDs, and hyperparameters and checkpoints belong to training.

@ Q002557 explain
G: ## Parameters are what a model learned
A neural network is made of layers of numbers called **weights** (and biases). Together these are the model's **parameters**.
- During training, the model makes a prediction, measures the error, and nudges every parameter slightly to reduce that error. After billions of examples, the parameters encode the patterns of language, images or code the model has seen.
- A model with "70 billion parameters" stores 70 billion such learned numbers. More parameters usually mean more capacity, but also more memory, slower inference and higher cost.
## Parameters versus inference settings
Values you set per request, such as `temperature`, `top_p` or `maxTokens`, are often called **inference parameters**, but they do not change the model. They only steer how it samples output from its fixed learned weights.
W: Learned weights: parameters are the internal numeric values adjusted during training that encode what the model knows, and their count is what "billions of parameters" describes.
X: A=Allowed users: who may invoke a model is controlled by IAM policies and roles in the account. Access control lives outside the model and has nothing to do with its size.
C=Searchable documents: how many documents are looked up per request is a property of a retrieval system such as a RAG knowledge base, not of the model's internal structure.
D=Per-prompt settings: values like temperature or maximum output length are inference settings chosen per request. They shape sampling but are not learned and do not change the weights.
T: Model parameters are the weights learned in training; per-request settings like temperature only steer output and are not what "billions of parameters" counts.

A **modality** is a type of data: text, image, audio, video. A **multimodal** model accepts more than one modality as input (and sometimes produces more than one as output).
- A vision-language model takes an image plus a text question and answers in text, for example: photo of a sneaker + "What material is the upper made of?" returns "The upper appears to be knit mesh."

A text-only model never sees pixels, no matter how large its context window is. The image and the question must be understood together to produce a grounded answer.
W: Multimodal image-and-text understanding: answering natural-language questions about photos requires a model that takes both the image and the question as input and reasons across them.
X: A=Text-only with longer context: a bigger context window lets the model read more text, but a text-only model still cannot accept or interpret an uploaded photo.
B=Speech synthesis: text-to-speech (for example Amazon Polly) turns text into spoken audio. It produces sound from words and does not look at images at all.

@ Q002559 explain
G: ## How an LLM writes text
Large language models are **autoregressive**: they generate output one **token** at a time, where a token is a word or a piece of a word.
- At each step the model looks at all tokens so far (the prompt plus what it has already generated) and computes a probability for every token in its vocabulary.
- A token is chosen from that distribution, appended to the text, and the process repeats until a stop sequence or the maximum length is reached.
## Example
For "The capital of France is", the model assigns high probability to " Paris". Settings such as `temperature` and `top_p` control whether it always picks the most likely token or samples more freely among the likely ones, but the choice is always conditioned on the previous context.
W: Next-token prediction: the model predicts the most likely next token given all previous tokens, appends it, and repeats, which is how every response is built.
X: A=Random words ignoring context: sampling can add some randomness, but the probabilities come entirely from the earlier text. Without context the output would be gibberish rather than coherent answers.
B=Stored copy of the internet: a model does not keep a searchable copy of its training data. Knowledge is compressed into its weights, which is also why it can hallucinate instead of quoting a source.
D=Translating to code and executing: plain generation does not compile or run programs. Some agent setups let a model call a code tool, but the model itself only predicts tokens.
T: LLMs generate text by repeatedly predicting the next token from all prior tokens; they neither pick words at random nor look sentences up in a stored copy of the web.

@ Q002562 explain
G: ## What a token is
A **token** is the unit a language model reads and writes. Tokenizers split text into common chunks: a short word may be one token, while a long or rare word may be split into several. As a rough rule for English, one token is about three quarters of a word, and other languages can use more tokens per word.
## Why tokens matter to the business
- **Cost:** on-demand text model pricing is per input token and per output token, so longer prompts, retrieved context and longer answers cost more.
- **Limits:** a model's **context window**, for example 200,000 tokens, caps the prompt plus output. Long documents or chat histories must fit inside it.
- **Latency:** output is generated token by token, so longer responses take longer, and very long inputs also add processing time.
## Example
A 3,000-token prompt with a 500-token answer costs roughly twice as much as a 1,500-token prompt with a 250-token answer on the same model.
W: D=Context window in tokens: the maximum amount of text a model can handle in one request is defined as a number of tokens, covering both input and output.
E=More tokens cost more: billing is per token processed, so longer prompts and longer responses directly raise the cost of each request.
X: A=One token equals one word: tokenizers often split words into subword pieces, and punctuation can be separate tokens, so token counts and word counts differ.
B=No latency effect: models generate one token at a time, so more output tokens mean a longer response time, and longer inputs also take more processing.
C=Only image models: tokens are the core unit for text and chat models, which is where token-based pricing and context windows apply most directly. Image generation is often priced per image instead.
T: Tokens determine cost, context window limits and latency; a token is often only part of a word, and all text models count them.

G: ## Multi-tenant RAG needs retrieval-time isolation
In retrieval-augmented generation, the app searches a vector store for chunks similar to the question and passes them to the model. If all tenants' documents sit in one index, a pure similarity search can return another customer's chunk, and the model will happily use it.
## Metadata filtering
- Each chunk is stored with **metadata** attributes, such as `tenant_id`, `department` or `year`. In Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases this can come from a `.metadata.json` file next to each source document in Amazon S3.
- At query time the application adds a filter, for example `tenant_id` equals the caller's tenant, in the retrieval configuration of the `Retrieve` or `RetrieveAndGenerate` call.
- The vector search then considers only matching chunks, so documents from other tenants never reach the prompt.

W: Metadata filter by tenant: tagging chunks with a tenant identifier and filtering retrieval on it restricts results to that customer's documents before anything is sent to the model.

C=Larger embedding model: a bigger model may improve similarity quality, but it still ranks every tenant's documents together. Better embeddings do not create an isolation boundary.

- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** leaves the model unchanged. @ Q002568 explain
G: ## Why long conversations get expensive
Language models are stateless: to "remember" a chat, the application resends earlier turns with every new request. Those turns count as input tokens each time.
- Turn 1 might send 300 tokens, but by turn 40 the history can be 12,000 tokens, so every new message costs far more than the first and can eventually exceed the context window.
## Managing context
- **Trim:** keep only the last few turns verbatim.
- **Summarize:** replace older turns with a short running summary (for example 200 tokens), which the model can produce itself.
- **Retrieve:** store past facts and pull back only the relevant ones.
These keep the tokens per request roughly steady, so cost per message stays predictable while key details are preserved.
W: Summarize or trim older turns: shrinking the history sent with each request caps input tokens per call, keeping costs predictable and the conversation within the context window.
X: A=Send full history every time: input grows with every turn, so cost per message climbs steadily and the conversation eventually hits the context window limit.
B=Raise max tokens: a higher output limit allows longer replies, which adds output tokens and cost, and it does nothing to control the growing input history.
D=Duplicate to two models: sending each request twice roughly doubles token spend and adds complexity without making long conversations cheaper to handle.
T: Keep chat costs predictable by summarizing or trimming older turns; resending the full history or raising output limits makes each request more expensive.

G: ## Prompt chaining
**Prompt chaining** breaks a complex task into a sequence of smaller prompts. Each step has one focused job, and its output becomes the input of the next step.
- Step 1: "Extract all revenue and cost figures from these notes as a list."
- Step 2: "Using this list, identify the three biggest changes from last quarter and explain why."
- Step 3: "Write a one-page executive report from this analysis."
## Why it helps
A single giant prompt asks the model to do everything at once, and quality drops. Chaining makes each step simpler, lets you check or fix intermediate results, and allows different models or settings per step. On AWS, Amazon Bedrock Flows can wire such steps together, or the application can call the model several times in code.
W: Prompt chaining: splitting the report into extract, analyze and write steps, each feeding the next, is exactly the sequential multi-prompt pattern that chaining describes.
X: A=Model distillation: distillation trains a smaller student model to imitate a larger teacher model's outputs. It changes which model is used, not how one task is split into prompts.
C=Data augmentation: augmentation creates extra or modified training examples, such as paraphrases or rotated images, to improve model training. No training happens in this scenario.
D=Negative prompting: a negative prompt tells an image model what to leave out of a picture, such as "no text, no blur". It is a single-prompt setting, not a multi-step workflow.
T: Splitting a hard task into sequential prompts where each output feeds the next is prompt chaining; distillation and augmentation are training techniques.

G: ## Elements of an effective prompt
A good prompt removes guesswork for the model. Common building blocks are:
- **Instructions:** what to do, in clear words, plus constraints such as length, tone and output format ("Answer in three bullet points, under 60 words").
- **Context:** the facts the model needs, such as the relevant policy excerpt, customer record or retrieved documents.
- **Input data:** the specific item to work on.
- Optionally, **examples** of good output and a role ("You are a support agent").
## What hurts results
Irrelevant text adds tokens and can distract the model; vague wording produces inconsistent output; contradictory rules force the model to guess which one wins.
## Example
"Summarize the customer email below for a support agent in 3 bullets. Use only the order details provided. Order: #7731, shipped 2 May. Email: ..." has clear instructions and the context the answer depends on.
W: D=Clear instructions and constraints: stating exactly what task to perform and which limits apply lets the model produce the intended output consistently.
E=Relevant context or data: giving the model the specific information it needs grounds the answer and reduces guessing and hallucination.
X: A=Unrelated background text: padding a prompt with irrelevant material increases token cost and can pull the model toward off-topic content. Length alone does not improve answers.
B=Vague wording: leaving the task open to interpretation leads to answers that vary in format and focus, because the model has to guess what the user wants.
C=Contradictory rules: conflicting instructions make the model pick one rule unpredictably, which produces inconsistent or partly incorrect responses.
T: Prompts improve with clear instructions and relevant context; padding, vagueness and contradictions degrade results.

G: ## The customization ladder
Adapting a foundation model to a domain usually proceeds from cheapest to most expensive:
- **Prompt engineering:** better instructions, examples and output formats. Minutes to try, no training cost.
- **RAG:** connect the model to domain documents with Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases so answers use current, specific facts.
- **Fine-tuning:** train on labeled prompt and response pairs to change style or task behavior. It works best with a good number of quality examples and adds training and hosting cost.
- **Continued pre-training:** train on large amounts of **unlabeled** domain text so the model absorbs domain language.
- **Training from scratch:** rarely justified outside model builders.

A legal team with 80 labeled Q&A pairs first writes a strong prompt with a few examples and adds a knowledge base of contracts; fine-tuning is considered only if quality is still short.

G: ## Model distillation
**Distillation** transfers knowledge from a large, capable **teacher** model to a smaller, faster **student** model.
- The teacher answers many representative prompts.
- The student is trained to reproduce those answers (or the teacher's output probabilities).
- The student ends up close to the teacher's quality on that specific task, but is cheaper and faster to run at inference time.
Amazon Bedrock offers **Model Distillation**, which uses a teacher model to generate responses for your prompts and fine-tunes a smaller supported student model, typically from the same model provider.
## Example
A support bot uses a large model that costs too much at 2 million requests a month. The team distills it into a small model that handles the same ticket categories at a fraction of the per-token cost.
W: Model distillation: training a smaller model to imitate a larger model's outputs, so it runs more cheaply at inference, is exactly what distillation means.
X: B=Transfer learning: transfer learning reuses a model pre-trained on one task as the starting point for another task, usually with fine-tuning. It is not specifically about a small model copying a big model's outputs.
C=Data poisoning: data poisoning is an attack where malicious examples are slipped into training data to corrupt a model's behavior. It is a security threat, not an efficiency technique.
D=Prompt chaining: chaining splits a task into sequential prompts to one or more models. It changes how requests are structured, not which model is trained.
T: A small student model trained to imitate a large teacher is distillation; transfer learning reuses a model for a new task and data poisoning is an attack.

G: ## What makes a good fine-tuning dataset
Fine-tuning teaches a model by example, so the model copies whatever patterns the data contains, good or bad.
- **Task and format match:** examples should look like real production requests and ideal responses, in the same structure. In Amazon Bedrock, training data is a JSONL file of prompt and completion pairs or conversation messages.
- **Accuracy and consistency:** responses should be correct and follow one style and labeling standard, otherwise the model learns conflicting behavior.
- **Hygiene:** remove sensitive personal data, deduplicate, and keep a separate validation set.
## Example
For a model that writes product summaries, 1,000 carefully reviewed pairs of product specs and on-brand summaries beat 20,000 scraped, inconsistent texts from unrelated sites.
W: A=Matches the target task and format: examples that mirror real inputs and the desired outputs teach the model exactly the behavior needed in production.
E=Accurate and consistent labels: correct, uniformly styled responses give the model a clear signal; errors and inconsistencies are learned just as faithfully.
X: B=Keep sensitive personal data: models can memorize and later reveal training data, so PII should be removed or masked. Keeping it creates privacy and compliance risk.
C=Many unrelated domains: data from unrelated topics dilutes the signal for the target task and can make the model worse at the job it is being tuned for.
D=Duplicate examples: repeating examples overweights them, encourages overfitting and memorization, and distorts the balance of the dataset instead of adding information.
T: Fine-tuning data must be relevant to the task and format and have accurate, consistent labels; PII, unrelated data and duplicates hurt.

G: ## Metrics depend on the task
- **BLEU** (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) scores machine translation by measuring how many word sequences (n-grams) in the model output also appear in one or more human reference translations, with a penalty for outputs that are too short. Scores range from 0 to 1 (often shown as 0 to 100); higher means closer to the references.
- **ROUGE** is the recall-oriented counterpart commonly used for summarization.
- Regression metrics such as **RMSE** and **MAPE** measure numeric prediction error, and **recall** measures how many actual positives a classifier found.
## Example
Reference: "The meeting starts at nine." Model: "The meeting begins at nine." Many shared words and word pairs give a fairly high BLEU; a garbled translation shares few n-grams and scores low.
W: BLEU: it is the standard metric for comparing machine translation output with reference translations through n-gram overlap.
X: A=RMSE: root mean square error measures how far numeric predictions are from actual values, for example in price forecasting. It cannot compare sentences.
B=MAPE: mean absolute percentage error expresses forecast error as a percentage of actual values, as in demand forecasting. It applies to numbers, not text.
C=Recall: recall is a classification metric, the share of actual positive cases the model correctly identified. It does not score how close a translation is to a reference text.
T: Use BLEU for translation and ROUGE for summarization; RMSE and MAPE are for numeric predictions and recall is for classification.

G: ## Evaluating open-ended answers
Chatbot answers can be correct in many different wordings, so metrics that compare against one reference answer miss good responses. Human review is the gold standard but is slow and costly at scale.
## Model as a judge
- A capable **judge model** receives the question, the chatbot's answer and a **rubric**, for example correctness, completeness, helpfulness and harmlessness on a 1 to 5 scale, and returns scores with reasons.
- Humans review a sample of judgments to confirm the judge agrees with expert opinion and to refine the rubric.
- Amazon Bedrock model evaluation and RAG evaluation support this **LLM-as-a-judge** approach with built-in and custom metrics, alongside human-based evaluation jobs.
## Example
A team scores 5,000 answers overnight with a judge model, then has two experts check 100 of them; agreement is high, so the scores are trusted for comparing versions.
W: Model as a judge with rubric and human spot checks: it scales evaluation of free-form answers without needing a single reference, and human sampling keeps the judge honest.
X: A=Exact string matching: open-ended answers rarely match a reference word for word, so correct but differently phrased responses would be marked wrong.
C=Longer means better: length does not measure correctness; long answers can be padded or wrong, and rewarding length encourages verbose responses.
D=Counting product names: keyword frequency says nothing about whether an answer is accurate or helpful, and it rewards unnatural repetition.
T: Evaluate open-ended outputs with a rubric-based judge model validated by human spot checks; exact match, length and keyword counts do not measure quality.
