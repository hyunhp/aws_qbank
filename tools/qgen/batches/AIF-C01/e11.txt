G: ## Kinds of supervised prediction
Supervised models learn from labeled examples and predict a target. The type of target decides the task:
- **Classification** predicts a category. With exactly two classes it is **binary classification** (fraud / legitimate, spam / not spam); with more it is multi-class (low / medium / high risk).
- **Regression** predicts a continuous number (transaction amount, delivery time).
## How a fraud classifier works
The model receives features such as amount, merchant type, time of day and distance from home, and outputs a score such as 0.92 for "fraud". A threshold (for example 0.5) turns the score into one of the two labels.
Unsupervised techniques, such as clustering, topic modeling and dimensionality reduction, have no target label at all.
W: Binary classification: the output is one of exactly two categories, fraudulent or legitimate, learned from labeled historical transactions.
X: A=Topic modeling: an unsupervised text technique that discovers themes in a collection of documents; it does not assign a fraud/legitimate label to a transaction.
B=Dimensionality reduction: compresses many features into fewer ones (for example with PCA); it is a preprocessing or visualization step, not a prediction of a class.
D=Linear regression: predicts a continuous numeric value such as a price, not a choice between two categories.
T: Two possible categories as output means binary classification; a continuous number would be regression.

G: ## Traditional ML versus deep learning
Both are machine learning: they learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules.
- **Traditional ML** (linear models, decision trees, gradient boosting) usually relies on **feature engineering**: people decide which inputs matter, such as "average purchase value" or "word counts".
- **Deep learning** uses **neural networks with many layers**. Early layers learn simple patterns and later layers combine them into complex ones, so the network learns useful features directly from raw data.
Example: to recognize cats in photos, a traditional model needs hand-crafted features like edges or color histograms; a convolutional neural network learns edges, then shapes, then "cat face" on its own from raw pixels.
Deep learning shines on unstructured data (images, audio, text) but typically needs more data and compute, often GPUs or accelerators.
W: Multi-layer networks that learn features: this is the defining trait of deep learning; stacked layers of neurons build their own representations from raw input instead of relying on hand-crafted features.
X: A=Deep learning needs no data: deep learning usually needs more training data than traditional ML, not none.
B=Deep learning only for tabular data: it is the reverse; deep learning is especially strong on images, audio and text, while traditional ML often excels on tabular data.
C=Traditional ML only on GPUs: most traditional algorithms run fine on CPUs, while deep learning is the one that commonly benefits from GPUs or accelerators.
T: Deep learning means many-layer neural networks that learn features automatically; traditional ML usually depends on engineered features.

G: ## The confusion matrix
For a fraud model, each prediction lands in one of four boxes:
- **True positive (TP)**: flagged, and really fraud.
- **False positive (FP)**: flagged, but legitimate.
- **False negative (FN)**: not flagged, but really fraud.
- **True negative (TN)**: not flagged, and legitimate.
## Precision versus recall
- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): of everything the model flagged, how much was right?
- **Recall** = TP / (TP + FN): of all real fraud, how much did the model catch?
Here the model flagged 100 transactions and 80 were fraud: 80 / 100 = **80% precision**. We cannot compute recall, because we do not know how many fraudulent transactions were missed.
W: Precision: it is the share of positive predictions that are correct, which is exactly 80 correct out of 100 flagged.
X: B=Recall: recall divides by all actual fraud cases (TP + FN); the question only describes the flagged set, so the missed fraud count needed for recall is unknown.
C=R-squared: a regression metric describing how much variance in a numeric target the model explains; it does not apply to fraud/legitimate labels.
D=Specificity: the true negative rate, TN / (TN + FP), which measures how well legitimate transactions are left unflagged, not how accurate the flags are.
T: "Of the items flagged, how many were right" is precision; "of the real positives, how many were found" is recall.

**Anomaly detection** learns what "normal" looks like and flags data points that deviate from it. It is usually unsupervised or semi-supervised, so it works even when very few examples of bad behavior are labeled.
How it works for accounts:
- The model studies typical patterns: login times, transaction sizes, locations, frequency.

- High scores are sent for review, even if the pattern has never been seen before.

On AWS, SageMaker offers the built-in Random Cut Forest algorithm for this kind of problem.

G: ## Parameters versus hyperparameters
- **Parameters** are learned by the model during training, such as the weights of a neural network or the split points in a tree.
- **Hyperparameters** are settings chosen before training that control how learning happens, such as **learning rate**, **max tree depth**, number of trees or batch size.
## Hyperparameter tuning
Tuning runs many training jobs with different hyperparameter combinations and keeps the one with the best validation metric.
Example: try learning rates `0.01`, `0.1`, `0.3` with depths `3`, `6`, `9`, measure validation AUC for each, and pick the best pair.
SageMaker **Automatic Model Tuning** does this for you with search strategies such as random search, Bayesian optimization and Hyperband, and can stop poor trials early.
W: Hyperparameter tuning: learning rate and tree depth are hyperparameters, and searching automatically for the combination that gives the best validation score is exactly what tuning means.
X: A=Data augmentation: creates extra training examples by modifying existing ones (for example rotating images); it does not choose training settings.
B=Model deployment: makes a trained model available for predictions; it happens after the model and its settings are chosen.
C=Feature engineering: creates or transforms input variables from raw data; it changes what the model sees, not the learning rate or depth.
T: Settings chosen before training, like learning rate and depth, are hyperparameters, and searching for their best values is hyperparameter tuning.

G: ## Underfitting versus overfitting
- **Underfitting**: the model is too simple or undertrained to capture the pattern. It performs **poorly on training data** and **poorly on validation/test data**. Training and validation scores are both low and close together.
- **Overfitting**: the model memorizes the training data. It scores **very high on training** but much lower on validation/test, a large gap.
Example: a straight line fitted to clearly curved data gets 60% on training and 58% on validation, which is underfitting. A very deep tree that gets 99% on training but 70% on validation is overfitting.
Typical fixes for underfitting: a more complex model, better features, longer training, or less regularization.
W: A=Poor validation accuracy as well: an underfit model has not learned the pattern, so it also performs poorly on unseen data.
E=Poor training accuracy: failing even on the data it was trained on is the clearest sign that the model is too simple or undertrained.
X: B=Improves only on test data: a model does not learn from the test set, and better test than training results is not a recognized sign of underfitting; it usually points to a small or unrepresentative split.
C=Near-perfect training accuracy: shows the model fits the training data very well, the opposite of underfitting and a common warning sign of overfitting.
D=Large train-test gap: high training but low test accuracy is the classic sign of overfitting, not underfitting.
T: Underfitting is poor results everywhere, training included; overfitting is great training results with a big drop on new data.

ML is useful when the rules are too complex, fuzzy or changing to write by hand, but historical examples exist to learn from. Signs of a good fit:

D=Alphabetical sorting: standard sorting algorithms give a perfect result; there is nothing to learn from data.

G: ## Training-serving skew
Features are often computed twice: in a batch pipeline for training and again in application code at inference time. If the two implementations differ, for example one computes "average spend over 30 days" and the other over 31 days, the model sees different inputs in production than in training. This is **training-serving skew**, and it quietly lowers accuracy.

- An **offline store** in `Amazon S3` holds historical feature values for building training datasets.
- An **online store** gives low-latency reads of the latest values for real-time inference.
- **Feature groups** define each feature once, so training and serving use the same definitions and values, and teams can discover and reuse them.
W: SageMaker Feature Store: it stores the same engineered features for both training (offline store) and real-time inference (online store), removing inconsistency between the two.
X: A=SageMaker Ground Truth: produces labels with human workers; it does not store or serve engineered features.

G: ## Scores and thresholds
Many classification models output a **probability score** between 0 and 1 rather than a hard yes/no. A score of `0.73` means the model estimates a 73% chance that this customer belongs to the "will buy" class, based on the features it saw.
To act on the score, the business picks a **threshold**:
- Threshold `0.5`: 0.73 becomes "likely to buy".
- Threshold `0.8`: the same customer is not targeted.
Raising the threshold gives fewer but more confident positives (higher precision); lowering it catches more buyers but also more false positives (higher recall).
A score is an estimate, not a promise: some customers with 0.73 will not buy, and how well scores match real frequencies (calibration) should be checked on validation data.
W: Probability score compared with a threshold: 0.73 is the model's estimated likelihood of the positive class, and the business decides which cutoff turns it into an action.
X: A=Share of training data from this customer: the score is about this prediction; it says nothing about how much of the training set came from any one customer.
C=Guaranteed purchase within 73 days: the output is a probability, not a guarantee, and it contains no time unit.
D=Training 73% complete: training happens before predictions are made and is not tracked per customer; the score reflects the prediction, not training progress.
T: A classifier output such as 0.73 is a probability estimate turned into a decision by a threshold, never a guarantee.

G: ## What a large language model is
A **large language model (LLM)** is a neural network, usually a transformer, with billions of parameters, pretrained on huge amounts of text.
- **Pretraining**: the model repeatedly predicts the next token (word piece) in a sequence, for example "The capital of France is" becomes "Paris".
- Doing this at massive scale teaches grammar, facts and reasoning patterns.
- Afterward, with prompts and often instruction tuning, the same model can answer questions, summarize, translate, classify and write code without a separate model for each task.
LLMs are a type of **foundation model**: one general model adapted to many tasks. On AWS, LLMs from several providers, including Amazon Nova, are available through Amazon Bedrock.
W: Large language model: next-token prediction pretraining on large text corpora, followed by use on many tasks such as Q&A, summarization and translation, describes an LLM.
X: A=k-nearest neighbors: predicts from the labels of the most similar stored examples; it does not generate text or learn language from next-word prediction.
B=Decision tree: splits tabular features with if/then rules to predict a class or number; it cannot produce fluent text or summaries.
D=Linear regression: predicts a single numeric value as a weighted sum of features; it has no ability to generate language.
T: Pretrained on massive text to predict the next token and then reused for many language tasks means a large language model.

G: ## Inference parameters for text generation
When you call a model, for example through Amazon Bedrock, you can shape the output with inference parameters:
- **Temperature**, **top-p** and **top-k**: control how random or focused word choice is.
- **Maximum tokens**: caps the length of the response.
- **Stop sequences**: strings that make the model stop generating as soon as it produces them.
Example: with stop sequence `END`, the output "Your order ships Friday. END Is there..." is cut to "Your order ships Friday. " and nothing after the marker is generated. This keeps responses tidy, prevents the model from continuing into an extra turn, and saves output tokens.
W: Stop sequence: it tells the model to halt generation the moment the specified text, here `END`, appears in the output.
X: A=Seed value: makes random sampling repeatable so the same prompt gives the same output; it does not tell the model where to stop.
C=Temperature: adjusts how random or conservative word choices are; the model keeps generating until it reaches a stop condition or the token limit.
D=Top-k: limits sampling to the k most likely next tokens; it affects which words are chosen, not when generation ends.
T: Ending generation at a specific string is a stop sequence; temperature, top-k and seed only change how tokens are chosen.

G: ## How a GAN works
A **generative adversarial network (GAN)** trains two neural networks against each other:
- The **generator** takes random noise and produces synthetic samples, such as images.
- The **discriminator** receives both real samples and generated ones and learns to tell which is which.
- Each round, the discriminator gets better at spotting fakes, and the generator uses that feedback to make more realistic samples.
Over time the generator learns to produce outputs that look like the real data, for example new face images that belong to no real person.
GANs are used for image generation, synthetic data and image-to-image tasks. Many modern image generators instead use diffusion models, but GANs remain a core generative AI concept.
W: Generator and discriminator: a GAN is defined by this adversarial pair, one network creating samples and the other judging real versus fake, which pushes the generator toward realistic output.
X: B=Tree-based numeric prediction: describes a regression model such as gradient-boosted trees, not a generative neural network.
C=Two networks translating in opposite directions: resembles back-translation or cycle-consistent translation, not the generator-versus-discriminator setup of a GAN.
D=Single network returning the closest image: that is memorization or nearest-neighbor lookup; a GAN uses two networks and generates new samples rather than copying stored ones.
T: GAN means a generator making fakes and a discriminator catching them, training each other.

G: ## Risks of generating content at scale
A foundation model that writes one email is a small risk; one that writes millions multiplies every weakness.
- **Content risk**: hallucinated offers, wrong names, off-brand tone or inappropriate text could reach huge numbers of customers before anyone notices. Mitigations include templates, Amazon Bedrock Guardrails, sampling-based human review and automated checks.
- **Cost risk**: on-demand pricing for text models is per input and output token, so cost grows with number of messages times prompt and response length. Mitigations include shorter prompts, smaller models where quality allows, batch inference, and cost monitoring with budgets.
Example: 5 million emails with 500 input tokens and 300 output tokens each is roughly 4 billion tokens, so small per-token prices add up.
W: D=Incorrect or inappropriate content at scale: generated text can contain errors or harmful wording, and at millions of messages even a small error rate reaches many customers and harms trust.
E=Inference costs growing with volume: token-based pricing means cost rises with each message and its length, so budgeting and optimization are needed.
X: A=Refusing to generate English: mainstream foundation models are built to produce English text; this is not a realistic planning risk.

G: ## AWS machine learning chips
AWS designs its own accelerators for ML workloads, each aimed at one phase:
- **AWS Trainium** is built for **training** deep learning and generative AI models. Instance families such as `Trn1` and `Trn2` use it, and AWS positions them as offering lower cost to train than comparable GPU-based EC2 instances. Instances can be clustered in UltraClusters for very large models.
- **AWS Inferentia** is built for **inference**: serving already-trained models at high throughput and low cost per prediction (`Inf1`, `Inf2` instances).
Both are programmed through the **AWS Neuron SDK**, which integrates with frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX.
Example: a company pretraining a large language model runs the training job on a cluster of Trn2 instances, then deploys the finished model on Inf2 instances for serving.
W: AWS Trainium instances: Trainium is purpose-built by AWS for training large models and is designed to reduce training cost compared with alternatives.
X: B=AWS Wavelength Zones: place compute inside 5G carrier networks for ultra-low latency to mobile devices; they are not training hardware.
C=AWS Inferentia chips: designed for inference, running trained models cheaply at scale, not for training a very large model.
D=AWS Snowball devices: rugged devices for moving data into and out of AWS or for edge computing in disconnected sites; they are not built for large-scale model training.
T: Trainium is for training and Inferentia is for inference; pick Trainium to lower the cost of training large models.
