G: ## How Automatic Model Tuning searches
A SageMaker hyperparameter tuning job launches many training jobs, each with a different combination of hyperparameters, and uses a strategy such as Bayesian optimization to pick the next combination based on the results so far.
A brand-new tuning job starts with no history, so its first trials are close to random exploration.
## Warm start
A **warm start** tuning job names one or more earlier tuning jobs as parents. Their evaluated combinations and objective values seed the search, so the new job starts from what is already known instead of exploring from scratch.
- `IDENTICAL_DATA_AND_ALGORITHM`: same data and algorithm; you can still widen or change hyperparameter ranges.
- `TRANSFER_LEARNING`: the input data, ranges and static hyperparameters may differ more from the parent.
Example: a parent job explored `learning_rate` 0.001-0.1; the child widens it to 0.0001-0.2 and converges in fewer trials.
W: Warm start tuning jobs: the new job reuses the parent jobs' results as prior knowledge, which is exactly "reuse what the last tuning job learned and converge faster", and it allows the expanded range.
X: A=Model Registry versioning: the registry stores and versions trained model packages with approval status; it does not feed tuning results into a new hyperparameter search.
B=Multi-model endpoints: this is a hosting feature that serves many models from one endpoint; it has nothing to do with how a tuning job chooses hyperparameters.
D=Early stopping: early stopping ends unpromising training jobs inside a tuning job to save cost, but each new tuning job still starts without the history of previous tuning jobs.
T: To build on an earlier tuning job's results, start the new job as a warm start; early stopping only trims poor trials within one job.

G: ## Reading training and validation curves
Training accuracy shows how well a model fits data it has seen; validation accuracy shows how well it generalizes to data it has not seen.
- Both low: **underfitting** (high bias), the model is too simple or undertrained.
- Training high, validation clearly lower and falling: **overfitting** (high variance), the model memorizes noise in the training set.
Example: training accuracy goes 80% to 99% while validation peaks at 70% and then drops. Each extra epoch fits the training data more closely but makes predictions on new data worse.
## Remedies for overfitting
- Regularization: L1/L2 weight penalties, dropout.
- Reduce complexity: fewer layers or parameters, shallower trees.
- Early stopping at the best validation epoch.
- More or augmented training data.
W: Overfitting, add regularization or reduce complexity: the widening gap with falling validation accuracy is the classic overfitting signature, and constraining the model is the standard fix.
X: A=Training set too large: more data usually reduces overfitting; shrinking the training set would make memorization easier and the gap wider.
C=Learning rate too low: a low learning rate slows training on both curves; it does not explain training at 99% while validation falls.
D=Underfitting: underfitting shows low training accuracy too; at 99% training accuracy the model clearly has enough capacity, and adding more would worsen the problem.
T: Rising training accuracy with falling validation accuracy means overfitting, so constrain the model; adding capacity is the fix for the opposite problem.

G: ## Bias versus variance
- **Underfitting (high bias)**: the model is too simple to capture the pattern, so error is high on training and validation data alike, and more epochs barely help.
- **Overfitting (high variance)**: training error is low but validation error is much higher.
Example: fitting a straight line to a curved relationship leaves large errors everywhere, however long you train.
## Remedies for underfitting
- Increase capacity: more layers or units, deeper trees, more boosting rounds.
- Better features: interactions, polynomial terms, domain features.
- Reduce regularization: lower L2 weight decay or dropout rate.
- Train longer only if the loss is still clearly falling.
Tools that fight overfitting (stronger regularization, dropout, less data) make underfitting worse because they limit what the model can learn.
W: Increase capacity: high error on both sets that does not improve with more epochs means the model cannot represent the relationship, so a more expressive model (or richer features) is what helps.
X: A=Stronger L2 weight decay: L2 pushes weights toward zero and makes the model simpler, which increases bias and raises error further.
B=Smaller training set: less data gives the model fewer examples to learn from and never fixes a model that is too simple.
C=More dropout: dropout is an overfitting remedy; randomly dropping units further limits an already underfitting network.
T: High error everywhere is underfitting, so add capacity or features; regularization and dropout are for overfitting.

G: ## Two ensemble strategies
Ensembles combine many models (often decision trees) into one stronger predictor.
- **Bagging** (bootstrap aggregating): each model trains independently on a random sample drawn with replacement, so models can train in parallel. Predictions are averaged or voted, which smooths out individual models' quirks and mainly **reduces variance**. Random Forest is the best-known example.
- **Boosting**: models train one after another; each new model focuses on the examples the previous ones got wrong (reweighting in AdaBoost, fitting residual gradients in gradient boosting). This mainly **reduces bias**. XGBoost, LightGBM and AdaBoost are examples.
Example: deep, noisy trees benefit from bagging; shallow, weak trees benefit from boosting.
Both work with many base learners; trees are simply the most common choice.
W: Bagging parallel and variance, boosting sequential and bias: bagging averages independently trained models to cancel noise, while boosting chains models that correct earlier errors to reduce systematic error.
X: A=Same technique: they differ in how models are trained (independent versus sequential) and in which error component they mainly reduce.
B=Swapped definitions: it is boosting that trains sequentially on previous errors and bagging that trains independent models in parallel.
D=Tied to model types: both are general methods; bagging is most often used with trees (Random Forest) and boosting also works with other weak learners, so neither is limited to one model family.
T: Bagging runs models in parallel to reduce variance; boosting runs them in sequence to reduce bias, not the other way round.

G: ## Making models fit on edge devices
Trained deep learning weights are usually 32-bit floats. An edge device with little memory and no large GPU may not store or run them fast enough.
## Quantization
Quantization stores weights (and often activations) in lower precision, such as 16-bit floats or 8-bit integers.
- Size: moving from FP32 to INT8 cuts weight storage by about 4x, for example 400 MB to about 100 MB.
- Speed: integer math is cheaper, and many edge accelerators are built for INT8.
- Cost: a small, usually measurable accuracy loss; quantization-aware training or calibration keeps it small.
Related techniques include pruning (removing weights) and distillation (training a smaller student model), but only quantization works by lowering numerical precision.
Tools such as ONNX Runtime, TensorRT and TensorFlow Lite apply it when compiling models for a target device.
W: Quantization: it reduces the numerical precision of the weights, which directly shrinks the model and speeds up inference, accepting a small, controlled accuracy loss.
X: A=More hidden layers: extra layers add parameters, making the model larger and slower, the opposite of what an edge device needs.
C=Larger training batch size: batch size affects training speed and stability; the deployed model has the same number and precision of weights afterward.
D=More training epochs: training longer changes the weight values, not their count or precision, so size and latency stay the same.
T: Lower-precision weights (quantization) shrink and speed up a model for edge devices; training settings like epochs and batch size do not change model size.

G: ## Regularization as a loss penalty
Regularization adds a penalty to the loss so the optimizer prefers simpler models.
- **L2 (ridge, weight decay)**: `loss + λ·Σw²`. Large weights cost a lot, so all weights shrink toward zero but rarely reach exactly zero. This smooths the model and reduces overfitting.
- **L1 (lasso)**: `loss + λ·Σ|w|`. The absolute value penalty pushes many weights to exactly zero, giving sparse models and built-in feature selection.
Example: with λ = 0.01, a weight of 10 adds 1.0 to the loss under L2 but only 0.1 under L1, so L2 punishes big weights much harder.
Larger λ means stronger shrinkage; too large a λ leads to underfitting.
Other overfitting tools, such as dropout or learning rate schedules, change the training process rather than adding a weight penalty to the loss.
W: L2 regularization (weight decay): a penalty of λ times the sum of squared weights is the definition of L2, and it keeps weights small, which reduces overfitting.
X: B=Dropout: dropout randomly zeroes activations during training; it does not add any term to the loss function.
C=Learning rate decay: this lowers the optimizer's step size over time; the loss function itself is unchanged.
D=L1 regularization: L1 penalizes the sum of absolute weights, not squared weights, and that is what drives weights to exactly zero.
T: A squared-weight penalty is L2 and shrinks weights smoothly; an absolute-value penalty is L1 and creates exact zeros.

G: ## Two ways to split training across machines
- **Data parallelism**: every worker (GPU or instance) holds a full copy of the model and processes a different shard of each batch. After each step, gradients are averaged across workers (all-reduce) so all copies stay in sync.
- **Model parallelism**: the model itself is split across devices, used when it does not fit in one device's memory.
Example: one epoch over 100 million records takes 8 hours on one instance. With 8 instances each processing one eighth of the data, an epoch takes roughly 1 hour plus some communication overhead.
## On SageMaker
You set `instance_count` greater than 1 and a `distribution` option (for example PyTorch DDP or the SageMaker distributed data parallel library). Communication cost means scaling is close to, but not perfectly, linear.
W: Distributed data-parallel training: it processes different portions of the data on multiple instances at the same time, directly cutting wall-clock time, and uses the extra compute budget the team has.
X: A=Smaller dataset: training on less data is faster but throws away information and usually lowers model quality; it does not use the extra instances.
C=Plain SGD instead of Adam: plain SGD often needs more steps to converge and does not spread work across machines, so it is not a way to shorten training.
D=More epochs: more passes over the data increase total training time rather than reducing it.
T: With a massive dataset and budget for more machines, split the data across instances (data parallelism); model parallelism is for models too big for one device.

G: ## The confusion matrix trade-off
For a disease screening model, positives are sick patients.
- **Recall (sensitivity)** = TP / (TP + FN): the share of sick patients the model catches.
- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): the share of flagged patients who are truly sick.
- **Specificity** = TN / (TN + FP): the share of healthy patients correctly cleared.
Lowering the decision threshold flags more people: recall rises and precision usually falls.
Example: out of 100 sick patients, a model that catches 95 has 95% recall. If it also flags 200 healthy people, precision is only about 32%, but those people just get a follow-up test.
When a missed case (false negative) is far costlier than a false alarm, you maximize recall and accept lower precision.
W: Recall: recall directly measures how many actual positive cases are found, so maximizing it minimizes costly false negatives, at the price of more false positives.
X: A=Training accuracy: accuracy on the training set says nothing about generalization, and accuracy in general mixes both error types instead of targeting false negatives.
B=Specificity: specificity measures how well healthy patients are cleared, which is driven by false positives, the error that is cheaper here.
C=Precision: precision penalizes false positives; maximizing it makes the model cautious about flagging, which increases missed cases.
T: When missing a positive is the expensive mistake, maximize recall; precision is the metric to push when false alarms are the expensive mistake.

@ Q000939 explain
G: ## Balancing two error types in one number
For a spam filter (positive = spam):
- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): falls when good emails are wrongly sent to spam.
- **Recall** = TP / (TP + FN): falls when spam gets through.
- **F1** = 2 × P × R / (P + R), the harmonic mean of the two.
The harmonic mean is pulled toward the lower value. Example: precision 0.95 and recall 0.40 give an arithmetic mean of 0.675 but F1 of about 0.56, so a model cannot hide a weakness in one metric behind strength in the other.
## Why not accuracy
With imbalanced data, accuracy is dominated by the majority class. If 95% of emails are legitimate, a model that never flags spam still scores 95% accuracy. F1 ignores true negatives and focuses on how well the positive class is handled.
If one error type matters more, F-beta (for example F2) weights recall more heavily.
W: F1 score: it combines precision (lost emails) and recall (spam getting through) into one metric, penalizes imbalance between them, and is not inflated by the majority class.
X: B=R-squared: R-squared measures explained variance for regression outputs; it does not count false positives and false negatives of a classifier.
C=Accuracy on the full test set: on imbalanced data accuracy is dominated by the common class and can look high while one error type is severe.
D=RMSE of probabilities: RMSE measures average squared error of numeric predictions; it does not show the balance between precision and recall at a decision threshold.
T: One classification metric that balances false positives and false negatives on imbalanced data is F1; accuracy hides minority-class errors.

G: ## The four cells of a confusion matrix
With "sick" as the positive class:
- **True positive (TP)**: sick patient predicted sick.
- **False positive (FP)**: healthy patient predicted sick (a false alarm).
- **False negative (FN)**: sick patient predicted healthy (a miss).
- **True negative (TN)**: healthy patient predicted healthy.
## Which metric each error hurts
- Precision = TP / (TP + FP): false positives lower it.
- Recall = TP / (TP + FN): false negatives lower it.
- Specificity = TN / (TN + FP): false positives also lower it.
Example: the model flags 150 patients, 100 of them truly sick. The 50 healthy patients are false positives, so precision is 100 / 150, about 67%.
W: False positives, lower precision: healthy patients flagged as sick are false positives, and they enlarge the denominator of precision.
X: A=False negatives, recall: false negatives are sick patients predicted healthy; here the errors are healthy patients predicted sick.
B=True positives, accuracy: true positives are correct predictions of sick patients and raise accuracy rather than lowering it.
C=True negatives, specificity: true negatives are healthy patients correctly cleared; they are correct predictions and raise specificity.
T: Flagging healthy people is a false positive and hurts precision; missing sick people is a false negative and hurts recall.

G: ## MAE versus RMSE
Both measure regression error in the target's units, but they weight errors differently.
- **MAE** = mean of |error|. Every minute of error counts the same.
- **RMSE** = square root of the mean of error². Squaring makes big errors count much more.
Example with errors in minutes:
- Model 1: 5, 5, 5, 5 gives MAE 5 and RMSE 5.
- Model 2: 0, 0, 0, 20 gives MAE 5 but RMSE 10.
MAE rates both models equally; RMSE clearly prefers the model without the one very late prediction.
## Choosing
Use RMSE when a few large errors are disproportionately expensive. Use MAE when all errors cost about the same per unit and outliers should not dominate.
W: RMSE: squaring the errors before averaging makes large errors dominate the score, which matches a business where a few very late deliveries cost far more than many small errors.
X: B=MAE: MAE weighs every unit of error equally, so one big miss counts no more than several small ones, which understates the costly late deliveries.
C=AUC-ROC: AUC-ROC evaluates how well a classifier ranks positives above negatives; delivery time is a continuous regression target.
D=Accuracy of rounded predictions: rounding and counting exact matches treats a 1-minute miss and a 3-hour miss the same, ignoring error size.
T: When large errors are much more costly, use RMSE; MAE treats all errors in proportion to their size.

@ Q000942 explain
G: ## What the ROC curve and AUC show
The ROC curve plots true positive rate (recall) against false positive rate as the classification threshold moves from strict to lenient.
**AUC** (area under that curve) is the probability that a randomly chosen positive example gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative example.
- AUC 1.0: perfect ranking, every positive scores above every negative.
- AUC 0.5: the diagonal line, the ranking is as good as a coin flip.
- AUC below 0.5: worse than random, the scores are systematically inverted.
Example: a model that outputs random numbers ends up with an ROC curve along the diagonal and AUC close to 0.5.
AUC is threshold-independent, so it says nothing directly about precision or accuracy at a particular threshold.
W: No better than random guessing: an AUC of 0.5 means the model's scores do not separate positives from negatives any better than chance.
X: A=Highly overfit: overfitting is detected by comparing training and test performance; one test AUC of 0.5 shows poor ranking but not why it happens.
B=Perfect separation: perfect separation corresponds to AUC 1.0, not 0.5.
C=Precision exactly 50%: precision depends on a chosen threshold and on class balance; AUC 0.5 does not fix precision at any particular value.
T: AUC 0.5 means random ranking and 1.0 means perfect ranking; AUC is not the same thing as precision or accuracy.

When one class is rare, accuracy is dominated by the majority class.
Example: 10,000 transactions with 50 frauds (0.5%). A model that labels everything "legitimate" is 99.5% accurate and catches zero fraud. The model in the question, at 99.4%, is effectively doing the same.

G: ## Vanishing gradients
In a deep network, gradients are propagated backward layer by layer. With some activations and initializations they are multiplied by small numbers again and again, so the earliest layers receive gradients close to zero. Those layers stop learning and the loss plateaus early.
## SageMaker Debugger
Debugger saves tensors (weights, gradients, losses) from a training job at intervals and evaluates **rules** against them in parallel with training.
- Built-in rules include `vanishing_gradient`, `exploding_tensor`, `overfit`, `loss_not_decreasing` and `poor_weight_initialization`.
- A triggered rule changes status and can raise an Amazon CloudWatch event, notify the team, or use a built-in action to stop the training job.
Example: the `vanishing_gradient` rule flags when the mean absolute gradient falls below a threshold such as `1e-7`.
W: Debugger's vanishing gradient rule: Debugger monitors gradient tensors during training and its built-in rule detects exactly this condition and can trigger an alert.
X: B=Clarify bias detection: Clarify measures data and prediction bias and feature attributions; it does not watch gradients during training.
C=Model Monitor data quality: Model Monitor checks data captured from a deployed endpoint against a baseline; it runs after deployment, not inside a training job.
D=Ground Truth active learning: this reduces labeling effort by auto-labeling confident examples; it has no view into a model's training tensors.
T: Problems inside a running training job, such as vanishing gradients, are caught by Debugger rules; Model Monitor watches deployed endpoints.

G: ## ROC versus precision-recall curves
- **ROC curve**: true positive rate against false positive rate, FPR = FP / (FP + TN).
- **Precision-recall (PR) curve**: precision against recall; true negatives never appear.
## Why imbalance matters

W: Precision-recall curve: it focuses on how well the rare positive class is found and how reliable the alerts are, and it is not flattered by the large number of true negatives.

D=Accuracy only: with 1% positives, predicting all negatives already scores 99% accuracy, so accuracy is the least informative choice here.
T: For a rare positive class compare models on precision-recall curves; ROC and accuracy are inflated by the many easy negatives.

A baseline is the score of the simplest reasonable approach. It tells you whether a model has learned anything useful.
- Classification: always predict the majority class, or use a simple logistic regression.
- Regression: always predict the mean or median.

## Evaluating properly
- Report the gain over the baseline, not the raw score.
- Use metrics that expose minority-class performance: recall, precision, F1 and the confusion matrix.
- Weigh the gain against the model's added cost and complexity.
W: Compare to a majority-class baseline: always predicting the dominant class already achieves 90%, so 91% is only a 1-point gain and the model's real value is questionable.
X: A=Complex model as baseline: a baseline must be simple so it shows what can be achieved without the model; using the complex model as its own baseline tells you nothing.
B=91% means production-ready: the raw score ignores that 90% comes for free, and accuracy on imbalanced data hides how the minority class is handled.
C=Baselines only for regression: classification needs baselines just as much; majority-class and random predictors are standard classification baselines.
T: Judge a model by how much it beats a trivial baseline; 91% accuracy is weak when always guessing the majority class gives 90%.

G: ## Accuracy on rare-event problems
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all examples. When one class is 99% of the data, true negatives dominate the numerator.
Example: 10,000 patients, 100 with the disease. A "model" that always predicts "no disease":
- TN = 9,900, TP = 0, so accuracy = 99%.
- Recall = 0 / 100 = 0%: it finds no one.
So a reported 99% accuracy is fully consistent with a useless model.
## What to check instead
- Recall on the disease class (how many cases are found).
- Precision (how many alerts are real).

G: ## Per-class metrics in a multi-class confusion matrix
In a confusion matrix, rows are usually actual classes and columns are predicted classes.
For class A, treat A as "positive" and everything else as "negative" (one-vs-rest):
- **Precision for A** = correct A predictions / all predictions of A (the column total for A).
- **Recall for A** = correct A predictions / all actual A instances (the row total for A).
- **Accuracy** = all correct predictions (the diagonal) / all instances.
Example: the model predicts A 50 times; 40 are really A, 6 are B and 4 are C. Precision for A = 40 / 50 = 80%. If there are 60 actual A instances in total, recall for A = 40 / 60, about 67%.
Macro-averaging then averages the per-class values across A, B and C.
W: Correct A predictions divided by all predicted as A: that is TP / (TP + FP) for class A, the definition of precision: of everything labeled A, how much truly is A.
X: A=Divided by all actual A: that denominator is the row total, which gives recall for class A, not precision.
B=B or C predicted as A: this is just the count of false positives for A, one part of the precision denominator rather than a ratio.
C=All correct over all instances: this is overall accuracy across the three classes, not a per-class metric.
T: Precision divides by what was predicted as the class; recall divides by what actually belongs to the class.

G: ## SageMaker inference options by traffic pattern
- **Real-time endpoints**: always-on instances, low latency, billed per instance-hour even when idle.
- **Serverless Inference**: AWS provisions compute per request and scales to zero; you pay for compute time and data processed, not idle time. Trade-offs: possible cold starts, a payload limit of about 4 MB, a 60-second timeout, memory up to 6 GB and no GPUs.
- **Asynchronous endpoints**: queued requests with payloads up to 1 GB in S3; can scale to zero, but only if the minimum instance count is set to 0.
- **Batch Transform**: offline jobs over whole datasets.
Example: a model called a few times an hour with 2 MB requests fits serverless: the bill is only for seconds of compute, and an occasional 2-second cold start is acceptable.
W: SageMaker Serverless Inference: it scales to zero between the sporadic requests so there is no idle cost, the 2 MB payload fits its limits, and occasional cold starts are acceptable.

C=Batch Transform per request: each job provisions instances and takes minutes to start, which suits large offline datasets, not individual requests that need answers within seconds.

G: ## Matching request size and duration to an endpoint type
- **Real-time endpoints**: synchronous; the caller waits for the response, request payloads are capped in the megabyte range and invocations are meant to return within about a minute.
- **Serverless Inference**: synchronous, payload up to about 4 MB and a 60-second timeout.
- **Asynchronous inference**: you place the input in Amazon S3 and call `InvokeEndpointAsync`. Requests go into an internal queue, payloads can be up to 1 GB, and processing can take up to about an hour per request. Results are written to S3 and success or error notifications can be sent through Amazon SNS. The endpoint can also scale to zero when the queue is empty.
- **Batch Transform**: runs an offline job over a whole dataset, not a service that receives individual requests over time.
Example: a user uploads a 700 MB video for analysis; the app gets an inference ID immediately and is notified when the result lands in S3 15 minutes later.
W: Asynchronous inference endpoint: it queues individual requests, accepts payloads up to 1 GB through S3, allows processing times well beyond 10 minutes and delivers results reliably as capacity frees up.
X: A=Serverless endpoint: serverless payloads are capped at about 4 MB and invocations at 60 seconds, far below 800 MB and 10 minutes.
B=Batch Transform: it processes a stored dataset as a scheduled job; it has no request queue for individual requests arriving over time.
C=Real-time endpoint: real-time invocations are synchronous, with payload limits in the megabyte range and responses expected within about a minute, so 800 MB requests running over 10 minutes cannot be served.
T: Large payloads and long processing for individual requests call for asynchronous inference; real-time and serverless are for small, fast, synchronous calls.
