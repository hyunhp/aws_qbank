G: ## Watching spend without watching dashboards
AWS offers several cost tools, and they answer different questions:
- **Cost Explorer** shows historical and forecast spend, but someone has to look at it.
- **AWS Budgets** and **CloudWatch billing alarms** fire when spend crosses a threshold you set in advance.
- **AWS Cost Anomaly Detection** learns the normal spending pattern of each monitored dimension (per service, per linked account, per cost category or cost allocation tag) and flags spend that deviates from it.
## How anomaly detection works
You create a **cost monitor** (for example "AWS services" or "linked accounts") and an **alert subscription** that sends individual alerts through Amazon SNS or daily or weekly summaries by email. Each anomaly comes with a root-cause breakdown, for example "Amazon EC2 in account `111122223333`, usage type `BoxUsage:m5.4xlarge`, us-east-1".
Example: a team normally spends about $800 a day on EC2. A forgotten load test pushes it to $2,400; the monitor raises an anomaly naming the account and service, with no fixed threshold that someone had to guess.
W: Cost Anomaly Detection: it uses machine learning to detect unusual spend automatically, identifies the service and linked account behind the spike through root-cause analysis, and sends proactive alerts by email or SNS, which covers both the investigation and the future-alerting need.
X: A=Trusted Advisor cost checks: these look for optimization opportunities such as idle load balancers or underused instances. They do not learn a spending baseline, detect a sudden spike, or explain which account caused it.
C=Fixed-threshold billing alarm: the alarm fires only when estimated charges exceed a static number. It says nothing about which service or account caused the increase, and a threshold high enough to avoid false alarms misses smaller but abnormal spikes.
D=Manual daily review: this is exactly the reactive work leadership wants to stop. It depends on someone noticing the pattern and gives no automatic alert or root cause.
T: Thresholds and dashboards tell you that spend is high; Cost Anomaly Detection learns what normal looks like, alerts on deviations and names the service and account responsible.

@ Q001074 explain
G: ## What consolidated billing does
In AWS Organizations, the management account pays one bill for all member (linked) accounts. Besides the single invoice, consolidated billing treats the organization as one customer for pricing:
- **Volume tiers are combined.** S3 storage or data transfer from all accounts adds up, so the organization reaches cheaper tiers sooner.
- **Reserved Instance and Savings Plans discounts are shared.** If one account buys a Savings Plan or RI and does not fully use it, the unused benefit is applied to matching usage in other accounts of the organization.
## Controlling the sharing
Discount sharing is **on by default**. The management account can turn it off for specific accounts in the billing preferences, which keeps those accounts' purchases to themselves and stops them receiving others' discounts.
Visibility is separate: member accounts see their own charges, while the management account sees all accounts, for example in Cost Explorer.
Example: account A buys a 3-year Compute Savings Plan of $10/hour but only uses $7/hour; the remaining $3/hour of commitment covers eligible compute in account B.
W: Consolidated billing sharing: consolidated billing combines usage for volume pricing and, unless the management account turns sharing off, automatically applies RI and Savings Plans discounts across all linked accounts in the organization.
X: A=Disabled and cannot be enabled: the opposite is true. Sharing is enabled by default and can be turned off or back on per account from the management account's billing preferences.
C=Each account buys its own RIs: accounts can buy their own, but they do not have to. A purchase in one account benefits matching usage in other accounts through sharing, which is the main reason to centralize commitments.
D=Only the management account benefits: discounts apply to usage in any account in the organization, not just the management account. The management account usually has little usage of its own.
T: In an organization, consolidated billing shares volume tiers and RI/Savings Plans discounts across accounts by default; sharing is something you switch off, not something you have to build.

@ Q001075 explain
G: ## Why Spot Instances get interrupted
Spot Instances use spare EC2 capacity at a large discount. When EC2 needs that capacity back in a particular instance pool (one instance type in one Availability Zone), it reclaims Spot Instances from that pool. Interruptions are driven mainly by capacity, not by price.
## How to live with interruptions
- **Handle the notice.** EC2 sends a **Spot Instance interruption notice** two minutes before reclaiming an instance, through instance metadata (`spot/instance-action`) and an Amazon EventBridge event. The job can checkpoint its progress or drain work. A **rebalance recommendation** can arrive even earlier when an instance is at elevated risk.
- **Diversify.** Allow many instance types and sizes across several AZs, so the fleet is not tied to one pool.
- **Choose a capacity-aware allocation strategy.** `capacity-optimized` or `price-capacity-optimized` launch instances from the pools with the most spare capacity, which are the least likely to be reclaimed.
Example: an EC2 Fleet with 12 instance types across 3 AZs, `price-capacity-optimized`, and a handler that saves a checkpoint to S3 when the notice arrives.
W: Notice handling plus diversification: reacting to the two-minute notice lets jobs checkpoint before termination, while diversifying types and AZs with capacity-optimized allocation places instances in deep pools that are interrupted less often, so the team keeps Spot savings and loses less work.
X: A=Switch everything to On-Demand: interruptions can be reduced substantially with diversification and capacity-aware allocation, and moving the whole cluster to On-Demand throws away the savings the team is required to keep.
B=Disable interruption notices: the notice is the only warning the application gets. Ignoring it does not stop EC2 from reclaiming the instance; it just guarantees the job loses its progress.
D=Raise the maximum price: Spot interruptions are caused mostly by EC2 needing capacity back, not by the price rising above your maximum. A higher maximum price does not prevent reclamation.
T: Spot interruptions are a capacity event you design around: catch the two-minute notice, diversify pools and use capacity-optimized allocation instead of fighting with price or abandoning Spot.

@ Q001076 explain
G: ## Zonal versus Regional Reserved Instances
A Reserved Instance is a billing discount that matches running instances by attributes such as instance family, size, platform, tenancy and location.
- A **Zonal RI** is tied to one Availability Zone and one exact instance size. It also reserves capacity in that AZ, but its discount applies only to matching instances in that AZ.
- A **Regional RI** applies to matching usage in any AZ of the Region. For Linux/Unix instances with default tenancy it is also **size-flexible** within the family, using normalization factors (for example one `m5.xlarge` reservation covers two `m5.large` instances).
## Changing an existing RI
Standard RIs can be **modified**, for example changing the scope from Zonal to Regional, at no cost. Convertible RIs can also be exchanged for other configurations.
If a capacity guarantee is still needed after moving to Regional scope, **On-Demand Capacity Reservations** can be added separately in the AZs that need it.
Example: a workload spread over `us-east-1a`, `1b` and `1c` gets the discount from a Regional RI wherever the Auto Scaling group places the instances.
W: Regional RIs: changing the scope to Regional lets the discount follow instances into any AZ of the Region and, for eligible instances, across sizes in the same family, which matches an Auto Scaling group that spreads instances over three AZs.
X: A=RIs cannot be changed: Standard RIs can be modified (including Zonal to Regional scope), Convertible RIs can be exchanged, and eligible Standard RIs can be listed on the Reserved Instance Marketplace.
B=Cancel and go On-Demand: RIs are a commitment for their term and cannot simply be cancelled for a refund. Moving to On-Demand loses the discount while the commitment continues to be billed.
D=Buy more Zonal RIs: this adds commitment instead of fixing the mismatch, and the discount still breaks whenever the Auto Scaling group shifts instances between AZs or sizes.
T: Zonal RIs lock the discount to one AZ and size; Regional RIs let it float across AZs (and sizes for eligible instances), which is what a multi-AZ Auto Scaling group needs.

G: ## Deploying the same stack to many accounts
AWS CloudFormation **StackSets** deploy one template as stack instances into many accounts and Regions from a single place (the management account or a delegated administrator).
StackSets has two permission models:
- **Self-managed**: you create the administration and execution IAM roles in each target account yourself.
- **Service-managed**: StackSets uses trusted access with AWS Organizations and creates the needed roles for you. You target **organizational units (OUs)** instead of listing account IDs.
## Automatic deployment
With service-managed permissions you can turn on **automatic deployment**: when an account is added to a targeted OU, StackSets deploys the stack there, and when it leaves the OU the stack can be removed or retained.
Updating the template in the stack set rolls the change out to every stack instance, with controls such as maximum concurrent accounts and failure tolerance.
Example: a stack set with a baseline VPC, a CloudTrail trail and guardrail IAM roles targets the `Workloads` OU; a new account moved into that OU gets the baseline within minutes.
W: StackSets with service-managed permissions: targeting OUs with automatic deployment covers both current and future accounts, and one update to the stack set rolls out centrally to all of them without per-account role setup.
X: A=Config rules with auto-remediation: Config detects and fixes non-compliant configuration, but it is not a way to provision and version a baseline of VPCs, logging and IAM resources in every account.
B=Manual stack per account: deploying by hand in 40 accounts is slow and error-prone, does not reach new accounts automatically, and makes central updates a 40-step job.
C=Custom Lambda on account creation: it could work, but it rebuilds what StackSets already provides (cross-account roles, controlled rollout, central updates, drift detection) as custom code that the team must maintain, and it does not handle accounts moving between OUs.
T: For the same resources in every current and future account of an OU, use StackSets with service-managed permissions and automatic deployment rather than per-account or custom provisioning.

@ Q001078 explain
G: ## Cross-account CloudFormation deployments
A common pattern keeps CI/CD in a **tooling account** and deploys to separate workload accounts. Two IAM roles in the production account do the work:
- A **cross-account role** that the pipeline assumes. Its trust policy allows the tooling account's pipeline role, and its permissions are narrow: pass a specific role to CloudFormation, create or update specific stacks, and read the artifact bucket (plus `kms:Decrypt` on the artifact key).
- A **CloudFormation service role** that CloudFormation itself uses to create the stack's resources. Only CloudFormation can assume it, and it holds the permissions the template needs.
## Why two roles
The pipeline never gets the power to create resources directly; it can only ask CloudFormation to deploy with the approved service role (`iam:PassRole` limited to that role ARN).
Example: `arn:aws:iam::222233334444:role/PipelineDeployRole` may call `cloudformation:UpdateStack` on `app-prod` and pass `CfnExecRole`, which holds the EC2, ECS and RDS permissions.
W: Scoped cross-account role plus service role: the pipeline assumes a narrowly scoped role in production that can only drive CloudFormation, and the resource permissions live in a CloudFormation service role, so the pipeline itself never holds broad IAM rights in production.
X: A=AdministratorAccess: this is the broad permission the requirement forbids. A compromised pipeline or a bad commit could change anything in production.
C=Root credentials in Secrets Manager: root credentials cannot be scoped at all, and AWS recommends not using the root user for everyday tasks or automation. Storing them for a pipeline is the widest possible access.
D=Move the pipeline into production: it does not reduce the permissions the pipeline needs, and it mixes build tooling into the production account, undoing the account separation.
T: Cross-account deployments should use a narrow assumable role that only drives CloudFormation, with resource permissions held by a CloudFormation service role, not admin rights for the pipeline.

G: ## Running Kafka yourself versus managed Kafka
A self-managed Apache Kafka cluster on EC2 means the team handles broker patching, version upgrades, replacing failed brokers, storage growth and ZooKeeper or KRaft controllers.
**Amazon MSK** (Managed Streaming for Apache Kafka) runs open-source Apache Kafka for you:
- AWS provisions brokers across AZs, detects and replaces failed brokers, and applies patches.
- Clients use the same Kafka APIs and protocols, so producers, consumers, Kafka Connect and Kafka Streams applications usually need only new bootstrap broker strings and authentication settings.
- **MSK Serverless** removes capacity management entirely, and MSK Connect runs Kafka Connect connectors as a managed service.
Example: producers that point at `b-1.mycluster.xxxx.kafka.us-east-1.amazonaws.com:9098` with IAM authentication instead of self-managed brokers, with the same topic and consumer-group design.
W: Amazon MSK: it keeps the Kafka API while moving broker patching, failure recovery and multi-AZ operation to AWS, which is exactly the operational burden leadership wants removed.
X: A=Add an Auto Scaling group: an ASG replaces instances but does not know about Kafka partitions, replication or broker IDs. The team still patches brokers and handles failover, so the toil stays.
C=Kinesis Data Streams with rewrites: Kinesis is managed, but it has its own API, so all producers and consumers must be rewritten, which breaks the requirement to keep the Kafka API.
D=SQS standard queues: SQS is a message queue, not a log-based stream. It has no partitions, consumer groups or replay of retained records and does not speak the Kafka protocol.
T: To cut Kafka operations while keeping Kafka clients unchanged, move to Amazon MSK; Kinesis or SQS would force a rewrite.

G: ## Extending CloudFormation to things it does not know
CloudFormation manages AWS resources through built-in resource types. For anything else, such as a third-party SaaS object, you need a way to run your own logic during the stack lifecycle.
A **custom resource** (`AWS::CloudFormation::CustomResource` or `Custom::MyThing`) does this. You give it a `ServiceToken`, usually a Lambda function ARN. On stack **Create**, **Update** and **Delete**, CloudFormation invokes the function with the request type and properties. The function calls the SaaS API and then reports `SUCCESS` or `FAILED` (with any output data) to a pre-signed S3 URL, and CloudFormation continues or rolls back.
Example: `Custom::DatadogMonitor` with properties `Name` and `Query`; on Delete the function removes the monitor so the stack cleans up after itself.
For widely reused integrations, a registered **CloudFormation extension (resource type)** built with the CloudFormation CLI is another option.
W: Lambda-backed custom resource: CloudFormation invokes the function on create, update and delete, so the SaaS provisioning runs inside the stack lifecycle, its outputs can feed other resources, and a failure rolls the stack back.
X: A=Macro: a macro transforms the template text before deployment (for example, expanding shorthand). It runs at processing time, not on create, update or delete, so it cannot provision or clean up an external resource.
C=Config rule with remediation: Config evaluates the compliance of existing AWS resources. It is not tied to stack operations and does not create third-party SaaS objects.
D=Manual Automation runbook: running a document by hand after the stack is created is outside the lifecycle, so updates and deletes are not handled and the stack cannot roll back on failure.
T: To run custom provisioning inside CloudFormation's create, update and delete lifecycle, use a Lambda-backed custom resource; macros only rewrite templates.

@ Q001081 explain
G: ## Why builds repeat work
Each CodeBuild build runs in a fresh container, so by default every build downloads all dependencies again, such as `node_modules`, Maven `.m2` artifacts, pip wheels or Docker base layers.
## CodeBuild caching
- **Amazon S3 cache**: paths listed under `cache: paths:` in the `buildspec.yml` are uploaded to an S3 bucket at the end of a build and restored at the start of the next one. It works across build hosts.
- **Local cache**: stored on the build host and reused only while the same host is available. Modes are **source cache** (Git metadata), **Docker layer cache** and **custom cache** (directories you choose).
Example buildspec fragment: `cache: paths: - '/root/.m2/**/*'`. A build that spent 25 minutes resolving Maven dependencies may finish in a few minutes when the cache is warm, because only changed dependencies are fetched.
W: CodeBuild caching: persisting dependency directories in an S3 or local cache means each build restores them instead of downloading everything again, which directly removes the step that makes the build slow.
X: A=Persistent EC2 build server: it could keep dependencies around, but it gives up CodeBuild's managed, clean and scalable build environments and adds a server to patch and scale, while CodeBuild caching gets the same benefit.
C=More parallel stages: parallel stages help when there are independent jobs. Each stage would still reinstall all dependencies, so the slow step remains and may run several times.
D=Largest compute type: dependency download time is dominated by network and repository fetches, not CPU. A bigger instance costs more per minute and barely shortens the reinstall.
T: When builds are slow because they fetch the same dependencies each time, cache them in CodeBuild instead of adding compute or parallelism.

G: ## Injecting secrets into ECS tasks
An ECS task definition can reference a secret in the `secrets` section of a container definition, using the ARN of an AWS Secrets Manager secret or a Systems Manager Parameter Store parameter. The ECS agent fetches the value when the container starts and exposes it as an environment variable, so the image and task definition never contain the password.
## Why Secrets Manager fits rotation
- **Rotation**: Secrets Manager rotates on a schedule (for example every 30 days) using a rotation Lambda function; for Amazon RDS it provides ready-made rotation functions or managed rotation that change the password on the database and in the secret together, using staging labels such as `AWSCURRENT`.
- **Access control**: a resource policy on the secret and IAM permissions (`secretsmanager:GetSecretValue`) can restrict reading to the roles of this one service (ECS fetches the value with the task execution role).
- **Audit**: every `GetSecretValue` call is logged in CloudTrail.
The task definition never changes: new tasks read the current value at start, while running tasks keep theirs, so use alternating-users rotation or have the app re-read the secret.
W: Rotating Secrets Manager secret: the task definition references the secret ARN, so no redeploy of the definition is needed when the password rotates every 30 days; built-in RDS rotation, IAM/resource-policy restriction to one role and CloudTrail logging of each read cover the remaining requirements.
X: B=Plain environment variable: the password sits in clear text in the task definition, visible to anyone who can describe it, and changing it means registering a new revision and redeploying.
C=Parameter Store with a custom Lambda: Standard parameters have no built-in rotation, so the team must write and maintain code that changes the database password and the parameter in step, which Secrets Manager already provides for RDS.
D=SSE-S3 object: SSE-S3 keys are managed by S3, so encryption adds no access control beyond the bucket permissions. There is no rotation mechanism, and the container needs custom download code.
T: For database credentials that must rotate, stay out of task definitions and be read only by one role, reference a Secrets Manager secret with rotation from the ECS task definition.

@ Q001083 explain
G: ## How UPDATE_ROLLBACK_FAILED happens
When a stack update fails, CloudFormation rolls the resources back to their previous state. If the rollback itself cannot complete, the stack stops in `UPDATE_ROLLBACK_FAILED`. Typical causes: a resource was changed or deleted outside CloudFormation, a dependency (such as a security group) is still in use, or permissions or limits changed.
In this state you cannot update the stack until the rollback is finished.
## Continuing the rollback
The **ContinueUpdateRollback** action (console: "Continue update rollback", CLI: `aws cloudformation continue-update-rollback`) retries the rollback. First fix the underlying cause if you can. For resources that still cannot roll back, pass them in `--resources-to-skip`; CloudFormation marks them as rolled back so the stack can reach `UPDATE_ROLLBACK_COMPLETE`.
Example: `aws cloudformation continue-update-rollback --stack-name app --resources-to-skip MyDBParameterGroup`. Afterwards you make the skipped resource consistent with the template, often through a follow-up update.
W: ContinueUpdateRollback with skipped resources: it is the supported way to finish a failed rollback, optionally skipping the resources that cannot roll back, and it returns the stack to a state where normal updates work again.
X: A=Edit resources, then retry the update: the stack cannot be updated while it is in `UPDATE_ROLLBACK_FAILED`. The rollback has to be completed first; fixing resources by hand only helps as a step before continuing the rollback.
B=Wait for an automatic retry: CloudFormation does not retry a failed rollback on its own. The stack stays in this state until someone continues the rollback or deletes the stack.
D=Delete and recreate everything: deletion is a heavy, risky last resort that can destroy data and cause downtime. It is unnecessary when the rollback can be continued.
T: A stack in `UPDATE_ROLLBACK_FAILED` is recovered with ContinueUpdateRollback (skipping stuck resources if needed), not by retrying the update, waiting, or deleting the stack.

G: ## Blue/green deployments for ECS with CodeDeploy
With the CodeDeploy deployment controller, an ECS service has two **task sets** behind an Application Load Balancer: the current (blue) set and the new (green) set. CodeDeploy starts the green tasks, then moves listener traffic from blue to green using a deployment configuration:
- **All-at-once**: everything moves in one step.
- **Canary**: for example `ECSCanary10Percent5Minutes`, 10% first, the rest after 5 minutes.
- **Linear**: for example `ECSLinear10PercentEvery1Minutes`, 10% more every minute.
## Automatic rollback
The deployment group can list **CloudWatch alarms** (such as 5xx rate or latency). If an alarm goes into ALARM during the deployment, CodeDeploy stops it and shifts traffic back to the blue task set. Lifecycle hooks can also run validation Lambda functions between steps.
## ECS's own deployment options
The native ECS deployment controller now also offers blue/green, canary and linear strategies. A **rolling update** replaces tasks in batches and can roll back through the deployment circuit breaker or CloudWatch alarms, but it does not shift traffic by percentage to a separate new task set.
W: CodeDeploy blue/green for ECS: it provides gradual (linear or canary) traffic shifting between task sets and automatic rollback on CloudWatch alarms as configuration, so no custom scripting is needed.
X: A=New cluster and manual DNS switch: it is manual, DNS changes take effect unevenly because of caching, and there is no alarm-driven automatic rollback.
C=Adjust desired count and watch logs: this is a manual process with no controlled traffic percentages and no automatic rollback, the opposite of minimal scripting.
D=Plain rolling update: a rolling update replaces tasks in batches within one service and can roll back on alarms, but traffic is not shifted by percentage between a separate old and new task set, so it does not provide the gradual, controlled shift this design asks for.
T: For gradual traffic shifting between ECS task sets with alarm-based rollback and little scripting, use CodeDeploy blue/green with a linear or canary configuration.

G: ## Self-service without broad permissions
**AWS Service Catalog** lets a central team publish approved products for others to launch:
- A **product** is a CloudFormation template (or Terraform configuration) with versions, such as "three-tier web app".

D=Read-only access plus tickets: it avoids broad permissions but removes self-service; every environment waits on a manual ticket.
T: Service Catalog gives self-service provisioning of approved templates while a launch role, not the developer, holds the resource permissions.

@ Q001087 explain
G: ## RPO, RTO and replication lag
- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much data, measured in time, you can afford to lose. A 5-minute RPO means at most the last 5 minutes of writes may be gone.
- **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long recovery may take.
With **asynchronous replication**, the primary confirms a write before the replica has it. The replica is always behind by the **replication lag**. If the primary Region fails, everything inside that lag is lost, so the real RPO equals the lag at the moment of failure.
## Meeting a tight RPO
- Use replication built for low lag, such as **Aurora Global Database**, which uses storage-level replication with typical lag under a second.
- **Monitor lag** (for example the `AuroraGlobalDBReplicationLag` metric) with CloudWatch alarms, so drift above the target is noticed before a disaster.
Example: a logical replication job that fell 45 minutes behind during a batch load means 45 minutes of lost transactions when the failover happens.
W: Replication lag exceeded the RPO: losing 45 minutes of data is a data-currency problem, so the fix is lower-lag replication such as Aurora Global Database, plus alarms on replication lag to prove the 5-minute RPO is being met.
X: B=Slow Auto Scaling: scaling speed affects how quickly the DR Region can serve traffic (RTO and performance), not how many transactions were missing from the replica.
C=RTO and instance size: RTO is about time to recover, not data loss, and a larger standby instance does not recover writes that never reached it.
D=Long health check interval: a slower health check delays failover, but writes that reach a failed primary fail rather than being quietly accepted; it would not explain 45 minutes of committed transactions missing from the replica.
E=Log bucket replication: application logs are not the transactional data; missing log files would not cause 45 minutes of lost database transactions.
T: Data lost in a failover equals the asynchronous replication lag at that moment, so a tight RPO needs low-lag replication and lag monitoring, not faster scaling or DNS.

G: ## The four DR strategies, from cheapest to fastest
- **Backup and restore**: data is backed up to another Region; infrastructure is rebuilt after a disaster. RPO and RTO in hours.
- **Pilot light**: core data is replicated live, but compute is off or minimal and is started on failover. Minutes to hours.
- **Warm standby**: a scaled-down but fully working copy runs in the second Region and is scaled up on failover. Minutes.
- **Multi-site active-active**: full production capacity runs in two or more Regions and serves traffic all the time. RTO near zero, and RPO near zero with synchronous-like or very low-lag replication.
## What active-active looks like
Route 53 latency-based (or geolocation) routing with health checks spreads users across Regions; data is replicated with services such as DynamoDB global tables or Aurora Global Database. If one Region fails, health checks remove it and the other Region, already at full size, absorbs the load.
The price is double infrastructure plus the complexity of multi-Region writes and consistency.
W: Multi-site active-active: both Regions already run full production and take live traffic, and near-real-time replication keeps data current, so failover is nearly instant with near-zero data loss, which is what the trading platform needs and has agreed to pay for.
X: A=Pilot light with a manual runbook: compute must be started and scaled, and manual steps add more time, so RTO is tens of minutes or more, not near zero.
C=Warm standby: it is faster than pilot light, but the standby must still scale out to full capacity and traffic must be moved, so it cannot promise near-zero downtime.
D=Hourly backups: restoring from backups takes hours and loses up to an hour of trades, far from near-zero RTO and RPO.
T: Near-zero RTO and RPO, with budget for full capacity in two Regions, means multi-site active-active; every cheaper pattern trades some downtime or data loss for cost.

G: ## Matching DR cost to RTO and RPO
A generous target such as an **8-hour RTO** and **24-hour RPO** means you can rebuild the environment after a disaster and lose up to a day of data. The cheapest strategy that meets it is **backup and restore**.
## A repeatable backup-and-restore design
- **AWS Backup** backs up RDS and EC2 (and other resources) on a schedule and uses **cross-Region copy** to put recovery points in the DR Region.
- **S3 Cross-Region Replication** (or AWS Backup for S3) keeps the report files in the DR Region.
- **Infrastructure as code** (CloudFormation, CDK or Terraform) rebuilds the VPC, instances and database from the copies on demand, so recovery is the same every time and can be tested.
Standing cost in the DR Region is mostly backup storage; there is no running compute or database there.
Example: daily RDS backups copied to `us-west-2`, and a tested stack that restores the latest snapshot and brings the app up in about two hours.
W: Backup and restore: daily cross-Region backups meet a 24-hour RPO, IaC-driven rebuild fits within 8 hours and is repeatable, and nothing runs in the DR Region, which meets the near-zero standing cost budget.
X: A=Pilot light: a continuously running RDS replica in the DR Region is standing cost the budget does not allow, and it buys an RPO far tighter than the 24 hours required.
B=Warm standby: a scaled-down copy of the full stack runs all the time, adding compute and database cost for an RTO much shorter than needed.
C=Multi-site active-active: full capacity in two Regions is the most expensive option and is designed for near-zero RTO, far beyond the needs of an internal reporting app.
T: With hours of RTO and a day of RPO allowed and almost no standing budget, choose backup and restore with cross-Region copies and IaC for repeatable rebuilds.

G: ## Route 53 routing policies used here
- **Latency-based routing** answers with the Region that has the lowest measured network latency for the user's resolver location.
- **Geolocation routing** answers based on where the user is (continent, country or US state). It is the policy to use for compliance or data residency.
- **Health checks** on records make Route 53 stop returning an endpoint whose health check fails.
## Combining policies
Route 53 lets a record point to another set of records (alias to a record name), so policies can be nested:
- A geolocation record for **Europe** returns the `eu-west-1` endpoint.
- A geolocation **Default** record is an alias to a set of **latency records** for `us-east-1` and `ap-northeast-1` (and optionally `eu-west-1`), each with a health check.
Example: a user in Germany always gets `eu-west-1`; a user in Brazil gets whichever of the other Regions is fastest and healthy.
W: Geolocation for Europe plus latency with health checks: geolocation guarantees EU users are answered with the EU Region for compliance, while latency records with health checks give everyone else the fastest healthy Region.
X: B=Weighted routing: weights split traffic by fixed percentages and ignore where the user is, so users are not sent to the fastest Region and EU users could be sent outside the EU.
C=Geoproximity with bias: geoproximity routes by distance adjusted with bias, so near a boundary some EU users can still be routed elsewhere; "usually" in the EU does not meet a compliance requirement.
D=Latency for all users: latency depends on network conditions, so an EU user could be answered with a non-EU Region, and nothing enforces the EU residency rule.
T: Use geolocation where location is a legal requirement and latency-based records with health checks for performance everywhere else, nesting the two in Route 53.

G: ## Multi-Region writes in AWS databases
Most multi-Region database options have **one writer Region**; the others are read-only until a failover or promotion. **Amazon DynamoDB global tables** are different: every replica Region accepts reads and writes.
## How global tables work
- You add replica Regions to a table; DynamoDB replicates changes between them, typically within a second, in the default multi-Region eventual consistency mode.
- If the same item is written in two Regions at nearly the same time, a **last-writer-wins** rule resolves the conflict automatically.
- If a Region fails, applications simply keep writing to the other Regions; there is nothing to promote.
- An optional **multi-Region strong consistency** mode gives strongly consistent reads across Regions at the cost of higher write latency; there, conflicting concurrent writes are rejected instead of resolved by last writer wins.
Example: an order service in `us-east-1`, `eu-west-1` and `ap-southeast-1` writes shopping carts locally in each Region; if `eu-west-1` is unavailable, European traffic is directed to another Region and continues to read and write.
W: DynamoDB global tables: they are multi-active, so every Region can take writes, changes replicate between Regions with automatic conflict resolution, and losing one Region does not stop writes elsewhere.
X: B=Aurora Global Database: it has one primary (writer) Region; secondary Regions are read-only until a failover, so it is not active-active writing. Write forwarding still sends writes to the single primary.
C=ElastiCache Global Datastore: it replicates a cache from one primary cluster to read-only secondary clusters, and a cache is not the system of record for orders.
D=RDS Multi-AZ single primary: it protects against an AZ failure within one Region and has one writer, so it gives neither multi-Region writes nor Region-level continuity.
T: For writes accepted in every Region with automatic conflict resolution, use DynamoDB global tables; Aurora Global Database and similar options have a single writer Region.

G: ## Two kinds of RDS Multi-AZ
- **Multi-AZ DB instance deployment**: one primary plus one standby in another AZ with synchronous replication. The standby is **not readable**; it only exists for failover, which usually takes one to two minutes.
- **Multi-AZ DB cluster deployment** (RDS for MySQL and PostgreSQL): one writer plus **two readable standby instances** in three AZs. Failover is typically under about 35 seconds, and the cluster has a **writer endpoint** and a **reader endpoint**.
## Endpoints
The application keeps using the writer (cluster) endpoint; after failover it points to the new writer automatically. Reporting tools connect to the reader endpoint, which spreads read-only connections over the two standbys, so the standby capacity already paid for does useful work.
Example: `mydb.cluster-xxxx.us-east-1.rds.amazonaws.com` for the app, `mydb.cluster-ro-xxxx.us-east-1.rds.amazonaws.com` for reports.
W: Multi-AZ DB cluster: it fails over automatically within about a minute, the application keeps the same writer endpoint, and the two readable standbys behind the reader endpoint serve reporting queries using capacity that is already provisioned.
X: A=Single-AZ with manual promotion: promotion is manual, slower than a minute in practice, and the promoted replica gets a different endpoint, so the application must be changed.
B=Cross-Region replica: RDS does not promote a cross-Region replica automatically for an AZ failure, promotion creates a separate standalone instance with a new endpoint, and asynchronous replication can lose recent writes.
C=Multi-AZ DB instance with reads on the standby: the standby of a Multi-AZ DB instance deployment cannot accept connections for reads, so reporting cannot use it.
T: Need automatic failover in under a minute plus reads from the standbys? Choose a Multi-AZ DB cluster; a Multi-AZ DB instance's standby is not readable.

G: ## S3 replication
S3 replication copies objects automatically and asynchronously from a source bucket to one or more destination buckets. It requires versioning on both buckets and an IAM role that S3 uses to copy objects.
- **Cross-Region Replication (CRR)**: destination bucket in a different Region, used for compliance, lower-latency access or disaster recovery.
- **Same-Region Replication (SRR)**: destination in the same Region, used for log aggregation or copies between accounts.
- Rules can filter by prefix or tag, change the storage class or owner of replicas, and **S3 Replication Time Control** adds a replication SLA (99.99% of objects within 15 minutes) with metrics.
- Replication applies to new objects after the rule is created; **S3 Batch Replication** copies existing objects.
Example: a rule copies `patient-records/` from `us-east-1` to a bucket in `us-west-2` in the `S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval` storage class.
W: S3 Cross-Region Replication: it copies new objects automatically and asynchronously to a bucket in another Region, meeting the requirement for a separate-Region copy without scripts.
X: B=Same-Region Replication only: the copies stay in the same Region, so a Regional event affects both, which fails the separate-Region requirement.
C=Nightly CLI sync: it is manual or scripted rather than built-in replication, leaves up to a day of new objects uncopied, and depends on the job running successfully.
D=Transfer Acceleration: it speeds up uploads and downloads over long distances through edge locations; it does not copy objects to another bucket or Region.
T: For automatic copies of new S3 objects in another Region, use Cross-Region Replication; SRR stays in-Region and Transfer Acceleration only speeds up transfers.

G: ## What a Route 53 health check actually tests
Route 53 health checks can check an endpoint in different ways:
- **TCP**: can a TCP connection be opened on the port? A running instance with a listening process passes, even if the application returns errors.
- **HTTP/HTTPS**: Route 53 requests a path such as `/health` and treats 2xx and 3xx status codes as healthy.
- **HTTP/HTTPS with string matching**: the response body must also contain a given string.
- Health checks can also watch a CloudWatch alarm or combine other health checks (calculated).
## Failover routing
With failover routing, Route 53 returns the primary record while its health check is healthy and switches to the secondary automatically when it is unhealthy. The switch is only as good as the health check.
Example: an app whose database connection is broken still accepts TCP on port 443, but `GET /health` returns 500; only an HTTPS health check on `/health` notices.
W: TCP instead of application check: a TCP check passes as long as the port accepts connections, so an application that is broken at the HTTP layer still looks healthy and failover never triggers; an HTTP(S) check against a meaningful health endpoint fixes it.
X: A=No CloudWatch alarms on the ASG: Route 53 failover uses its own health checks, not Auto Scaling alarms. Missing ASG alarms would not stop Route 53 from failing over.
B=Low TTL on the secondary: a low TTL makes clients pick up DNS changes faster; resolvers do not ignore records because their TTL is low.
C=Manual update needed: failover routing switches to the secondary record automatically when the primary's health check becomes unhealthy.
T: Route 53 fails over only when its health check fails, so check the application with HTTP(S) and a real health path, not just TCP connectivity.

G: ## Giving another AWS account access safely
Cross-account access in AWS normally uses an **IAM role** instead of users and keys:
- The company creates a role with a **trust policy** that allows the vendor's account (or a specific role in it) to call `sts:AssumeRole`, usually with an **external ID** condition to prevent the confused-deputy problem.
- The role's **permissions policy** grants only what the audit needs, for example `s3:GetObject` and `s3:ListBucket` on the named buckets and read access to the CloudTrail log bucket.
- The vendor's users assume the role and receive **temporary credentials** that expire, typically after one hour.
Each assume-role call and the vendor's API actions are logged in CloudTrail (S3 object reads need data events enabled), and access can be removed by deleting the role or changing the trust policy.
Example trust principal: `arn:aws:iam::444455556666:root` with condition `sts:ExternalId` = `audit-2026`.
W: Cross-account read-only role: the vendor assumes a role trusted for its account and gets short-lived credentials scoped to the specific buckets and logs, with no IAM users or access keys to manage or leak.
X: A=Share root credentials: root has unrestricted access to the account and cannot be limited, so sharing it gives the vendor full control, the opposite of least privilege.
B=Public buckets with IP restriction: making buckets public for regulated data is risky, source IPs can change or be shared, and it does not identify the vendor as an AWS principal.
D=IAM user with a shared key: this is exactly the long-lived credential the requirement rules out; the key must be distributed, rotated and revoked manually.
T: Third-party AWS accounts should get access by assuming a narrowly scoped cross-account role (with an external ID), never through shared users, keys or root.

@ Q001096 explain
G: ## Security groups versus network ACLs
- A **security group** is attached to network interfaces and is **stateful**: if inbound traffic is allowed, the reply is allowed automatically.
- A **network ACL** is attached to a subnet and is **stateless**: requests and replies are evaluated separately, in rule-number order, in each direction.
## Ephemeral ports
A client picks a random high source port for each connection, and the server replies to that port. Linux clients typically use `32768-60999`; AWS guidance often uses `1024-65535` to cover all clients.
Traffic between two subnets crosses both subnets' NACLs. For app `10.0.1.0/24` to RDS `10.0.2.0/24` on port 3306:
- App subnet NACL: outbound TCP 3306 to `10.0.2.0/24`, inbound TCP `1024-65535` from `10.0.2.0/24`.
- RDS subnet NACL: inbound TCP 3306 from `10.0.1.0/24`, outbound TCP `1024-65535` to `10.0.1.0/24`.
Missing any one of these breaks the connection even though the security groups allow it.
W: Missing ephemeral-port NACL rules: because NACLs are stateless, the reply traffic on high ports needs its own inbound and outbound rules; without them the MySQL connection is blocked although the security groups are correct.
X: A=Missing route between subnets: every VPC route table has a built-in `local` route for the VPC CIDR that cannot be removed, so subnets in the same VPC can always route to each other.
C=Security groups are stateless: security groups are stateful, so replies to allowed traffic are permitted automatically; no extra outbound rule is needed for responses.
D=RDS needs a public IP: traffic inside a VPC uses private IP addresses. A public IP matters only for access from the internet.
T: Stateless NACLs need explicit rules for reply traffic on ephemeral ports in both subnets, while stateful security groups handle replies automatically and the VPC local route always exists.

@ Q001097 explain
G: ## KMS key types for RDS encryption
RDS encrypts storage, automated backups, snapshots and replicas with an AWS KMS key.
- An **AWS managed key** (`aws/rds`) is created and controlled by AWS. You cannot change its key policy, disable it or share it, so snapshots encrypted with it **cannot be shared with another account**.
- A **customer managed key** is created and controlled by you, and adds these controls:
- you can **disable** or schedule deletion of it in an emergency, which blocks use of the data;
- **automatic rotation** can be enabled (yearly by default, with a configurable period);
- every use (`Decrypt`, `GenerateDataKey`, `CreateGrant`) is logged in **CloudTrail**;
- its **key policy** can allow another account, such as a DR account, to use the key.
## Sharing a snapshot with a DR account
Share the encrypted snapshot with the DR account and allow that account in the key policy. The DR account then copies the snapshot, usually re-encrypting it with its own key, and restores from it.
W: Customer managed KMS key: it meets every requirement at once: company control including emergency disable, automatic annual rotation, CloudTrail logs of key usage, and a key policy that lets the DR account use the key to copy and restore shared snapshots.
X: A=AWS managed aws/rds key: the company cannot disable it or edit its policy, and snapshots encrypted with it cannot be shared with other accounts, so the DR requirement fails.
C=TLS plus SSE-S3: TLS protects data in transit, not the database storage at rest, and RDS snapshots are not something you encrypt with SSE-S3; it also gives no company-controlled, disable-able key.
D=Application-level column encryption only: leaving storage encryption off leaves the rest of the database, logs and snapshots unencrypted at rest, and it provides none of the KMS key controls required.
T: When you need to control, disable, rotate, audit and share the RDS encryption key across accounts, use a customer managed KMS key; the AWS managed `aws/rds` key cannot be shared or disabled.
