@ Q003073 explain
G: ## RTO is a number you must measure
The recovery time objective (RTO) is the longest acceptable time between a disruption and the restored service. A written runbook says what to do, but only running it shows how long it really takes and where it breaks.
## Game days and fault injection
- A **game day** is a planned exercise where the team simulates an incident (for example, losing a database primary or a whole Availability Zone) and follows the real recovery procedure while timing each step.
- **Fault injection** deliberately causes controlled failures. AWS Fault Injection Service (FIS) can stop instances, throttle APIs, add network latency or disrupt an AZ, with stop conditions tied to CloudWatch alarms so the experiment halts if impact grows too large.
- Example: a team injects a primary database failure in staging, measures 22 minutes to recover against a 15-minute RTO, then automates the DNS switch and re-tests until it passes.
W: Game days and fault injection: they exercise the actual procedures under controlled conditions and produce measured recovery times, so gaps against the RTO are found and fixed before a real incident.
X: A=Test only after a real outage: the first "test" then happens when customers are already affected, and any gap between the procedure and the RTO is discovered too late to fix.
C=Engineer estimates: estimates are opinions, not measurements; they routinely miss hidden steps such as DNS propagation, data restore time, or missing permissions.
D=Clear documentation: well-written runbooks can still be wrong or stale (renamed resources, expired credentials, changed dependencies); only execution proves they work within the RTO.
T: Only regular, measured recovery exercises such as game days and FIS experiments prove an RTO; estimates and documentation do not.

@ Q003074 explain
G: ## AWS Resilience Hub
Resilience Hub is a service for defining, assessing and tracking the resilience of an application as a whole.
- You describe the application from sources such as a CloudFormation stack, Terraform state, an AppRegistry application or an EKS cluster.
- You attach a **resiliency policy** that sets target RTO and RPO for disruption types: application, infrastructure, Availability Zone and Region.
- An **assessment** estimates the RTO and RPO the current architecture can achieve and reports whether the policy is met or breached.
- It then gives **recommendations** (for example, enable Multi-AZ on an RDS instance or add cross-Region backups), and can suggest alarms, SOPs and AWS FIS experiments to validate them.
Example: a policy of RTO 1 hour and RPO 15 minutes for a Regional disruption flags a DynamoDB table with no cross-Region copy as a breach.
W: AWS Resilience Hub: it is purpose-built to assess applications against RTO/RPO targets in a resiliency policy and to recommend concrete improvements to close the gaps.
X: B=AWS Trusted Advisor: it runs account-wide best-practice checks (cost, security, fault tolerance, limits) on individual resources, but it has no notion of an application's RTO/RPO targets.
C=Amazon Inspector: it scans EC2, container images and Lambda functions for software vulnerabilities and network exposure, which is a security finding, not a resilience assessment.
D=AWS Config aggregator: it collects configuration and compliance data from multiple accounts and Regions into one view; it does not calculate achievable RTO/RPO or recommend resilience changes.
T: Assessing an application against RTO/RPO targets with recommendations is Resilience Hub; Trusted Advisor and Config only check individual resources and rules.

@ Q003075 explain
G: ## What CloudWatch Logs charges for
CloudWatch Logs bills mainly for two things:
- **Ingestion**: every GB written to a log group. Verbose debug logging from every service multiplies this.
- **Storage (archival)**: every GB kept per month. By default a log group's retention is **Never expire**, so logs accumulate forever.
## The two levers
- **Retention settings** per log group (from 1 day up to 10 years) automatically delete older events. For example, `PutRetentionPolicy` with 30 days on application log groups; long-term copies, if needed, can be exported to S3 lifecycle tiers.
- **Log levels**: running production at `INFO` or `WARN` instead of `DEBUG` cuts the volume ingested at the source, which lowers both ingestion and storage costs.
The Infrequent Access log class is another option for logs that are rarely queried.
W: D=Retention periods: setting retention on each log group stops indefinite accumulation, so storage charges stop growing and old debug logs are deleted automatically.
E=Lower log levels: reducing production verbosity shrinks the amount of data ingested, which is usually the largest part of the bill.
X: A=More metric filters: metric filters turn log patterns into metrics; they do not delete or reduce log data, and custom metrics they publish can add cost.
B=Detailed monitoring: this sends EC2 metrics at 1-minute intervals and is charged extra; it has nothing to do with log volume or retention.
C=Second log group copy: duplicating logs into another log group doubles ingestion and storage rather than reducing them.
T: Log costs fall when you ingest less (lower log levels) and keep less (retention periods); filters, metrics and copies do not reduce stored logs.

@ Q003076 explain
G: ## Match the purchase model to the workload shape
- **Steady baseline**: capacity that runs all the time. Savings Plans (Compute Savings Plans cover EC2, Fargate and Lambda) or Reserved Instances give large discounts for a 1- or 3-year commitment to a steady amount of usage.
- **Spiky, interruptible work**: batch jobs that can retry. Spot capacity uses spare AWS capacity at a steep discount but can be reclaimed with a two-minute notice.
## ECS capacity providers
Amazon ECS lets each service or task choose a **capacity provider strategy**:
- An Auto Scaling group capacity provider backed by On-Demand instances for the steady services.
- `FARGATE_SPOT`, or an Auto Scaling group capacity provider using Spot Instances, for batch tasks.
Example: web services run on the On-Demand ASG covered by a Savings Plan, while nightly batch jobs use `FARGATE_SPOT` and simply retry if a task is interrupted.
W: Savings Plans plus Spot for batch: the commitment discounts only the always-on baseline, and batch spikes run on cheap interruptible capacity that does not touch the steady services.
X: A=All On-Demand: it is predictable but the most expensive way to pay for capacity that runs all month, and it gives no discount on the batch spikes.
B=Reserved Instances sized for peak: the reservation is paid whether used or not, so all capacity above the steady baseline sits idle and wasted between batch spikes.
C=Everything on Spot: steady customer-facing services would be exposed to Spot interruptions, which risks outages for the workloads that must stay stable.
T: Commit (Savings Plans) for the baseline, use Spot for interruptible spikes; never reserve for peaks or put critical steady services on Spot.

@ Q003077 explain
G: ## Why NAT gateway bills grow
A NAT gateway charges per hour and **per GB processed**. When private instances pull container images from Amazon ECR or read objects from S3 through it, every byte is billed as NAT data processing, even though the traffic only goes to AWS services.
## VPC endpoints keep AWS service traffic off the NAT
- **Gateway endpoint for S3** (also available for DynamoDB): added as a route target in route tables, and has no hourly or data charge.
- **Interface endpoints** (AWS PrivateLink) for ECR: `com.amazonaws.<region>.ecr.api` and `com.amazonaws.<region>.ecr.dkr`. They have hourly and per-GB charges, but the per-GB rate is lower than NAT processing.
- ECR stores image layers in S3, so the S3 gateway endpoint is also needed for image pulls to avoid the NAT. Other services used (for example CloudWatch Logs) can get their own interface endpoints.
W: VPC endpoints for ECR, S3 and related services: traffic to those services goes directly over the endpoints instead of through the NAT gateway, removing most of the NAT data processing charges.
X: A=Larger instances: download speed does not change how many GB pass through the NAT gateway, so processing charges stay the same while instance cost rises.
C=Second NAT gateway in the same AZ: it adds another hourly charge and still processes the same data per GB; it adds no savings.
D=Public subnets with public IPs: it removes the NAT but exposes instances directly to the internet, breaking the private-subnet security design, and public IPv4 addresses are also charged hourly.
T: Traffic from private subnets to AWS services should use VPC endpoints (free S3 gateway, ECR interface endpoints), not a per-GB NAT gateway.

@ Q003078 explain
G: ## Dynamic pages that rarely change
If a page is rebuilt from the database on every request but its content changes only a few times a day, almost all of that work produces the same result. Caching the rendered output removes it.
## CloudFront caching
- Amazon CloudFront caches responses at edge locations. A cache policy sets the **TTL** (how long an object stays cached) and which headers, cookies and query strings are part of the cache key.
- The origin can also control TTL with `Cache-Control: max-age=...` headers.
- When the catalog changes, the publishing process calls `CreateInvalidation` for the affected paths (for example `/products/*`) so the next request fetches the fresh page.
Example: with a 1-hour TTL and invalidation on update, thousands of requests per minute for a product page hit the edge cache and only a handful reach the web servers and database.
W: CloudFront page caching with TTL and invalidation: repeated requests are served from edge caches, removing the rendering and database load that makes pages slow, while invalidation keeps content correct after updates.
X: A=Move to DynamoDB: changing the database still renders every page on every request, so the repeated work that causes the slowdown remains, plus a costly migration.
B=More read replicas: replicas spread database reads but every request is still rendered from scratch, so web servers stay loaded and cost rises with each replica.
D=Longer web server timeout: it only lets slow requests wait longer before failing; users still see slow pages and servers hold more connections open.
T: For content that changes rarely, cache rendered pages at the edge with a TTL plus invalidation instead of scaling the rendering path.

@ Q003080 explain
G: ## Progressive delivery on Kubernetes
Progressive delivery releases a new version to a small share of traffic, checks health metrics, and only then increases the share, rolling back automatically if metrics degrade.
- **GitOps**: a controller such as Argo CD or Flux continuously syncs the cluster to manifests stored in Git, so every release is a reviewed commit.
- **Argo Rollouts** replaces a standard `Deployment` with a `Rollout` resource that supports canary and blue/green steps, for example `setWeight: 10`, pause, `setWeight: 50`.
- An **AnalysisTemplate** queries a metrics provider (for example Prometheus or CloudWatch) at each step; if the error rate crosses a threshold, the rollout aborts and traffic returns to the stable version.
Traffic shifting can use a service mesh or ingress controller that supports weighted routing.
W: GitOps with metric-driven canary rollouts: traffic moves in steps, automated analysis checks metrics at each step, and failed analysis triggers automatic rollback.
X: A=Deploy once a year: huge infrequent releases bundle many changes, which increases the risk and blast radius of each release and provides no gradual traffic shift.
B=Manual kubectl apply from a laptop: it has no traffic shifting or metric analysis, depends on one person's machine and credentials, and leaves no reliable audit trail in Git.
D=Delete and recreate all pods: this is a recreate strategy that causes downtime and sends all users to the new version at once, with no gradual shift or automatic rollback.
T: Gradual traffic shifting with automatic metric-based rollback on EKS comes from a progressive delivery controller such as Argo Rollouts, not manual or all-at-once deployments.

@ Q003081 explain
G: ## The four DR strategies
From cheapest and slowest to most expensive and fastest:
- **Backup and restore**: backups copied to another Region; RPO and RTO in hours.
- **Pilot light**: data replicated continuously, core infrastructure defined but application servers off or not deployed; RTO in tens of minutes to hours because compute must be provisioned and scaled.
- **Warm standby**: data replicated continuously and a scaled-down but fully working copy of the application running; recovery means scaling it up and switching traffic, so RTO in minutes.
- **Multi-site active/active**: full capacity serving traffic in both Regions; near-zero RTO at the highest cost.
## Mapping the requirement
An RPO of 1 minute needs continuous replication (for example Aurora Global Database or DynamoDB global tables). An RTO of 15 minutes leaves little time to launch and configure a whole stack, so something must already be running.
W: Warm standby: continuous replication meets the 1-minute RPO, and the already-running scaled-down stack only needs to scale up and receive traffic, which fits a 15-minute RTO at moderate cost.
X: A=Multi-site active-active: it meets the targets but runs full production capacity twice, which exceeds the moderate-cost requirement for a 15-minute RTO.
B=Backup and restore with daily backups: daily backups mean up to 24 hours of data loss, and restoring a full stack takes hours, missing both RPO and RTO.
C=Pilot light with no compute: data is current, but launching, configuring and scaling all compute during the event puts a 15-minute RTO at risk.
T: Minutes of RTO with near-real-time RPO at moderate cost is warm standby; pilot light is cheaper but slower, active-active faster but costlier.

@ Q003082 explain
G: ## S3 replication options
S3 replication copies new objects asynchronously from a source bucket to a destination bucket; both buckets need versioning enabled.
- **Same-Region Replication (SRR)**: destination in the same Region, useful for log aggregation or separate accounts, but no protection against a Regional failure.
- **Cross-Region Replication (CRR)**: destination in another Region, which protects against a Regional event.
- **Replication Time Control (RTC)**: an add-on designed to replicate 99.99% of new objects within 15 minutes and backed by an SLA, with replication metrics and S3 event notifications for objects that miss the threshold.
Example: a bucket in `us-east-1` replicates with RTC to a bucket in `us-west-2`; if `us-east-1` is impaired, the application reads from `us-west-2`.
W: S3 CRR with Replication Time Control: CRR puts copies in a second Region to survive Regional failure, and RTC adds the contractual 15-minute replication SLA with monitoring.
X: B=Same-Region Replication: both copies stay in one Region, so a Regional failure affects source and destination together.
C=S3 Object Lambda access points: they transform data as it is read (for example redacting fields); they do not copy data anywhere.
D=Lifecycle to Glacier Deep Archive: this moves objects to a cheaper class in the same Region, with restores taking hours; it adds neither a second Region nor an SLA.
T: Regional resilience with a replication-time SLA for S3 is CRR plus RTC; SRR, Object Lambda and lifecycle rules stay in one Region.

@ Q003083 explain
G: ## Image signing and admission control
Scanning shows what is inside an image; signing proves who built it and that it has not changed since.
- In the build pipeline, an approved image is signed after tests and scans pass. With AWS Signer and the open-source Notation client, the signature is stored alongside the image in Amazon ECR.
- At deployment, an **admission controller** in the Kubernetes cluster (for example Kyverno, or OPA Gatekeeper with Ratify) checks each pod's images against trusted signing profiles and rejects any unsigned or wrongly signed image.
- Deploying by digest (`image@sha256:...`) instead of a mutable tag ensures the exact image that was signed is the one that runs.
Example: a developer pushes an unsigned test image and tries to deploy it to production; the admission webhook denies the pod.
W: Sign images and verify at deployment: signatures establish provenance and integrity, and admission controls enforce that only approved, signed images can start in production.
X: A=Any public image with a recent tag: a tag says nothing about who built the image or whether it was tampered with, and tags are mutable, so untrusted images could run.
C=Yearly scan and deploy regardless: scanning once a year misses new vulnerabilities, and ignoring findings means no image is actually blocked.
D=Images copied from laptops: manual copies bypass the registry, scanning and any verification, so there is no provenance or control over what runs.
T: Only a signature check enforced at admission guarantees that only approved images run; tags, occasional scans and manual copies do not.

G: ## Publishing one service privately with PrivateLink
- The provider places the service behind a **Network Load Balancer** (or a Gateway Load Balancer for appliances) and creates a **VPC endpoint service** from it.
- Each partner creates an **interface VPC endpoint** in its own VPC. It appears as network interfaces with private IPs from the partner's subnets.
- The provider allows specific partner AWS account principals and can require manual acceptance of each connection.

Example: partner apps call `vpce-0abc...-example.vpce-svc-...` or a private DNS name, and traffic stays on the AWS network.
W: VPC endpoint service behind an NLB (PrivateLink): partners reach just the one service privately from their own VPCs, with no internet exposure and no network-level access to the rest of the VPC.

C=Public ALB with IP allow list: the service stays internet-facing, and traffic crosses the public internet, which violates the requirement even with IP restrictions.
D=Shared IAM user credentials: IAM credentials control API permissions, not network paths; sharing long-term keys is a security risk and does not make the service private.
T: Exposing one service privately to other VPCs is PrivateLink (NLB plus endpoint service); peering exposes whole networks.

G: ## Application authorization as policy
Application permissions such as "a user can edit a document they own in their own department" depend on attributes of the user, the action and the resource. Coding them as `if` statements spreads rules across services and makes changes and audits hard.
## Amazon Verified Permissions
- A managed authorization service that stores and evaluates policies written in **Cedar**, an open-source policy language.
- Example policy idea: `permit` action `EditDocument` when `resource.owner == principal` and `resource.department == principal.department`.

X: A=Security groups per user: security groups filter network traffic by IP, port and protocol; they know nothing about users, documents or departments.
B=Hard-coded if-statements: the rules live inside application code, which is exactly what must be avoided; every change needs a redeploy and rules drift between services.
D=IAM policies per end user: IAM authorizes access to AWS APIs for AWS principals, not application-level decisions for millions of end users on application documents.
T: Fine-grained application authorization managed outside the code is Verified Permissions (Cedar); IAM and security groups protect AWS resources and networks.

@ Q003086 explain
G: ## Fan-out with SNS
Amazon SNS is a publish/subscribe service. A producer publishes a message once to a **topic**, and SNS pushes a copy to every subscription.
- Subscriptions can be SQS queues, Lambda functions, HTTP/S endpoints, email, SMS and mobile push.
- For internal services, the common pattern is **SNS to SQS fan-out**: each service has its own SQS queue subscribed to the topic, so each processes every event at its own pace and retries independently.
- **Subscription filter policies** let a subscriber receive only the events it cares about.
Example: an `OrderShipped` event goes to one topic; SNS sends an email and a push notification, and queues for billing and analytics each receive their own copy.
W: SNS topic fan-out: one publish delivers the event to every channel (email, SMS, push) and to each service's SQS queue, decoupling the producer from all consumers.
X: A=Shared polled database table: every consumer must poll constantly and track what it has processed, adding latency and database load, and it cannot send email, SMS or push by itself.
B=One SQS queue for all services: each message is consumed and deleted by one consumer, so the services compete for events instead of each receiving every event.
C=Direct synchronous calls: the producer must know and call every endpoint, waits on each, and fails or needs custom retries whenever any consumer is down.
T: When every consumer needs every event, publish to an SNS topic and fan out to per-service SQS queues; a single queue splits events between consumers.

@ Q003087 explain
G: ## Kinesis Data Streams basics
- A stream is made of **shards**. Each shard accepts up to 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s of writes, so 200,000 small events per second needs about 200 shards, or on-demand capacity mode that scales automatically.
- Records are kept for 24 hours by default; retention can be extended up to 365 days, so seven days allows replay from any point in that window.
- Consumers read by position, so several independent applications can read the same data and each keeps its own checkpoint.
## Enhanced fan-out
Standard consumers share 2 MB/s of read throughput per shard. With **enhanced fan-out**, each registered consumer gets its own 2 MB/s per shard delivered by push (`SubscribeToShard`), so adding consumers does not slow the others.
W: Kinesis Data Streams with enhanced fan-out: shards scale to high ingest rates, extended retention keeps seven days for replay, and enhanced fan-out gives each independent consumer dedicated read throughput.
X: B=SNS with email targets: a standard SNS topic pushes messages and does not keep them for replay (message archiving is only for FIFO topics), and email delivery is not a way to feed analytics consumers at this rate.
C=S3 with daily uploads: batching once a day is not streaming, and writing each event as its own object at 200,000 per second is inefficient and costly.
D=SQS standard queue: messages are deleted once processed and each goes to one consumer, so multiple independent consumers and replay are not possible.
T: High-rate ingest with replay and many independent readers is Kinesis Data Streams (with enhanced fan-out); queues delete messages and standard SNS topics do not retain them.

@ Q003088 explain
G: ## Decouple intake from processing
If uploads are processed inside the upload request, any downstream outage turns into failed uploads. The fix is to accept and store the upload durably first and hand processing off asynchronously.
- The client uploads the file to **Amazon S3** (often directly with a presigned URL). S3 stores it durably regardless of the processing service's state.
- An event (an S3 event notification, or a message from the app) goes into an **SQS queue**. Messages can be retained for up to 14 days.
- When processing is healthy, workers read the queue and handle the backlog; a dead-letter queue catches messages that keep failing.
Example: processing is down for 6 hours; users keep uploading, and 30,000 messages pile up in the queue and are processed after the service recovers.
W: S3 plus SQS: uploads are stored durably and processing events wait in the queue, so intake keeps working for hours while processing is down and catches up later.
X: A=Synchronous processing: the upload request depends on the processing service, so when it is down every upload fails or times out.
B=Reject uploads during outages: this directly breaks the requirement to keep accepting uploads and pushes the failure onto users.
D=Web server memory: memory is limited and lost on restart, scaling or instance failure, so hours of uploads could exhaust it or disappear.
T: Accept work durably (S3) and queue it (SQS) so intake survives downstream outages; synchronous or in-memory designs fail with the backend.

G: ## Sorted sets for leaderboards
Redis OSS and Valkey, available in Amazon ElastiCache, are in-memory key-value engines with a **sorted set** data type: each member has a score, and the set stays ordered by score.
- `ZADD leaderboard 4200 player123` adds or updates a score, `ZREVRANGE leaderboard 0 9 WITHSCORES` returns the top 10, and `ZREVRANK leaderboard player123` returns a player's rank.
- These operations are logarithmic in the set size, and because data is in memory, typical latencies are sub-millisecond even with millions of members.
- Replicas and cluster mode add read scaling and availability.
Example: each game result runs one `ZINCRBY`, and the leaderboard page reads the top 100 and the player's own rank on every refresh.
W: ElastiCache for Redis OSS or Valkey sorted sets: the ranking is maintained in memory as scores change, so top-N and rank queries return in sub-millisecond time at millions of players.
X: B=S3 JSON file per score: rewriting a whole object on every score update does not scale, and concurrent writers overwrite each other's changes.
C=Redshift with hourly materialized view: a warehouse is built for analytical queries with seconds of latency, and hourly refresh makes rankings up to an hour stale.
D=RDS PostgreSQL with frequent ORDER BY: repeatedly sorting millions of rows under heavy read load adds CPU and latency, far from sub-millisecond reads.
T: Real-time rankings with sub-millisecond reads call for in-memory sorted sets (ElastiCache Redis OSS/Valkey), not repeated sorts in a database or warehouse.

G: ## Graph databases and multi-hop queries
Fraud rings show up as relationships: several accounts sharing a device, devices linked to the same card, transactions flowing between related accounts. In a relational database, each hop is another join, and queries several hops deep become slow and complex.
## Amazon Neptune
- A managed graph database that stores **vertices** (accounts, devices, transactions) and **edges** (uses, pays, owns) and is optimized for following relationships.
- Queries use Gremlin, openCypher or SPARQL. For example, an openCypher query can find all accounts within three hops of a flagged device.
- Neptune ML can add graph neural network predictions, and read replicas scale query load.
W: Amazon Neptune: it is built to traverse relationships across many hops efficiently, which is exactly the account-device-transaction pattern used to detect fraud rings.
X: A=Amazon Timestream: a time series database for measurements over time such as IoT metrics; it does not model or traverse relationships between entities.
C=Amazon DocumentDB: a JSON document database with MongoDB compatibility; multi-hop relationships require repeated lookups or aggregations instead of native graph traversal.
D=Amazon QLDB: a ledger database for an immutable, verifiable history of changes, not graph traversal, and AWS has ended support for it.
T: Querying relationships several hops deep calls for a graph database (Neptune), not time series, document or ledger stores.

G: ## Serverless, pay-per-query analytics
Amazon Athena runs SQL directly on data in S3 with no clusters to manage. It uses the AWS Glue Data Catalog for table definitions and charges by the amount of data each query scans; when no queries run, there is no compute charge.
## Why columnar formats matter
- **Parquet** and **ORC** store data by column and include statistics, so a query reading 3 of 50 columns scans only those columns.
- Compression and **partitioning** (for example `s3://lake/sales/year=2026/month=09/`) cut scanned bytes further.
Example: converting CSV to compressed, partitioned Parquet can reduce the data scanned per query many times over, lowering cost and speeding up queries.
W: Athena on Parquet: it is serverless and billed per data scanned, so infrequent, unpredictable queries cost nothing when idle, and columnar storage keeps each query's cost low.
X: A=Always-on EMR cluster: the instances bill every hour even when no one queries, which wastes money for infrequent workloads.
B=Load into RDS before each query: copying large S3 datasets into a database each time is slow and costly, and the RDS instance bills while running regardless of query volume.
D=Provisioned Redshift cluster 24/7: provisioned nodes are paid around the clock whether or not queries run, the opposite of paying only for queries.
T: For occasional, unpredictable SQL on S3, use Athena on columnar data; always-on clusters charge even when idle.

G: ## Scaling capacity to demand
An Auto Scaling group behind an ALB can change the number of instances automatically. Two policy types fit a daily pattern:
- **Scheduled actions** change minimum, maximum or desired capacity at set times, for example scale out at 08:00 before users arrive and scale in to a small minimum at 20:00.
- **Target tracking** keeps a metric near a target, such as average CPU at 50% or `ALBRequestCountPerTarget` at 1,000, adding or removing instances for unexpected variations.
- **Predictive scaling** can also learn the daily pattern and launch capacity ahead of forecast demand.
Example: 20 instances during business hours and 2 overnight means paying for roughly half the instance-hours of a fixed 20-instance fleet.
W: Scheduled plus target tracking scaling: capacity rises for business hours and drops near zero at night, so the company pays only for capacity it needs while still handling daytime load.
X: A=Dedicated Hosts for peak: they are the most expensive way to run instances and are paid per host whether used or not, so night-time idle capacity is still billed.
B=Fixed peak-sized fleet 24 hours: the fleet is idle most of the night but billed every hour, wasting money whenever demand is low.
C=Three-year Reserved Instances for peak: the discount applies but the full peak capacity is paid for around the clock, including the hours it is idle.

G: ## Cross-Region DR for stateful EC2
A stateful server needs two things in the recovery Region: an up-to-date copy of its disks, and a way to send users there.
## AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery (AWS DRS)
- An agent on each source server continuously replicates its block storage to a low-cost staging area in the target Region, giving an RPO of seconds.
- At failover (or a drill), DRS launches recovery instances from the latest or a chosen point in time, typically within minutes.
## Route 53 failover routing
- A **failover** record set has a primary record and a secondary record. A **health check** watches the primary endpoint.
- When the health check fails, Route 53 answers DNS queries with the secondary record, sending users to the recovered application.
Example: DRS replicates app servers from `eu-west-1` to `eu-central-1`; when the primary fails, recovery instances launch and Route 53 switches traffic.
W: B=Route 53 failover routing: health checks detect the failed primary and DNS automatically points users to the recovery Region's endpoint.
E=AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery: continuous block-level replication keeps the servers' data current in the second Region, so loss is minimal and recovery instances launch quickly.
X: A=Larger instances in the primary Region: more capacity in one Region does not survive the loss of that Region.
C=Single NAT gateway in the primary Region: it provides outbound internet access for private subnets; it neither copies data nor redirects traffic to another Region.
D=Snapshots kept only in the primary Region: if that Region fails, the snapshots are unavailable too, and periodic snapshots lose more data than continuous replication.
T: Cross-Region failover for stateful EC2 needs continuous data replication (Elastic Disaster Recovery) plus traffic redirection (Route 53 failover).

G: ## Restricting S3 access to a VPC endpoint
A VPC endpoint for S3 lets instances reach S3 privately. By itself it adds a path; it does not stop access from elsewhere. The bucket policy closes the other paths.
- A **gateway endpoint** (or interface endpoint) has an ID such as `vpce-1a2b3c4d`.
- The bucket policy adds a `Deny` for `s3:*` on the bucket and its objects with the condition `StringNotEquals` on `aws:SourceVpce` (or `aws:SourceVpc`), so any request not arriving through that endpoint is refused, even with valid credentials.
- Be careful: such a deny also blocks console access and administration from outside the VPC, so admins often add an exception for a specific role.
Example: a request from a laptop on the internet with valid keys receives `403 AccessDenied` because it did not come through `vpce-1a2b3c4d`.
W: Bucket policy denying non-endpoint requests: the `aws:SourceVpce` condition makes S3 itself refuse every request that does not come through the company's VPC endpoint.
X: B=IAM user with long-term key: credentials say who is calling, not where from; the key works from anywhere on the internet and long-term keys add leak risk.
C=NAT gateway route to S3: traffic still goes to S3's public endpoints over the NAT's public IP, and nothing prevents other internet clients from reaching the bucket.
D=Security group on the bucket: security groups attach to network interfaces in a VPC; S3 buckets have no security groups, so this control cannot exist.
T: To force all S3 access through your VPC, deny requests in the bucket policy unless `aws:SourceVpce` matches your endpoint.

G: ## AWS Global Accelerator
Global Accelerator gives an application **two static anycast IPv4 addresses** (dual-stack accelerators add IPv6) announced from AWS edge locations worldwide.
- Users connect to the nearest edge, and traffic travels over the AWS global network to **endpoint groups** in one or more Regions (ALBs, NLBs, EC2 instances or Elastic IPs).
- Health checks monitor endpoints; if a Regional endpoint becomes unhealthy, new traffic is sent to a healthy Region without any DNS change, so client DNS caching does not delay failover.
- **Traffic dials** and endpoint weights shift traffic between Regions for blue/green or maintenance.
Example: a customer's firewall allows only the accelerator's two static IPs; when `us-east-1` fails, traffic moves to `eu-west-1` behind the same two IPs.
W: AWS Global Accelerator: it provides fixed anycast IPs customers can allow-list and fails over quickly between Regional endpoints using health checks, without relying on DNS changes.
X: A=Amazon CloudFront: by default it serves from a large, changing set of edge IP addresses (static IP lists are an optional, special-purpose feature), and it is an HTTP/HTTPS caching CDN whose origin failover covers only some request methods, not a static-IP entry point for any application traffic.
B=AWS Direct Connect: it provides dedicated private network links from on-premises to AWS; it does not give internet customers static IPs or failover between Regional endpoints.
C=Amazon Route 53: DNS failover returns different endpoint IPs, which breaks allow-lists, and clients may keep cached answers until the TTL expires, slowing failover.
T: Static IPs to allow-list plus fast Regional failover is Global Accelerator; Route 53 changes IPs through DNS and CloudFront is a caching CDN, not a static-IP front door.

G: ## S3 storage classes for a cold data lake
- **S3 Standard**: millisecond access, highest storage price; right for data read often.
- **S3 Glacier Flexible Retrieval**: much cheaper storage; retrieval in minutes (expedited), 3-5 hours (standard) or 5-12 hours (bulk); 90-day minimum storage duration.
- **S3 Glacier Deep Archive**: lowest storage price; standard retrieval within 12 hours, bulk within 48 hours; 180-day minimum storage duration.
## Lifecycle rules
A lifecycle configuration moves objects automatically, for example `Transition` to `GLACIER` or `DEEP_ARCHIVE` 90 days after creation. At 5 PB, the difference between Standard and archive pricing is very large every month.
Example: an audit needs 20 TB from last year; a standard or bulk restore from Glacier Flexible Retrieval, or a standard restore from Deep Archive, completes within the 12-hour window.
W: S3 Standard then lifecycle to Glacier after 90 days: recent data stays fast, rarely accessed data moves to archive classes that cost a fraction as much, and both archive classes can restore within 12 hours for audits.
X: A=EBS volumes on EC2: block storage is priced per provisioned GB and far more expensive than S3 at 5 PB, needs running instances, and is not a data lake storage layer.
B=S3 Standard forever: it pays top storage price for petabytes that are rarely read, even though a 12-hour retrieval window allows much cheaper archive classes.
D=S3 One Zone-IA for all data: it stores data in a single AZ, so an AZ loss can destroy it, and without lifecycle rules it never moves to the far cheaper archive tiers.
T: When cold data only needs retrieval within hours, lifecycle it from S3 Standard to Glacier classes after the hot period instead of keeping it in costlier tiers.
